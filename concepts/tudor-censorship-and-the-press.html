Tudor Censorship and the Press

Tudor Censorship and the Press

The Tudor and early Stuart press was controlled through a system of interlocking mechanisms: royal proclamations establishing what could not be printed; licensing requirements (pre-publication approval by ecclesiastical or civil authorities); the Stationers’ Company’s power of search; the patent system restricting which printers could legally produce which categories of text; and (for theatrical publication) a parallel system administered through the Master of the Revels. No single piece of legislation governed the press until the 1643 Parliamentary licensing order; instead, control accumulated through proclamations, charter provisions, star chamber orders, and ecclesiastical regulation. The result was a porous but functionally effective censorship apparatus that shaped the entire landscape of Elizabethan and Jacobean print culture. Albright’s comparative assessment is notable: England’s censorship regime, for all its apparatus, operated with more freedom than France or Spain, partly because commercial self-interest in the Stationers’ Company was not always aligned with political suppression.

The Foundation: Henry VII and Henry VIII

Henry VII’s appointment of Peter Actors as the first royal printer in 1485 established the precedent of a Crown-licensed press — though Actors’s patent was, Patterson notes, “remarkably free of any restrictions.” The censorship apparatus was built by Henry VIII in response to the Lutheran crisis:

  • 1529 proclamation: Against Lutheran books specifically; named particular prohibited titles.
  • 1538 proclamation: Required royal pre-publication licence for all books in English — the first general pre-publication censorship requirement.

Under Edward VI, Protestant reformers used the press aggressively but found the licensing infrastructure inadequate to restrain opponents. Under Mary I the problem was reversed — against Protestant polemic. Mary’s 1557 Stationers’ Company charter addressed this directly: it created an institutional mechanism for press control in exchange for the Company’s collective monopoly over printing. ^[source: copyright_patterson_raw.txt, hazlitt_livery_companies_raw.txt]

Foxe records an inhibition from Queen Mary for preaching and printing at the opening of her reign (foxe-acts-monuments-vol6.txt). The Marian books reproduce proclamations, articles to Bonner, and the restored Mass-Book — the inverse of Edward’s command to use the Book of Common Prayer and surrender Latin service books.

The 1557 Charter as Censorship Instrument

The charter granted by Philip and Mary on May 4, 1557, was explicitly a censorship instrument. The preamble — quoted in full by Hazlitt — identifies the suppression of “seditious and heretical books, rhymes, and treatises” as the charter’s primary purpose. The Company received a power of search throughout England in exchange for taking on the function of press regulation. Patterson argues this was the defining structural bargain of Elizabethan press history: private economic monopoly in exchange for public censorship function. Elizabeth’s confirmation of the charter on November 10, 1559, preserved the censorship framework under a Protestant rather than Catholic regime — the apparatus built to suppress Protestants was redeployed against Catholics. ^[source: hazlitt_livery_companies_raw.txt, copyright_patterson_raw.txt]

The 1559 Injunctions and 1566 Ordinances

Elizabeth’s Injunctions of 1559 required all books to be licensed before printing by the Archbishop of Canterbury, Bishop of London, or other designated authorities — and required the licenser’s name to appear in the register entry. The formula established was: “Entred for his copie vnder th[e] handes of [licenser(s)]” — binding the copyright system to the censorship system in a single administrative act.

The Ordinances of June 29, 1566 (“decreed for reformation of diuers disorders in printing”) strengthened the licensing requirements and the Company’s enforcement capacity. Both documents are reproduced in Arber Vol. I. They represent the Elizabethan systematisation of the Henrician and Marian ad hoc arrangements into a coherent administrative structure. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_vol1_raw.txt, hazlitt_livery_companies_raw.txt]

The 1586 Star Chamber Decree

The most important Elizabethan press enactment, as Arber calls it. Issued on June 23, 1586, transcribed in full in Arber Vol. II. Key provisions:

  • All printing restricted to London (with one press each permitted at Oxford and Cambridge).
  • The number of authorised master printers limited to 25.
  • All new books required licence from the Archbishop of Canterbury or Bishop of London before printing.
  • The Wardens of the Stationers’ Company were empowered to search and suppress unlicensed printing.

The Decree emerged from the climate created by the Marprelate controversy (1589) and the proliferating unlicensed religious press of the early 1580s. Arber Vol. II reproduces the September 1589 secret report to Burghley identifying Waldegrave, Sharpe, and Hodgkins as the Marprelate printers; Knightley and Wiston as the hosts of the secret press; and describing the dramatic escape of the type-box when the press was discovered. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_vol2_raw.txt, copyright_patterson_raw.txt]

The 1599 Bishops’ Order

On June 1, 1599, the Archbishop of Canterbury (Whitgift) and Bishop of London (Bancroft) issued a major suppression order — delivered to fourteen named printers on June 4. Its provisions:

  • Specific books ordered burned: Marston’s Pygmalion and The Scourge of Villainy; Davies’s Epigrams bound with Marlowe’s Elegies; Marriage and Wyvinge; 15 Foyes of Marriage; and Nash and Harvey’s works.
  • Books “stayed” (called in but not destroyed): Caltha Poetarum, Hall’s Satires, Willobie’s Aviso.
  • No more plays or satires to be printed without special authority.
  • English history books not to be printed without Privy Council approval.

Richard Field was among the fourteen named printers who received the Order. The satire-burning was a cultural intervention reflecting anxiety about the satirical literary mode that had flourished in the 1590s — the same anxiety that produced Ben Jonson’s imprisonment. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_vol3_raw.txt]

Specific Suppressions and Their Limits

The Arber Registers and Hazlitt’s chapter record the practical operation of censorship through specific cases:

  • French books burned by the Archbishop (1595)
  • Raleigh’s History of the World (1614): the Archbishop directed it be suppressed; it was eventually allowed after offensive passages were removed; the book circulated without a title-page in the interim.
  • Cartwright’s Confutation of the Rhemish Testament: Suppressed for 30 years solely because the author was a Puritan; eventually printed at Leyden by Edward Brewster in 1618 — demonstrating that Continental presses provided an effective escape route for works the English system could not permit.
  • Lithgow’s Travels (1616): erased from the Register.
  • Prynne’s Histriomastix (1634): erased from the Register; Prynne fined £5,000, his ears cropped, and imprisoned for life.
  • Buchanan’s History of Scotland: MS and whole impression seized.

The suppression was not total. Albright documents that suppressed books continued to circulate: Raleigh’s History without its title-page; Thomas Woodcock was committed to Newgate in 1578 for the renewed sale of An Admonition to the Parliament. The system’s limits were as revealing as its powers. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_vol3_raw.txt, stationers_registers_arber_vol4_raw.txt, hazlitt_livery_companies_raw.txt, dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

Dramatic Censorship: The Dual System

Drama faced two censorship systems operating simultaneously, which Albright’s Dramatic Publication in England analyses in detail:

Performance licensing (the Master of the Revels): All plays intended for public performance required the Master of the Revels’ approval. Edmund Tilney held the office from 1578 to 1610; George Buc from 1610 to 1622. The Master read scripts, required alterations, and could suppress plays entirely. The Sir Thomas More manuscript (Harley 7368) survives with Tilney’s censorship marks throughout — he struck the riot scenes dealing with immigrants (“Ill May Day” scenes) and required their replacement, providing direct documentary evidence of how performance censorship operated at the manuscript level.

Publication licensing (Stationers’ Register): A play published as a quarto required entry in the Stationers’ Register, implying ecclesiastical approval. The two systems were distinct and could diverge: a play licensed for performance was not automatically approvable for print, since print reached a broader audience.

Major dramatic suppressions:

  • Isle of Dogs (Nashe/Jonson, 1597): caused the Privy Council to suppress all plays in London for the summer and to order demolition of The Curtain and The Theatre — the most extreme single response to a theatrical work.
  • Richard II (Shakespeare, 1601): the deposition scene performance on the eve of Essex’s rebellion was investigated; actors questioned but not prosecuted.
  • A Game at Chess (Middleton, 1624): depicted Philip IV of Spain and the Count Gondomar allegorically in a chess game; ran nine consecutive days before suppression — the clearest case of a systematic attack on a foreign king passing the censor and then being suppressed by diplomatic complaint.
  • Eastward Ho (Chapman/Jonson/Marston, 1605): Chapman and Jonson imprisoned for passages mocking the Scottish at James I’s court. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt, stationers_registers_arber_vol3_raw.txt]

The 1637 Star Chamber Decree

The most comprehensive pre-Civil War press regulation, issued July 11, 1637, transcribed in full in Arber Vol. IV with Lambe’s preliminary list of authorised printers. Key provisions:

  • Master printers reduced to 23 (Lambe’s list specifying each printer’s identity and equipment).
  • All imported books to be inspected by Company Wardens.
  • Statutory penalties for unlicensed printing including imprisonment, pillorying, and whipping.
  • Detailed succession rules for when a patent-holder died.

The Decree was the last systematic attempt at press control before the Civil War disrupted the licensing apparatus. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_vol4_raw.txt, copyright_patterson_raw.txt]

The Quo Warranto of 1663

The Stationers’ Company was subjected to a Quo Warranto writ in 1663 — not for over-censorship but for insufficient censorship. The writ arose “from a belief that the Company was not sufficiently vigilant and strict in its capacity as a curb on the press, and connived at the publication of inflammatory or scurrilous literature.” This is the inverse of what historians typically assume about the Company’s censorship role. The charter was restored by Act of 2 William and Mary (1690). ^[source: hazlitt_livery_companies_raw.txt]

Print Fixity and Social Construction: The Eisenstein-Johns Debate

The most enduring historiographical question about Tudor press censorship is why such an elaborate apparatus was necessary at all, if print was — as Elizabeth Eisenstein argues — an inherently stabilizing technology. The debate between Eisenstein and Adrian Johns structures the entire historiography of the English book trade and gives the censorship story its deeper significance.

Eisenstein’s thesis. The Printing Press as an Agent of Change (1979) argues that print conferred “fixity” on texts — the same words in every copy, distributable across distances and time — making it qualitatively different from manuscript culture, in which every copy diverged from every other. On this reading, the Protestant Reformation succeeded where the Lollards failed precisely because Luther had print and Wycliffe did not: once Protestant ideas were fixed in printed form and distributed across Europe’s networks of literate clergy and merchants, suppression became structurally harder than it had been for manuscript heresies. The Archbishop of Mainz could confiscate manuscripts; he could not confiscate every printed copy already distributed across the German-speaking world. The English application is specific: the Great Bible of 1539 — an identical text placed by Cromwell’s royal command in every parish church — is Eisenstein’s “fixity” argument in practice. Standardization by printed duplication created, for the first time, a single authorized text simultaneously present across the entire country. The Elizabethan settlement’s durability rested partly on the same logic: Prayer Book, Homilies, and authorized Bible had been printed in such numbers and distributed so widely that removal became practically impossible. ^[source: eisenstein_printing_press_raw.txt]

Johns’s counter-argument. The Nature of the Book (1998) challenges Eisenstein directly. Print did NOT automatically confer fixity or credibility — these had to be socially constructed through a complex and contested process. Piracy was ubiquitous: any printed book could be reprinted without authorization, falsely attributed, or corrupted in transmission. Johns’s key claim: “In early modern England, knowledge had to be made, articulated, communicated, and defended in a Stationers’ commonwealth” — and that commonwealth was “distinctive not only socially, but epistemologically. Credit was fragile and ephemeral to an extraordinary degree. The conditions in which knowledge must be constructed, maintained, and defended were riven by private deals, intrigue and distrust.” The Stationers’ Company’s elaborate system of “copy” (proprietary rights in a text), combined with the licensing apparatus, was precisely the social construction of fixity that Eisenstein had attributed to technology. On Johns’s reading, Tudor censorship was not an obstacle imposed on print’s natural tendency toward fixity but a necessary condition for that fixity’s achievement. ^[source: nature_of_the_book_raw.txt]

The practical illustration: Prynne’s Histriomastix (1634). The Prynne case, which appears in the Arber registers as a suppression, illustrates Johns’s point with particular force. Prynne was prosecuted not for what he had written but for what interpretation could be made of it. Lord Carrington told him in Star Chamber that he “should not have written any thing that would bear construction, for he doth not accompany his books, to make his intention known to all that reads it.” Archbishop Laud agreed that a writer “cannot tell of what dispositions his readers will be.” The court’s position — that a text’s danger lay in its possible interpretations, not its author’s intentions — is precisely Johns’s central claim about the Stationers’ commonwealth: the credibility of a printed text was always hostage to the reader’s response, not just the author’s design. The censorship apparatus existed because fixity was not automatic; it had to be continuously constructed and defended. ^[source: nature_of_the_book_raw.txt, stationers_registers_arber_vol4_raw.txt]
