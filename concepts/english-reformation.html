English Reformation

The English Reformation was the process by which England broke from the Roman Catholic Church, established the Church of England under royal supremacy, dissolved the monasteries, and developed a reformed Protestant liturgy — a process that occupied roughly the years 1529 to 1559 and was irreversible by Elizabeth’s accession. Unlike the Continental Reformation (which began as a theological revolt by Luther in 1517), the English Reformation began as a constitutional and dynastic crisis: Henry VIII’s desire to annul his marriage to Catherine of Aragon. Thomas Cromwell designed the legislative framework; Thomas Cranmer designed the ecclesiastical and theological framework; the printing press disseminated both. The Reformation transformed the English state, the English Church, the English landscape (through the dissolution of the monasteries), and the English book trade.

The Dynastic Trigger

Henry VIII had been married to Catherine of Aragon since 1509. By the late 1520s, Catherine had failed to produce a surviving male heir (their son Henry lived only weeks; Mary was their only surviving child), and Henry was convinced that his marriage was invalid — citing Leviticus 20:21, which prohibits marriage to a brother’s wife (Catherine had briefly been married to Henry’s brother Arthur before Arthur died in 1502). He sought an annulment from Pope Clement VII; Clement, under political pressure from Catherine’s nephew Emperor Charles V, refused. The impasse required an alternative constitutional solution. The solution required not merely a legal ruling but a theory of English sovereignty that could justify overriding papal jurisdiction entirely. ^[source: wolf_hall_raw.txt, hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt]

The Intellectual Framework: Marsiglio and St German

Cromwell’s legislative programme was not improvised — it drew on a coherent theory of royal and parliamentary sovereignty over the church developed by two thinkers.

Marsiglio of Padua’s Defensor Pacis (1324) argued that secular rulers, not popes, should have ultimate authority over the church within their territories. The work had been suppressed for two centuries as heretical. Cromwell had it translated and circulated, and reportedly read its key propositions aloud to Henry — using the medieval theoretical apparatus as justification for what Henry wanted to do in practice. The Defensor Pacis provided the intellectual ancestry for the Act of Supremacy.

Christopher St German was an English common lawyer whose works (Doctor and Student, Treatise on the Division between Spiritualty and Temporalty) developed the argument that Parliament had jurisdiction over the English church and could reform its laws and practices. St German argued from English common law precedent rather than continental theory — his approach was more persuasive to English parliamentarians than Marsiglio’s scholastic framework. Cromwell used both: the theoretical breadth of Marsiglio and the common law credibility of St German. ^[source: wolf_hall_raw.txt, hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt]

Cromwell’s Legislative Programme

Thomas Cromwell, from approximately 1532, designed and drove through Parliament the statutes that made the break with Rome a legal reality. The programme was incremental — each act building on the last — and used financial leverage as well as constitutional argument:

  • Act in Conditional Restraint of Annates (1532): Suspended the payment of annates (first-year revenues of bishoprics) to Rome — the first use of financial pressure as a diplomatic weapon. The “conditional” framing kept open the possibility of negotiation while demonstrating England’s willingness to act.
  • Act in Restraint of Appeals (1533): Declared England an “empire” subject to no external jurisdiction — the legal foundation for the rupture with Rome. Henceforth English legal and ecclesiastical cases would be decided within England, not referred to Rome. This was the act that enabled Cranmer to grant Henry’s annulment without papal sanction.
  • Act of Supremacy (1534): Made Henry VIII “Supreme Head of the Church of England” — placing the English Church under the Crown rather than the papacy.
  • Treasons Act (1534): Made denial of the Royal Supremacy a capital offence — the instrument under which More and Fisher were executed. ^[source: wolf_hall_raw.txt, hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt]

Resistance: Gardiner, More, Fisher

Not all senior churchmen complied. Stephen Gardiner, Bishop of Winchester, accepted the Supremacy (writing De Vera Obedientia in its defence) but resisted the doctrinal Protestantism Cromwell and Cranmer sought to introduce — he was the leading conservative on the Privy Council who kept the Reformation in England closer to Catholicism minus the pope than to continental Protestantism. The Six Articles (1539), which reaffirmed transubstantiation and clerical celibacy, represented Gardiner’s counter-pressure on Cromwell’s programme.

Thomas thomas-more and john-fisher, Bishop of Rochester, refused to take the oath required by the Act of Supremacy and were executed in 1535. Their refusal — and More’s strategy of silence, which Cromwell had to overcome through Richard Rich’s perjured testimony — illustrated both the moral limits of the legislative programme and Cromwell’s willingness to overstep those limits when necessary. ^[source: wolf_hall_raw.txt, hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt]

Hume’s “Digression on Ecclesiastical State”

Hume inserts a long analytical digression into his Tudor history to explain the structural preconditions for the Reformation’s success. His argument is that the medieval church had so accumulated wealth, jurisdiction, and privilege that reform had become both economically attractive (to those who would benefit from redistributed church property) and politically necessary (to rulers who resented the check that church courts and papal appeals placed on their authority). The dissolution of the monasteries was not an incidental by-product of the Reformation but, Hume argues, its primary economic driver: Henry and Cromwell dissolved the monasteries because they were wealthy and because their suppression would create a class of new landowners dependent on the Protestant settlement’s permanence. The theological justifications were, in Hume’s reading, secondary to the fiscal and political logic. ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt]

The Dissolution and Social Transformation

The dissolution of the monasteries (1536–40), directed by Cromwell, was the Reformation’s most visible material consequence. Approximately 800 monasteries and religious houses were suppressed; their lands (about one-quarter of all English land) were transferred to the Crown and thence, through sale, to the gentry. This transfer created a large class of landowners with a direct economic interest in the permanence of the Protestant settlement — they could not return the land without ruin. The Pilgrimage of Grace (1536), a northern rising in defence of the monasteries and the old religion, was the most serious domestic rebellion of the Tudor period; it was suppressed with mass hangings. ^[source: the_mirror_and_the_light_raw.txt, hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt]

The Edwardian and Elizabethan Settlements

Under Edward VI (1547–53), Archbishop Cranmer implemented the doctrinal Reformation deferred through Henry’s reign: the First Prayer Book (1549) and Second Prayer Book (1552) established English-language liturgy; the 42 Articles (1553) defined Anglican theology along broadly Calvinist lines. Mary I (1553–58) reversed the settlement, returning England to Rome and burning nearly 300 Protestants — a campaign that produced Foxe’s Acts and Monuments (the “Book of Martyrs”) as both memorial and propaganda. Elizabeth I’s accession in 1558 established a durable Protestant settlement: the Acts of Supremacy and Uniformity (1559) restored the Royal Supremacy and mandated a revised Prayer Book (a modified version of the 1552 book). The Elizabethan settlement was deliberately capacious — avoiding the sharper Calvinist positions that might alienate moderates — and required only outward conformity rather than inward conviction, which allowed many crypto-Catholics to survive within it. ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt, hume_history_tudor_vol2_raw.txt]

Foxe’s Narrative of the Reformation

Foxe’s Acts and Monuments structures the English Reformation as a chronicle of persecution and deliverance across the Tudor century. Book VIII (vol5-pratt) carries Henrician matters from the abolishing of the pope through Frith, Tyndale, the Six Articles, and Anne Askew. Book IX (vol6) narrates Edward VI: the Book of Common Prayer, altars taken down, and Gardiner’s trial. Book X opens with Mary’s accession and the alteration of religion; Books XI–XII (vol7, vol4) record examinations, disputations at Oxford, and burnings under Mary, closing with her death and Elizabeth’s accession. Foxe gathered material from parties that suffered and from bishops’ registers; the 1563 edition was printed by John Day with dedications to Elizabeth comparing her to Constantine.

The Press and the Reformation

The printing press was the medium through which the Reformation was conducted — and Elizabeth Eisenstein’s comparative argument about Protestant reform movements illuminates why it succeeded in the 16th century when earlier attempts had not. The Lollard reform movement of the 14th and early 15th centuries — Wycliffe’s followers, who produced the first English Bible in manuscript — was suppressed within a generation. The Lutheran Reformation a century later, with analogous theological emphases (vernacular scripture, rejection of clerical mediation, denial of transubstantiation), became irreversible across half of Europe. Eisenstein’s argument is that the difference was print: once Luther’s ideas were fixed in printed form and distributed across the networks of literate clergy, merchants, and humanist scholars, suppression became structurally harder than it had been for manuscript heresies. A bishop could confiscate and burn manuscripts; he could not recover every printed copy already distributed. “The first movement to fully exploit the printing press for mass communication” was Protestant — and it could not be undone. ^[source: eisenstein_printing_press_raw.txt] Cromwell commissioned the first official English Bible (the Coverdale Bible, 1535; the Great Bible, 1539, placed in every parish church). Every subsequent religious change required print: Protestant polemics against Rome, Catholic responses, the successive Prayer Books, Protestant martyrologies, and anti-Protestant responses. Printers who served the successive religious regimes — Grafton and Whitchurch who printed Cromwell’s Great Bible; Berthelet who served as Henry’s royal printer; the printers who produced Edwardian Protestant material and then scrambled under Mary — navigated an exceptionally dangerous market.

The Stationers’ Company’s 1557 charter — the founding document of the English press-control apparatus — was itself a response to the proliferation of Protestant and Catholic polemic in the preceding decades. The charter was granted by Mary to create a mechanism for Catholic censorship of Protestant printing. The profound irony is that when Elizabeth came to the throne in 1558, the same institutional apparatus — the Stationers’ Company monopoly, the licensing system, the wardens’ authority to search and seize — was simply redirected to suppress Catholic printing. The machinery built for one religious purpose was redeployed for its exact opposite without structural modification. Ames/Herbert’s Typographical Antiquities traces the careers of the printers who served the successive regimes; each change of monarch required printers to adapt or face suppression. ^[source: typographical_antiquities_ames_raw.txt, stationers_registers_arber_vol1_raw.txt, hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt]

The Mantel Trilogy and the Reformation

Hilary Mantel’s Cromwell trilogy is the most sustained fictional treatment of the English Reformation as a human experience rather than a theological or constitutional abstraction. In Wolf Hall, the Reformation is something that happens through the accumulation of individual decisions — Cromwell’s drafting, Henry’s will, Cranmer’s compliance. In Bring Up the Bodies, the dissolution is prefigured by the stripping of Wolsey (rooms inventoried, tapestries rolled up). In The Mirror and the Light, the dissolution is shown at the granular level of abbots hanged in their own churches, monks dispersed, buildings stripped of their lead and stone within years of suppression. The trilogy does not evaluate the Reformation as progress or loss; it renders it as the consequence of power and self-interest operating in a specific political configuration — one in which Cromwell was simultaneously the architect and, ultimately, one of its victims. ^[source: wolf_hall_raw.txt, the_mirror_and_the_light_raw.txt]
