Drake’s Caribbean operations: the 1568 San Juan de Ulúa betrayal (✕); the 1572–73 Nombre de Dios raid and Isthmus crossing with the Maroons (gold dashed); the 1585 Indies Voyage sacking San Domingo and Cartagena (teal). The isthmus overland route is where Drake first sighted the Pacific.

Elizabethan privateering was state-licensed maritime raiding against enemy shipping and ports, conducted by private individuals under royal commission. It occupied the legal and strategic space between piracy (unlicensed, criminal) and formal naval warfare (state-organized, publicly declared). Elizabeth’s government used privateering as an instrument of foreign policy — attacking Spanish economic infrastructure while maintaining formal peace and plausible deniability. The careers of John Hawkins and Francis Drake defined the practice, and its methods — absorbed into fleet tactics by the Armada campaign — transformed English naval strategy. Corbett calls Hawkins the “exemplary merchant-prince” of the Elizabethan maritime world.

Legal Structure: Letters of Marque and Minor Remedies

Privateers operated under letters of marque — royal commissions authorizing the holder to attack ships (and sometimes ports) of a named enemy. Corbett notes that the letter of marque belonged to what lawyers called “minor remedies” in international law: technically lawful redress for grievances (like the unpunished Spanish attack at San Juan de Ulúa), but calibrated to stop well short of formal war. The letter specified the scope of the commission; attacks within its terms were legal; attacks outside them were piracy. In practice the line was difficult to police, and Elizabeth’s government was content with ambiguity: Drake’s circumnavigation (1577–80) operated under secret royal sanction that could be denied if Spain complained, while the profits flowed to both the queen and the investors. ^[source: corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol1_raw.txt]

Hawkins and the Slaving Voyages (1562–68)

John Hawkins conducted three slaving voyages to West Africa and the Spanish Caribbean between 1562 and 1568, pioneering the triangular Atlantic trade. He bought or seized Africans on the Guinea coast, transported and sold them in Spanish colonial ports (illegally, since Spain prohibited foreign trade in its colonies), and returned with silver and trade goods. The third voyage ended in disaster at San Juan de Ulúa (1568), where the Spanish viceroy launched a surprise assault on the English squadron sheltering under a negotiated truce. Most English ships and men were lost; Drake, commanding the bark Judith (50 tons), escaped ahead of Hawkins. Hawkins later accused Drake of deserting him in the crisis. The survivors left behind — some died in Spanish dungeons, one was burned at an auto-da-fé — became a documented grievance that English diplomatic correspondence cited for years. ^[source: corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol1_raw.txt]

San Juan de Ulua as Founding Grievance

San Juan de Ulúa established the emotional and political framework for Drake’s entire subsequent career. The Spanish had violated a flag-of-truce agreement; the English had been betrayed under formal protection. Camden recorded that sailors across England “fretted and demanded war” when the outcome became known. For Drake personally, the event was the founding grievance he spent his career repaying. For Walsingham and the Protestant war party at court, the failure to obtain diplomatic satisfaction reinforced the argument that only active military pressure on Spain could protect English interests. ^[source: corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol1_raw.txt]

Drake’s Caribbean Operations and the Maroon Alliance (1572–73)

Drake’s first independent command was the raid on Nombre de Dios — the Atlantic terminus of the Peruvian silver route on the Isthmus of Panama. He took the harbour but was wounded before reaching the treasure house. He then allied with Maroon communities — escaped African slaves, fugitives from Spanish colonial labour, who had established free communities in the Panamanian interior. Their leader Pedro brought Drake accounts of how their towns had been sacked and their people killed or captured; Drake treated them with genuine respect and they trusted him. Sugden notes the Maroon alliance was unprecedented in English privateering: Drake using the empire’s own victims against it.

Drake scuttled his brother’s ship (Swan) during the overland operations — a calculated ruthlessness, stripping it for stores and men so the pinnaces could be properly manned. Operating overland across the Isthmus with the Maroons as guides, Drake’s party intercepted a mule train of Peruvian silver from Panama. He climbed a tree from which he could see both the Atlantic and Pacific simultaneously and vowed to sail upon the Pacific — a vow fulfilled five years later in the circumnavigation. The campaign established his operational style: intelligence, local alliance, speed, and surprise rather than frontal assault. ^[source: corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol1_raw.txt, sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

The Circumnavigation as Global Privateering (1577–80)

The circumnavigation voyage was a privateering operation at global scale. The true objective — raiding the undefended Spanish Pacific coast — was kept secret from the Spanish ambassador. Drake scuttled two more ships during the outward passage to consolidate his force, demonstrating again his willingness to sacrifice assets for operational efficiency. He captured the treasure galleon Nuestra Señora de la Concepción (Cacafuego) in the Pacific, leaving the Golden Hind “literally ballasted with silver” after six days of transferring cargo. The voyage returned approximately 200,000 ducats to the queen and investors. Walsingham was among the private investors; Elizabeth knighted Drake at Deptford in 1581. The circumnavigation demonstrated that English sea power could reach every ocean — and that Spanish Pacific possessions were indefensible. ^[source: corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol1_raw.txt, walsingham_raw.txt]

The Indies Voyage and Transition to State Warfare (1585)

The Indies Voyage of 1585 — approximately 2,300 men, sacking San Domingo and Cartagena — marked the transition from privateering to state-directed amphibious warfare. Drake operated under a royal commission, with royal ships and government strategic objectives: destroying the infrastructure of Spanish Atlantic power. The voyage was a direct response to Philip II’s embargo on English ships in Spanish ports; Corbett argues it constitutes the beginning of the Elizabethan naval war. The private enterprise model of privateering was being absorbed into state strategy. The Indies Voyage directly precipitated Philip II’s decision to build the Armada. ^[source: corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol2_raw.txt]

Walsingham as Patron and Strategic Architect

Francis Walsingham was the court patron without whom English privateering could not have operated at its Elizabethan scale. He invested in the circumnavigation, supported Drake’s commissions against Spain, provided intelligence about harbour vulnerabilities, and pressed Elizabeth to fund offensive operations. The career of privateering as an instrument of foreign policy was inseparable from Walsingham’s strategic vision: active military pressure on Spain as the only reliable guarantee of Protestant England’s security. When Walsingham died in April 1590 and Leicester in 1588, Drake lost both his primary court patrons and the strategic framework that had sustained his career. ^[source: walsingham_raw.txt, corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol1_raw.txt, corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol2_raw.txt]

Legacy: From Privateering to Naval Strategy

Corbett’s thesis is that English privateering was not merely commerce raiding but the practical school in which English naval strategy was developed. Drake’s tactical innovations — line-ahead formation, sustained broadside gunnery, fire-ships — were learned in the Caribbean and refined in the Channel. The Armada campaign of 1588 was the moment when the methods of privateering were applied to fleet action at national scale. After 1588, Spain professionalised its convoy escorts (the Indian Guard and Tertia squadrons) and the opportunities for classic privateering shrank. Drake’s final voyage (1595–96) failed because the strategic world had adapted to him. ^[source: corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol1_raw.txt, corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol2_raw.txt]
