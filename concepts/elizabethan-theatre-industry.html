The Elizabethan theatre industry was a commercial entertainment business that developed from the 1570s onwards — purpose-built playhouses, professional acting companies organised as joint-stock enterprises, and a repertory system that produced new plays at a rate no other theatrical tradition in history has matched. It operated under a dual censorship apparatus: performances were licensed by the Master of the Revels (an officer of the royal household); publication of dramatic texts required entry in the Stationers’ Register (implying ecclesiastical approval). Evelyn May Albright’s Dramatic Publication in England, 1580–1640 is the foundational scholarly analysis of the relationship between the theatrical industry and the print trade; Greenblatt’s Will in the World provides the literary-biographical context.

Legal Framework and Licensing Requirements

The legal framework for professional theatre was established through a sequence of statutes and council orders:

  • 1572 Act for the Punishment of Vagabonds: Required all actors to have a noble patron or face prosecution as vagabonds. This forced companies to attach themselves to aristocratic households, creating the naming convention (Lord Chamberlain’s Men, Lord Admiral’s Men) that governed the industry until James I’s patronage system absorbed the major companies directly into the royal household.
  • 1578 Privy Council Order: Named six specific playing companies permitted to perform in or near London, effectively creating a licensed oligopoly. Companies outside this list operated in legal uncertainty.
  • 1581 Commission to the Master of the Revels: Formalised the Master’s authority to license all plays intended for public performance — extending from court entertainment licensing (the office’s original purpose) to the entire public commercial theatre. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

The Companies

Playing companies were organised as partnerships of “sharers” — actors who owned shares in the company’s costumes, scripts, and equipment — supplemented by hired actors and boy apprentices who played female roles. There was an important distinction between sharers (who participated in profits and losses) and housekeepers (who owned the physical playhouse building and received a fixed proportion of receipts regardless of artistic success). In practice, the leading companies’ sharers and housekeepers overlapped: Shakespeare and his colleagues in the Lord Chamberlain’s Men were both sharers in the company and housekeepers in the Globe.

The major companies:

  • Lord Chamberlain’s Men (from 1594): Shakespeare’s company; shareholders included Shakespeare (approximately one-tenth share), Richard Burbage, and others. From 1603 they became the King’s Men under James I’s direct patronage.
  • Lord Admiral’s Men: The rival company, managed by Philip Henslowe, at the Rose and Fortune theatres; Christopher Marlowe’s plays were part of their repertory.

Companies built their repertory through purchasing scripts from playwrights, performing each play a limited number of times before moving to the next, and jealously guarding their scripts against competitors. A company’s scripts were its capital. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt, greenblatt_will_in_the_world_raw.txt]

Playwright Contracts and the Economic Structure

Playwrights sold their scripts outright to companies, with no ongoing royalty or residual rights. A 1589 agreement (associated with the Admiral’s Men) specified that the playwright sold the manuscript absolutely — any further profit from performance or publication belonged to the company. Henslowe’s Diary (the financial and contractual ledger of Philip Henslowe, operator of the Rose and Fortune theatres) records the specific terms:

  • Thomas Heywood had a two-year exclusive contract with the Admiral’s Men for approximately £40, during which he could not write for any other company.
  • A Dawes agreement specified a profit-share arrangement for a particular series of plays.
  • Individual script prices ranged from £4 to £10 in the 1590s; collaborative scripts (written by two or more dramatists) were common and similarly priced.

The playwright had no legal claim to the text once sold. This is the economic structure Patterson cites when arguing that the author’s position in Elizabethan copyright law was effectively non-existent: the stationer owned the print rights, the company owned the performance rights, and the playwright owned nothing after the first sale. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

The Playhouses

The first purpose-built London playhouse was The Theatre, built by James Burbage in Shoreditch in 1576. The industry expanded rapidly:

  • The Rose (1587): Philip Henslowe’s playhouse on Bankside; the Admiral’s Men’s base.
  • The Globe (1599): Built by the Chamberlain’s Men using the timbers of the dismantled Theatre (transported across the Thames on a snowy December 1598 night by Peter Streete). Shakespeare held approximately one-tenth share; the Globe carried the Hercules device and the motto Totus mundus agit histrionem — “The whole world plays the actor.”
  • The Fortune (1600): The Admiral’s Men’s new house, built to compete with the Globe.
  • The Blackfriars (1608): An indoor playhouse enabling year-round playing regardless of weather.

The playhouses were outside the City of London (in the Bankside liberty and in Shoreditch) partly because the City corporation was hostile to theatrical entertainment and would not grant playing licences within the walls. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt, greenblatt_will_in_the_world_raw.txt]

Plague and Closures

Playing companies were acutely vulnerable to plague closures. The Privy Council ordered theatres closed whenever plague deaths in London rose above a threshold of approximately thirty per week. Major closure periods during the 1590s disrupted company finances severely and forced companies to tour the provinces — a less profitable substitute that also removed plays from London audiences. The 1592–94 outbreak was the longest closure of the decade; it was during this enforced gap in London playing that Shakespeare wrote and published his narrative poems (Venus and Adonis, 1593; The Rape of Lucrece, 1594) through Richard Field. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt, greenblatt_will_in_the_world_raw.txt]

The Dual Censorship Apparatus

Albright’s central analytical contribution is her detailed account of the two licensing systems operating simultaneously:

Performance licensing (the Master of the Revels): All plays intended for public performance required the Master’s approval. Edmund Tilney (Master 1578–1610) and George Buc (1610–22) are the most documented. The Master read scripts, required alterations, and could suppress plays entirely. The Sir Thomas More manuscript (Harley 7368) survives with Tilney’s censorship marks throughout — he struck the Ill May Day scenes dealing with foreign immigrants and required their replacement, providing direct documentary evidence of how performance censorship operated at the manuscript level.

Publication licensing (the Stationers’ Register): A play published as a quarto required entry in the Stationers’ Register, implying approval from ecclesiastical licensers. The two systems were distinct and could diverge: a play licensed for performance was not automatically approvable for print (which reached a broader audience), and the ecclesiastical licensers sometimes required cuts for publication that had not been required for performance. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt, stationers_registers_arber_vol2_raw.txt, stationers_registers_arber_vol3_raw.txt]

Companies and Publication Strategy

Theatrical companies had ambivalent relationships with the publication of their plays. Publication had advantages (revenue, advertising) but risks (a competitor could purchase the printed quarto and perform the play, bypassing the company’s exclusivity). Albright shows that companies used the Stationers’ Register strategically:

  • Blocking entries (“staying”): Entering a play in the Register without intending immediate publication, to prevent a rival publisher from registering and printing it. The 1600 entries “to be staied” for As You Like It, Henry V, Much Ado About Nothing, and Jonson’s Every Man in His Humor are Albright’s primary example — interpreted as ownership claims, not suppressions.
  • Authorised vs pirated quartos: Some quartos were authorised by the company (relatively accurate text); others appear to have been reconstructed from memory by actors or stenographers — the so-called “bad quartos.” A.W. Pollard’s influential bibliographical theory (1909) distinguished “good” and “bad” quartos based on textual characteristics, attributing bad quartos to memorial reconstruction. Subsequent scholarship has been skeptical of applying this distinction mechanically, since the evidence for any specific transmission history is rarely conclusive.
  • The First Folio (1623): 36 Shakespeare plays assembled after his death by his former colleagues John Heminges and Henry Condell, entered in the Register by Blount and Jaggard — 18 of them previously unregistered. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt, stationers_registers_arber_vol3_raw.txt, stationers_registers_arber_vol4_raw.txt]

The Print Trade and Drama

Certain Stationers’ Company members specialised in publishing dramatic texts: John Charlewood, Thomas Creede, George Eld, and Edward Allde were regular publishers of play quartos. Print runs for dramatic quartos were typically 800–1,000 copies — smaller than non-dramatic texts — and the economics were marginal. Companies that authorised publication received a one-off fee rather than a continuing share of sales; the stationer retained all print-sale profit. Albright documents that even theatrically successful plays did not automatically translate into print success, and that the two markets (playhouse audiences and reading publics) were only partially overlapping. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

The 1599 Bishops’ Order and the Essex Affair

The 1599 Bishops’ Order — issued by the Archbishop of Canterbury and Bishop of London on June 1, 1599, and delivered to fourteen named printers on June 4 — included a provision that “no plays or satires” be printed without special authority, that English histories not be printed without Privy Council approval, and that the works of Nashe and Harvey be burned. Richard Field was among the fourteen named printers. The order reflects the sensitivity of dramatic and satirical publication at a moment of political tension (Essex was at the height of his influence and on the eve of his Irish disaster). The 1601 Richard II performance — staged on Essex’s behalf with the deposition scene — demonstrated that theatrical performance could be deployed as a direct political instrument; the subsequent investigation of the actors did not result in prosecutions. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_vol3_raw.txt, hume_history_tudor_vol2_raw.txt]
