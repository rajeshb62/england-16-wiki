Conyers Read, Mr Secretary Walsingham and the Policy of Queen Elizabeth. First published Oxford/Clarendon Press, 1925; reprinted Archon Books, 1967. ~1.12 MB, 30,564 lines extracted from PDF (Internet Archive scan, Trent University Library copy). The foundational scholarly biography of Walsingham: archivally exhaustive, thematically organised by policy domain rather than chronologically, still unreplaced as the standard life.

Covers Walsingham’s career from his Protestant formation and Marian exile through his French embassy (1570–1573), his tenure as Principal Secretary (1573–1590), and his death in poverty in 1590. Read’s organizing argument is that Elizabethan foreign policy cannot be understood without reconstructing the factional politics of the Privy Council — specifically the sustained division between the activist Protestant interventionist caucus (Leicester, Walsingham, Sidney) and the cautious pragmatist caucus (Burghley, Sussex). Walsingham consistently occupied the former position, and the gap between his policy recommendations and Elizabeth’s decisions is the book’s central tension throughout. ^[source: walsingham_raw.txt]

Contents — Chapter-by-Chapter

Preface (lines 98–268)

Read explains his thematic rather than chronological organisation: Walsingham’s personal papers do not survive in the way Burghley’s do, and the gaps in the record require him to reconstruct the man from dispatches, state papers, and the correspondence of others. He introduces the Leicester/Burghley factional framework as the essential key: most of Elizabeth’s major foreign policy decisions are explicable only as resolutions of a recurrent tension between these two camps, with the queen characteristically mediating between them — accepting neither the full interventionist programme nor the full withdrawal that Burghley sometimes counselled. ^[source: walsingham_raw.txt]

Ch I: Early Life (lines 523–5237)

Walsingham’s family ancestry is traced from the town of Walsingham in Norfolk — a great medieval pilgrimage shrine destroyed in 1538 under Henry VIII. The family had been prosperous London citizens since the fifteenth century (Alan Walsingham, cordwainer, 1403). Sir Francis’s father died young; his mother remarried Sir John Carey, which gave young Walsingham a connection to the powerful Carey family network and, through them, to the Protestant inner circle.

Protestant formation: Walsingham went up to King’s College Cambridge (1548–1550) under Edward VI’s Protestant regime. He subsequently studied law in the Italian states (Padua was a common destination for English Protestant lawyers), acquiring languages and the Italian diplomatic intelligence methods he would later systematize. When Mary I came to the throne in 1553 and began the Catholic restoration, Walsingham went into exile on the Continent — part of the substantial Protestant émigré community based in Frankfurt, Zurich, and Strasbourg. This exile shaped him permanently: he returned to England in 1558 convinced that Catholicism was not only a theological error but a political threat coordinated from Rome and Madrid. He knew, because he had lived among them, how the Protestant international worked — and by extension how Catholic intelligence could work.

His legal and diplomatic education, his Continental languages, his connections in the Protestant exile community, and his personal encounter with Catholic persecution made him, by 1558, precisely the kind of man the Elizabethan regime needed for delicate intelligence work abroad. He came to Elizabeth’s service through his connexion with William Cecil, entered Parliament in 1563, and was used on several minor diplomatic missions before the Paris embassy. ^[source: walsingham_raw.txt]

Ch II: The French Embassy (lines 5238–12223)

Walsingham was sent as ambassador to Paris in 1570, at a moment of acute anxiety for Elizabeth. The Rising of the North (1569), the papal bull Regnans in Excelsis (February 1570) excommunicating Elizabeth and releasing her subjects from allegiance, the assassination of the Scottish Regent Murray, and the revival of the Marian faction in Scotland all seemed to Elizabeth to be manifestations of “some large design against her.” The French embassy was the pivot of her response: she needed to know whether France would join Spain in a Catholic crusade against England, or whether the current weakness of the French crown (civil war, weak king) offered an opportunity for an Anglo-French alliance.

Read’s account of these negotiations is detailed and shows Walsingham’s distinctive operating style: he was not a conciliator but an analyst. Where most Elizabethan diplomats sought to smooth relations, Walsingham sought to force clarity — he wanted to know whether France was England’s friend or enemy, and he pressed for terms that would expose the answer. His reports to Burghley from Paris are the most valuable part of this chapter: precise, pessimistic, and often ignored by Elizabeth, who preferred ambiguity.

The context of the massacre: Coligny’s rise at the French court and his advocacy of a joint Anglo-French war against Spain gave Walsingham genuine hope in 1572. The Treaty of Blois (April 1572) and the Navarre marriage — between Henry of Navarre (Protestant) and Margaret of Valois — seemed to fulfil Coligny’s programme. Walsingham believed a Protestant coalition capable of challenging Spain was almost in being. The assassination attempt on Coligny in August 1572 shattered that hope. ^[source: walsingham_raw.txt]

Ch III: The Treaty of Blois (lines 12224–14066)

The Anglo-French defensive alliance signed at Blois in April 1572 was the diplomatic achievement of the embassy, but Read shows it as fragile from the start. Two obstacles dominated the negotiations: the etiamsi religionis causa clause (whether the alliance would hold even if one party were attacked for religious reasons — England needed this, France resisted it) and the Scottish question (France would not abandon Mary Stuart’s cause; England would not recognize the Marian faction as the legitimate government of Scotland).

Walsingham’s role in the Blois negotiations was primarily as an intelligence gatherer and analyst, not a principal negotiator. Read uses his dispatches to show how the diplomatic sausage was made: the French insisted on keeping Scotland out of the alliance’s protection; the English accepted terms that left that ambiguity unresolved because they needed the alliance more than the French did. The treaty was less a settlement than a suspension of hostility — which is all it remained when the massacre ended its value as a Protestant coalition instrument. ^[source: walsingham_raw.txt]

Ch IV: The Massacre of St. Bartholomew’s (lines 14067–18827)

He was lodging in the Faubourg-Saint-Germain with his wife, his young daughter, and his guest — the young Philip Sidney, who had come to France with the Earl of Lincoln. His house was across the river from the centre of the killing. In the night of 23–24 August he heard the church bells of Saint-Germain-l’Auxerrois ring out faintly, too distant to wake him. By morning the sounds across the river were unmistakable — shoutings, bells, pistol shots, the clash of arms.

Walsingham sent a friend to Count Montgomery to inquire. Montgomery said the King had suppressed a small disorder and placed guards around the Admiral’s house. Before night, Walsingham knew the truth from the streams of terrified Englishmen — and Dutch, German, and a few Huguenots — who poured to his doors. Read quotes Timothy Bright’s dedication to Walsingham (1589) describing the embassy as “a very sanctuary, not only for all of our nation, but even to many strangers then in peril.” The Spanish ambassador’s report alleged that Walsingham’s house was actually attacked by the mob and his life saved only by concealment.

The king’s audience (1 September 1572): Charles IX sent a guard of twelve armed gentlemen to conduct Walsingham to the Louvre. The king explained the massacre as the suppression of a Huguenot plot against the royal family. He proposed to send the results of trials of Coligny and his associates to Elizabeth. Walsingham’s self-control in this interview is remarkable: he thanked Charles for his care of English subjects and complained only of the murder of three Englishmen. He said nothing else of the massacre. He wrote home guardedly, fearing his letters might be intercepted, and “confided the account of the massacre to the messenger’s memory.”

Lasting effect: Read argues that the massacre permanently hardened Walsingham’s position. He had come to Paris believing a Protestant coalition was possible; he left knowing that the French crown could never be trusted as an ally of Protestant England. From 1572 onward his policy recommendations were shaped by this conviction: England must build its own Protestant defensive capacity rather than rely on continental alliances. ^[source: walsingham_raw.txt]

Ch V: French Affairs, 1573–1578 (lines 18828–22068)

Walsingham’s first years as Principal Secretary (appointed 1573). He returned from France a man of established reputation and genuine Protestant celebrity — his shelter of refugees in the Paris embassy was known throughout the Protestant world. His intelligence work in France continued through agents: he maintained a network of informants in the French court, among the Huguenot leadership, and in Catholic émigré communities in Paris and Lyon.

The chapter covers the continuing instability of French politics under the weak Charles IX and then Henry III, the ongoing Huguenot wars, and Walsingham’s efforts to maintain some Protestant leverage in France despite Elizabeth’s reluctance to commit money or troops. He corresponded with William of Orange, with the Huguenot leadership, and with Protestant princes in Germany — building what Read calls an informal “Protestant international” that existed parallel to Elizabeth’s official diplomacy and sometimes contradicted it. ^[source: walsingham_raw.txt]

Ch VI: Dutch Affairs, 1573–1578 (lines 22069–26432)

Walsingham’s most sustained advocacy. William of Orange and the Dutch rebels fighting Spain represented, in Walsingham’s view, England’s best strategic buffer: if Philip II crushed the revolt and re-established control of the Netherlands coast from Dunkirk to Flushing, England would face a hostile power across the Channel with access to the best harbours for an invasion fleet. He pressed Elizabeth repeatedly to give the rebels financial support, military support, or both.

Elizabeth’s counter-argument — that she could not afford to provoke a war with Spain, that supporting rebels against their legitimate sovereign was a dangerous precedent, and that the rebels might simply turn to France if England’s help was insufficient — receives a fair hearing in Read’s account. The chapter is notable for the quality of Walsingham’s correspondence with Leicester: his letters reveal frustration, despair, and eventually a kind of resigned professionalism. “Seeing it seemeth to be a thing decreed by Him that governeth all things, we are to digest the same with patience and pray unto Him, who is hope against hope, to give another issue of success of these causes than in reason or policy is to be looked for.” This is Walsingham’s characteristic mode: Protestant providentialist fatalism underneath the administrative tenacity. ^[source: walsingham_raw.txt]

Ch VII: The Embassy to the Low Countries (lines 26433–~27500)

Walsingham’s mission to the Netherlands in 1578, jointly with Lord Cobham, to negotiate between the Spanish governor Don John and the rebel States-General. The mission was a failure: Elizabeth wanted a negotiated peace that would end the costs of the conflict without committing England; the States-General wanted English troops and money; Don John wanted to crush the rebellion without English interference. None of the parties’ actual requirements were compatible.

The episode reveals a permanent structural feature of Elizabethan foreign policy: Elizabeth would send expensive, high-status missions to achieve diplomatic goals that required military commitment to be credible. Walsingham understood this; he wrote home that the States would not treat seriously with ambassadors who could not promise real support. Elizabeth censured the ambassadors for failing to prevent an accord between the Estates and the Duke of Alençon — an accord she had herself made inevitable by withholding the support that might have given England leverage. The chapter includes one of the book’s most characteristic Walsingham moments: receiving Elizabeth’s letter refusing troops, refusing money, and offering only to guarantee a loan of £28,000 against plate and jewels, Walsingham simply notes the letter and continues his mission. ^[source: walsingham_raw.txt]

Ch VIII: The Second Alençon Courtship

Francis, Duke of Alençon (brother of Henry III), renewed his suit for Elizabeth’s hand in 1578–1581. The Alençon courtship was the most dangerous political crisis Walsingham faced as Secretary, because Elizabeth appears to have genuinely considered the marriage and Walsingham was ferociously opposed to it. His grounds were religious and strategic: Alençon was Catholic; a marriage alliance with France would compromise the Protestant character of Elizabeth’s regime, divide the Privy Council, and potentially deliver England into French influence. He circulated a memorandum to the Council enumerating objections; he worked with the Puritan pamphleteers (including John Stubbs, who lost his hand for it) to create public pressure against the match. Elizabeth was furious at the interference and briefly contemplated having Walsingham prosecuted.

Read’s account shows Elizabeth at her most decisive and most alien to Walsingham’s worldview: she seems to have genuinely wanted to marry Alençon, genuinely valued the diplomatic weight a French marriage would give her, and genuinely resented her servants’ presumptuousness in telling her whom she could and could not marry. The courtship eventually collapsed (1582) from a combination of Dutch objections, Alençon’s death (1584), and Elizabeth’s characteristic inability to commit to any permanent course of action. ^[source: walsingham_raw.txt]

Ch IX: Scottish Affairs, 1570–1585

Mary Queen of Scots in English custody was the central security problem of the Elizabethan regime. As long as she lived she was a focus for Catholic conspiracy: every plot against Elizabeth — Ridolfi (1571), Throckmorton (1583), Babington (1586) — involved some version of a plan to put Mary on the English throne. Walsingham’s handling of the Scottish dimension of this problem ran on two tracks: diplomatic management of the successive Scottish regents (Lennox, Mar, Morton, Arran) to maintain a Protestant pro-English government in Edinburgh; and intelligence operations to monitor Mary’s correspondence and identify her English supporters.

The Bond of Association (1584) — a document by which Protestant nobles pledged to kill anyone who benefited from an assassination of Elizabeth — was partly Walsingham’s instrument. It committed the Protestant political nation to ensuring that Mary’s death would follow Elizabeth’s murder, removing the incentive for conspirators to kill the queen if they could count on Mary succeeding her. Read shows the legal and political difficulties the Bond created: it was technically extra-judicial mob vengeance dressed as constitutional loyalty. Parliament eventually transformed it into an Act for the Queen’s Safety (1585) with proper judicial procedure — but Walsingham had his extra-judicial safeguard too, and he used it when the Babington crisis broke. ^[source: walsingham_raw.txt]

Ch X: Catholics and Puritans

The dual domestic security problem. From 1574 the seminary priests from Douai and Rome began infiltrating England, trained specifically for clandestine ministry to recusant households. From 1580 the Jesuits — Campion and Parsons — followed, better organized and more dangerous. Walsingham’s intelligence network tracked them: his agents penetrated the seminaries in France and Italy, identified priests before they crossed to England, and maintained lists of the Catholic households that sheltered them. His use of informants inside the Catholic community was systematic and included double agents operating in both directions.

His Puritan sympathies are a genuine and complicating element of Read’s portrait. Walsingham personally favoured further Protestant reformation of the English church — he was dissatisfied with the Elizabethan settlement’s retention of Catholic liturgical forms and episcopal hierarchy. He gave covert support to Puritan preachers and protected some of the Presbyterian agitators whom Archbishop Whitgift was persecuting in the 1580s. This made him suspect to Elizabeth (who hated Puritanism as a threat to royal authority over the church) and created an internal contradiction in his own position: he was the government’s chief instrument for suppressing Catholic dissent while quietly sympathizing with Protestant dissent. ^[source: walsingham_raw.txt]

Ch XI: Plots and Counter Plots

The Ridolfi Plot (1571) — involving the Duke of Norfolk, Mary, and Philip II in a plan to assassinate Elizabeth, free Mary, and invade with Spanish forces from the Netherlands — was the first major conspiracy Walsingham’s intelligence methods helped to unravel, though he was still in Paris during its exposure. The Throckmorton Plot (1583) was the first he managed directly as Secretary: his agents in France identified Francis Throckmorton as a courier between Mary, the French ambassador Mendoza, and the Guise faction planning an invasion. Throckmorton was arrested, tortured, and eventually executed; Mendoza was expelled from England.

Read’s treatment of Walsingham’s intelligence methodology is the most analytically valuable section of the book. His basic technique was network penetration rather than cryptanalysis: he placed agents inside the Catholic network who could identify couriers, safe houses, and principals. He also used Thomas Phelippes, his cipher expert, to decrypt Mary’s correspondence — but the decryption was only possible because Walsingham’s agents had first identified and intercepted the channels through which the letters moved. The intelligence operation was a system: identify the network, penetrate it, redirect the communications to pass through Phelippes’s hands, and wait for a conspiracy to expose itself. ^[source: walsingham_raw.txt]

Ch XII: The Babington Plot

The decisive operation. Anthony Babington and a group of young Catholic gentry were planning to assassinate Elizabeth and free Mary — a design that required Mary’s assent. Walsingham’s agent Robert Poley penetrated the conspiracy from the beginning; his agent Gilbert Gifford created the beer-barrel letter channel through which Mary’s correspondence with Babington was conducted, with every letter passing through Phelippes for decryption and copying before delivery. Walsingham allowed the conspiracy to ripen until Mary had explicitly assented to the assassination of Elizabeth in writing. Only then did he move.

The postscript in Mary’s letter, asking Babington for the names of the six men designated to kill Elizabeth, was probably added by Phelippes after decryption — a forgery designed to extract the names from Babington before the arrests. Read is careful about this: he presents the evidence that the postscript was forged but does not assert it as certain. What is certain is that Walsingham allowed the plot to develop far enough to incriminate Mary beyond any judicial rescue, and that his management of the subsequent prosecution secured the evidence that took Mary to the scaffold at Fotheringay in February 1587.

Walsingham’s own verdict on the operation was characteristically ambivalent. He had achieved what he regarded as the security of the realm and the completion of a necessary act. But he had used methods — entrapment, forgery, the deliberate sacrifice of the conspirators’ lives to secure Mary’s — that left him personally uncomfortable. He wrote to Leicester after the executions that he wished the thing had been done differently, without specifying what “differently” meant. Read does not press the point; it is one of the book’s few moments of moral reticence. ^[source: walsingham_raw.txt]

Ch XIII: The Beginning of War, 1584–1586

The assassination of William of Orange (July 1584), the fall of Antwerp to Parma (August 1585), and Philip II’s seizure of English merchant ships in Spanish ports forced Elizabeth’s hand. The Treaty of Nonsuch (August 1585) committed England to sending an army to the Netherlands under Leicester. Walsingham had advocated this intervention for a decade; its arrival was a vindication but not a triumph — the terms were inadequate, the command structure was divided, and Leicester’s performance in the field was poor.

The chapter covers the logistics of the intervention: the recruitment and provisioning of Leicester’s army, the financial arrangements (England advanced money the Dutch were supposed to repay — they did not), the political complications when Leicester accepted the title of Governor-General from the States over Elizabeth’s explicit instructions. Walsingham’s role was primarily administrative and intelligence: he maintained his network of Continental agents throughout and continued to receive reports on Spanish preparations in the ports of Spain and Portugal. ^[source: walsingham_raw.txt]

Ch XIV: Scotland and France, 1585–1588

The final diplomatic effort before the Armada. James VI of Scotland, now governing in his own right, was potentially a crucial ally — his Protestant credentials were good but his financial dependence on Elizabeth made him tractable. Walsingham managed the Scottish relationship through agents and through the threat of withholding James’s English pension. The key moment was James’s response to the execution of his mother Mary (February 1587): he protested formally but did not break with England, because he could not afford to. Walsingham had calculated correctly that dynastic calculation would override filial sentiment.

French policy in these years was complicated by the Wars of the League — the Catholic League, backed by Spain and the Guises, was threatening to overwhelm Henry III. Walsingham corresponded with Henry of Navarre (the Protestant heir-presumptive) and supported a subsidy to the Huguenot cause. He correctly identified Henry of Navarre as England’s most important long-term asset in France — the man who, if he survived and came to the French throne, would provide a genuine counterweight to Spain. ^[source: walsingham_raw.txt]

Ch XV: The Coming of the Armada

Walsingham’s intelligence on Spanish preparations was extensive and, on the whole, accurate. His agents in Spain, Portugal, and the Netherlands — including the merchant Anthony Standen, operating in Lisbon — provided early warning of the Armada’s assembly, its likely composition, and the coordination planned with Parma. He forwarded these reports to the Council and to Drake, who used them as the basis of his argument for the Cádiz pre-emptive strike (April 1587).

Read’s account of the intelligence failure that preceded the Armada is balanced: the English did not fail to know the Armada was coming; they failed to know exactly when, and they underestimated the logistical difficulties that delayed it by over a year. The delay was partly Drake’s doing (the Cádiz raid), partly Philip’s administrative complexity, partly the death of Santa Cruz (February 1588). When the Armada finally sailed in July 1588, England was as prepared as it could be given its financial constraints. Walsingham was by this point seriously ill — he attended Council meetings irregularly — but he managed the intelligence side of the Armada campaign from his sickbed. ^[source: walsingham_raw.txt]

Ch XVI: The Year after the Armada

Walsingham’s health had been deteriorating since the mid-1580s — he suffered from a urinary disorder that caused him severe and chronic pain. The Portugal expedition (1589), which he supported and which Drake commanded, was the last major policy initiative he was associated with before his strength gave out. Its failure was a blow. He had hoped it would complete the Armada’s destruction and establish England’s naval dominance; instead it demonstrated the limits of what could be achieved by joint-stock naval expedition without sustained government investment.

He died in poverty on 6 April 1590. His debts were enormous — he had spent his own money on intelligence operations for which he was never reimbursed. Elizabeth refused to pay his funeral expenses; he was buried at night at St Paul’s without ceremony. His son-in-law Philip Sidney had died in 1586; his daughter Frances subsequently married the Earl of Essex — a connection that placed the Walsingham intelligence network at Essex’s disposal and would have unforeseen consequences after 1597. ^[source: walsingham_raw.txt]

Ch XVII: English Maritime Enterprise

Walsingham was an investor in and patron of exploration and privateering — a dimension of his career that connects him directly to the Drake and Frobisher enterprises. He was among the backers of Drake’s circumnavigation (1577–1580), of Frobisher’s Northwest Passage attempts, and of the Raleigh Virginia colony. Read treats this not as a hobby but as an extension of his strategic vision: England’s long-term security required the development of an oceanic trading and raiding capacity that would challenge Spain’s monopoly on American wealth and give England economic independence from the Spanish silver supply.

His intelligence networks overlapped with his commercial investments: several of his agents in Spain and Portugal were merchants whose trading contacts gave them access to information about ship movements, naval preparations, and colonial trade. The line between espionage and commerce in Elizabethan England was always porous, and Walsingham exploited this systematically. ^[source: walsingham_raw.txt]

Ch XVIII: Private Affairs

Walsingham’s household at Barn Elms (Surrey) was a centre of Protestant intellectual and political life: Sidney dedicated works to him, Edmund Spenser moved in his orbit, and Continental Protestant refugees found hospitality there. He educated his daughter Frances carefully; she married Philip Sidney in 1583 (an arrangement that was also a political alliance, linking Walsingham to the Sidney family’s prestige and Continental connections). Sidney died at Zutphen in 1586, leaving Frances a widow with an infant daughter and substantial debts — Sidney had spent lavishly and left little. Frances subsequently married the Earl of Essex, who inherited both her and Walsingham’s political networks and eventually used them in his own disastrous bid for power in 1601.

Read’s portrait of Walsingham’s private character is the most admiring in the book: he was genuinely devout (his Puritanism was conviction, not posture), personally incorruptible in an age of spectacular corruption, and, by the standards of Elizabethan statecraft, remarkably honest about his own failures and the limits of what intelligence could accomplish. He never acquired wealth; he spent what he had on the state’s service; he died with his accounts in chaos and his family in financial difficulty. His epitaph in St Paul’s recorded “Secretary of State to the most glorious Queen Elizabeth” — which is all he would have wanted. ^[source: walsingham_raw.txt]
