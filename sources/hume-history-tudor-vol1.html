David Hume, The History of England Under the House of Tudor, Vol. I. First published A. Millar, London, 1759. Digital edition: Oxford Text Archive, 2011. ~9.7 MB extracted from mobi via HTML. Covers Henry VII (1485–1509), Henry VIII (1509–1547), Edward VI (1547–1553), and Mary I (1553–1558).

Hume’s 18th-century narrative history of the Tudor dynasty’s first three reigns and the Marian restoration. The analysis is consistently Enlightenment in temper: religious zeal is always traced to self-interest or passion; rulers are judged by “prudence,” “enlarged views,” and “wisdom”; constitutional liberty is the implicit standard against which power is measured. Hume wrote against the partisan Whig and Tory historiographies of the previous century, and his Tudor volumes show this corrective ambition — he is sympathetic to neither Catholic nor Protestant bigotry, and sees both Henry VIII and Cranmer as figures of mixed capacity and rampant self-deception. ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt]

Contents and Coverage

Henry VII (3 Chapters; lines 218–3942)

Ch I — Accession and the problem of legitimacy (line 218)

Hume opens not with victory at Bosworth but with Henry’s dynastic predicament: his Lancastrian title is weak (derived through a legitimated but adulterous line), his right of conquest is invidious, and his claim through his wife Elizabeth of York would subordinate him to her. Hume argues Henry chose “present possession” — raw power solidified by survival — as his actual foundation, and that his lifelong antipathy to the Yorkist party was both a personal passion and a political mistake: by treating Yorkists as enemies he taught them to examine his title’s flaws and find them. Covers the marriage to Elizabeth of York, Lambert Simnel’s rebellion and the Battle of Stoke (1487), Perkin Warbeck’s first appearance, and the Duchess of Burgundy’s intrigues. ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt]

Ch II — Foreign affairs and Perkin Warbeck (line 1008)

European survey: Scotland under James III; Spain under Ferdinand and Isabella; the Low Countries under Maximilian; France under Charles VIII annexing Brittany. Hume analyses the shifting alliance logic of early Tudor diplomacy with characteristic attention to “interest” rather than honour or religion. The Perkin Warbeck imposture is treated as a serious diplomatic crisis, credible to European courts because Henry’s title was genuinely vulnerable. Trial and execution of Sir William Stanley for Warbeck complicity. ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt]

Ch III — Perkin’s end, Arthur, and Henry’s character (line 2180)

Perkin’s return from Scotland, the Cornish insurrection against taxation (Battle of Deptford Bridge), Perkin’s capture and execution, and the execution of the Earl of Warwick — the last legitimate Plantagenet — to clear the path for the Spanish match. Marriage of Prince Arthur to Katherine of Aragon; Arthur’s death; marriage of Princess Margaret to James IV of Scotland. Hume’s assessment of Henry VII: a king who accumulated immense treasure and maintained peace, but whose avarice and partisan spirit left him unloved and whose title never felt secure. His laws — Star Chamber, the uses statute, fiscal reform — are assessed as instruments of a centralising monarchy. ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt]

Henry VIII (7 Chapters; lines 3943–23166)

Ch I — The new reign and Wolsey’s rise (line 3943)

Henry VIII’s accession is greeted with widespread joy — the contrast with his father’s unloved final years is the chapter’s governing theme. Hume catalogues Henry’s gifts: handsome, learned, musical, athletic, apparently generous. Empson and Dudley are sacrificed to popular anger (Hume gives Empson’s defence of strict law-enforcement considerable weight). Wolsey emerges as the king’s minister through his willingness to accommodate Henry’s desire for pleasure and magnificence; Surrey does the same. The dissipation of Henry VII’s treasures begins at once. Battle of Flodden (1513) and its Scottish context. ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt]

Ch II — Wolsey’s administration and the Field of Cloth of Gold (line 5578)

Wolsey’s foreign policy in the context of the Habsburg-Valois struggle: the League of Cambrai, the Battle of Pavia (1525) and the captivity of Francis I, the Sack of Rome (1527) by imperial forces. Field of the Cloth of Gold (1520). Henry’s early opposition to Luther: he writes the Assertio Septem Sacramentorum, earns the title “Defender of the Faith.” Hume is sardonic about the theological pretensions of a king who would soon repudiate Rome. The divorce question begins to form in the background. ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt]

Ch III — Digression on the Ecclesiastical State (line 6784)

Hume suspends the narrative to argue that established churches are socially useful precisely because they bribe the clergy into indolence: competitive, market-driven religion produces enthusiasm, superstition, and faction, while an endowed establishment produces torpor — which is safer for the civil order. The Church of Rome failed on this logic by making the clergy too powerful, too wealthy, and answerable to a foreign potentate whose interests diverged from the nation’s. Protestantism failed in the opposite direction by releasing private judgment, which encourages the very enthusiasm that makes men ungovernable. ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt]

Ch IV — The Divorce and the Break with Rome (line 8860)

Detailed account of Henry’s divorce campaign: Katherine’s age and infirmities, the Leviticus argument (Hume notes drily that Henry “being so great a casuist and a divine, proceeded to examine the question by his own learning”), the papal politics complicated by Katherine’s nephew the Emperor Charles V, Wolsey’s manipulation of the question for his own ends, and Anne Boleyn’s role. Wolsey’s fall (1529) is traced to his failure to secure the divorce. Cranmer is appointed Archbishop. Parliament of 1529 begins the legislative Reformation. The Act of Supremacy (1534) is treated as a political rather than theological event. ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt]

Ch V — The Reformation’s human cost (line 10944)

Extended analysis of why people converted: not theological conviction but the pleasure of opposing authority, the fervor of new preachers, and resentment of clerical extortion. Henry’s religious position is anatomised: he hated Lutheran doctrine because Luther had insulted him personally, and he made the real presence a point of honour. The executions of John Fisher and Thomas More are given substantial treatment; Hume is respectful of both men’s integrity while criticising their inflexibility. The dissolution of the monasteries is assessed as economically and morally complex — monasteries were corrupt but also poor relief. Anne Boleyn’s fall (1536): Hume treats the charges of adultery with cautious scepticism. Birth of Edward; death of Jane Seymour. The Pilgrimage of Grace is mentioned but not developed at length. ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt]

Ch VI — The Six Articles and the fall of Cromwell (line 13693)

The Act of Six Articles (1539) — Hume reads it as Henry asserting his personal orthodoxy on the real presence against both Lutherans and Catholics. Lambert’s disputation with the king. Anne of Cleves: the marriage arranged by Cromwell, Henry’s disgust on meeting her, and the rapid fall of Cromwell. Hume’s assessment of Cromwell is notably hostile: he is an “artful and dextrous” minister but one who manipulated the king’s passions and enriched himself through the dissolution. The execution of Cromwell is presented as judicially corrupt but historically intelligible. Catherine Howard’s story and fall. ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt]

Ch VII — Scotland, France, and Henry’s death (line 15351)

Henry’s war with Scotland: the Battle of Solway Moss (1542), James V’s death (“The crown came with a woman, and it will go with one”), and the birth of Mary Queen of Scots. Hume quotes James’s dying words in full. War with France; the campaign that takes Boulogne. Execution of the Earl of Surrey for heraldic presumption. Henry’s final illness and death. Hume’s full character assessment: immense ability, brutal tyranny, theological vanity, but also the political genius that broke the power of the papacy and the nobility simultaneously. He sees Henry as the decisive figure in England’s transition from medieval to modern governance. ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt]

Ch I: Somerset as Protector — Hume opens the reign by noting the paradox that Henry’s will, designed to prevent any single minister seizing control, was immediately circumvented. The executors voted to name a Protector, and Somerset (Seymour) took the role. Gardiner’s principled resistance to the new homilies and to the dissolution of chantries is given extended treatment — Hume is sympathetic to Gardiner’s argument that wholesale religious change required parliamentary authority. The assassination of Cardinal Beaton in Scotland is treated as a consequence of his brutality toward reformers (George Wishart burned). Battle of Pinkie (1547): English victory over a larger Scottish force, won by combined artillery and cavalry. The young Mary Queen of Scots is sent to France for safety. Thomas Seymour’s intrigues against his brother Somerset — Hume’s account of how Seymour cultivated the young Edward VI and sought to outmanoeuvre the Protector — ends with Seymour’s attainder and execution, Somerset acquiescing in his brother’s death.

Ch II: Kett’s Rebellion (1549) — the agrarian rising in Norfolk, driven by enclosures and the collapse of the old manorial order. Hume takes Kett’s rebels seriously as a social phenomenon rather than mere disorder. Fall of Somerset: the council turns against the Protector after the double crisis of Kett’s rising and the western Prayer Book rebellion; Northumberland (Warwick) replaces him. Lady Jane Grey and the succession manoeuvre: Hume analyses the legal fragility of the device altering the succession and why it collapsed the moment Mary appeared in public as queen. Death of Edward VI and the brief nine-day reign of Jane. ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt]

Mary I (2 Chapters) — Readable after fix

^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt]

Ch I: Northumberland’s failure and Mary’s accession — the speed with which the political class deserted Lady Jane Grey once Mary raised her standard in Suffolk. Hume’s account of Northumberland’s arrest and recantation to Rome is notably sardonic (“Whether that such were his real sentiments, which he had formerly concealed, could not easily be determined”). Catholic restoration: Gardiner returned as chancellor; Bonner restored to London; the Edwardian bishops displaced. Marriage negotiations with Philip II of Spain and the parliamentary resistance. Wyatt’s Rebellion (1554): the Kentish rising against the Spanish match; Hume treats Wyatt as a genuine patriot rather than a dynastic schemer. Execution of Lady Jane Grey, whom Hume regards as an innocent casualty of others’ ambition.

Ch II: Hume’s philosophical digression on religious toleration. He argues toleration is a social policy question, not a theological one, and that persecution achieves nothing except creating martyrs, whose deaths convince more people than any preaching. The Marian burnings (Latimer, Ridley, Cranmer) are treated in this frame: Hume is horrified but analytical. Cranmer’s recantation and then his final withdrawal of it at the stake (“he stretched out his right hand into the flame, and held it there with a steady resolution”) is given full dramatic weight. War with France and the loss of Calais (1558). Death of Mary: Hume’s closing assessment is the harshest in the volume — she is judged to have accomplished nothing except making Catholicism synonymous with foreign domination and religious violence. ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt]
