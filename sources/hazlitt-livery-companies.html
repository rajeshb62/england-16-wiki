W. Carew Hazlitt, The Livery Companies of the City of London: Their Origin, Character, Development, and Social and Political Importance. London: Swan Sonnenschein & Co. / New York: Macmillan & Co., 1892. ~31,305 lines extracted from a zip of 724 OCR’d page files. Pennsylvania State University Libraries copy.

A comprehensive survey of all the London livery companies, written by Hazlitt of the Inner Temple in response to the Royal Commission on Livery Companies whose oral inquiry sat in 1882–83. Covers approximately seventy companies in individual chapters, preceded by a long Preface and General Introduction on the gild system from its medieval origins. Each company chapter typically includes: history and charter; governance (Master, Wardens, Court of Assistants, Livery, Yeomanry); finances and property; hall history; notable members; and Literary Notices — a bibliography of company-specific publications and documents. Hazlitt draws on the 1882 Return (companies’ replies to the Commission), Rivington’s Company records, and published company histories. ^[source: hazlitt_livery_companies_raw.txt]

Contents — Section by Section

Preface (lines ~1118–3290)

The Preface is Hazlitt’s response to the 1882–83 Royal Commission on Livery Companies, which had sat to inquire into whether the Companies should be disendowed or their funds redirected. His argument throughout is that the Companies remain “an indefeasible element of London life” regardless of their departure from original craft purposes, and that disendowment would destroy valuable social institutions.

The Preface covers: the history and operation of the Royal Commission and what its oral inquiries revealed; the Common Hall; the difference between Trust Estates and Corporate Estates; the Gild as forerunner of the poorhouse and hospital; the Irish Plantation and the Companies’ sub-shares (the Great Companies each took a proportion of the Ulster Plantation scheme from 1610; some Minor Companies took sub-shares from Great Companies); the Quo Warranto proceedings (the writ of Quo Warranto challenged individual charters from time to time; revocation by the Star Chamber, and restoration by Cromwell, are noted); the Yeomanry and their discontents; plurality (holding membership of more than one company); the City Freeholds and their diverse origins; the Great Fire of 1666 and its effects on company records and property; the Return of 1882 as Hazlitt’s primary statistical source; the scope and methods of the present book; and Mr. Welsh’s bibliography of company publications. ^[source: hazlitt_livery_companies_raw.txt]

General Introduction (lines ~3483–5688)

A history of the gild from its earliest forms in London through the consolidation of the livery company system.

The introduction begins with the two medieval gild types that underlie all London companies: the Gild Merchant (an aristocratic body of prosperous traders that eventually yielded to the popular Craft Gilds in all European towns) and the Craft Gild (a voluntary fraternity for mutual protection, common worship, the relief of poverty, and the regulation of a trade). In London the popular party’s victory over the patrician Gild Merchant was assured by Edward II. The parallel with Roman Collegia Opificum is drawn, and Hallam’s definition of the Gild — “fraternities by voluntary compact, to relieve each other in poverty, and to protect each other from injury. Two essential characteristics belonged to them: the common banquet and the common purse” — is quoted as the central description.

Specific topics covered:

  • Anglo-Saxon and Norman origins: the Frith Gild and Knighten Gild of London (the Knighten Gild, thirteen military persons given waste land east of London by King Edgar 958–68, was confirmed by Edward the Confessor; by Stow’s time its successor, the Prior of Holy Trinity Aldgate, still rode in procession with the mayor)
  • The constitution of the Gild: Master, Wardens, the Livery (senior freemen entitled to wear the company’s distinctive clothing), the Yeomanry (general body of freemen below the Livery), the Court of Assistants (the governing board)
  • Power of search: the usual accompaniment of incorporation — the right to search for and seize goods of inferior quality or unlicensed production
  • Apprenticeship: the standard route to freedom; the regulation of numbers and conditions
  • The word Mistery: from Mestier = craft or art; the Companies are properly “the Mysteries of [their trade]”
  • Amalgamating movement: some companies merged (e.g., Armourers and Braziers); others absorbed earlier Gilds
  • The Ulster Plantation: the mechanism by which the Great Companies became corporate landlords in Ireland from 1610; each was allocated a proportion of the plantation land at a purchase price of £5,000; the subshares held by Minor Companies are described
  • Provincial and Continental Gilds: the number of London companies compared to those of other English towns and Continental cities; London’s total at various periods
  • Expenditure data (1882): separate accounting for Trust estates (held in trust for specific charitable purposes) and Corporate estates (the company’s own funds); aggregate income figures for Great and Minor Companies from the 1882 Return ^[source: hazlitt_livery_companies_raw.txt]

Voluntary Associations which have Disappeared or Merged (lines ~5688–7957)

Short entries on gilds and fraternities that existed in medieval London but did not survive to 1892 as incorporated livery companies, or that were absorbed into larger bodies. These include: White and Brown Bakers, Bladesmiths (or Bladers), Block-makers, Braelers (or Brace-makers), Burillers, Cappers, Carmen, Cheesemongers, Comb-makers, Corders of the Ropery, Free Fishermen, Forcers (Casket-makers), Fullers, Furbishers, Gardeners, Haberdashers of St Katherine the Virgin, Hatband Makers, Hatters, Heaumers, Hostelers and Haymongers, Hurers (Milliners), Linen Drapers, Marblers, Parish Clerks, Paviours, Pepperers (absorbed by the Grocers), Pinners (Pin-makers), Planers, Potters, Pouch-makers, Pursers (Glovers’ Pursers), Shearmen (Pannarii, or Retunders), Sawyers, Sheathers, Shivers, Silkmen, Silk-Throwers (Throwsters), Soapers (Soap-makers), Spicers (absorbed by the Grocers), Spurriers, Starch-makers, Stock-fishmongers, Stringers, Surgeons (later incorporated; noted here in their early form), Tapissers (Tapestry-makers), Grey Tawyers (Tanners), White Tawyers, Tobacco-pipe Makers, Watermen, Woodmongers, Wool-packers, the Fellowship Porters. Also: the Alien Weavers and Flemish Weavers (foreign corporations operating in London). ^[source: hazlitt_livery_companies_raw.txt]

The Great Companies (lines ~8044–15500; pp. 169–283)

Twelve companies of highest precedence, ranked by arbitration in 1515. The order (Mercers, Grocers, Drapers, Fishmongers, Goldsmiths, then Skinners and Merchant Taylors alternating at six and seven, then Haberdashers, Salters, Ironmongers, Vintners, Clothworkers) was disputed between the Skinners and Merchant Taylors, who still alternate positions six and seven. Each receives a full chapter covering medieval origins, successive charters, notable benefactors, financial history, hall, and distinguished members.

The Mercers (lines ~8044–8242): The Mercers originated as a Fraternity dealing in fine fabrics and received their first charter from Richard II, becoming the pre-eminent civic company. Richard Whittington, dying in 1423–4, appointed the Mercers trustees of his almshouses; their Whittington trust generated £280 annually in 1424 and grew considerably thereafter. Dean Colet selected the Mercers to govern his new St Paul’s School (founded 1509), stating he found “less corruption in such a body of citizens (as the Mercers) than in any other order or degree of mankind.” The Great Fire of 1666 destroyed muniments but not the plate (recovered, weighing 200 lbs); the Company received renewed charters from Charles I, Charles II, James II, William and Mary, and George I. Irish Plantation investment began 1617 (5,000l.); by the 1890s corporate income exceeded £12,000 annually.

The Grocers (lines ~8667–8856): Descended from the amalgamation of the Pepperers of Sopers’ Lane and the Spicers of Cheap in 1345; they adopted the name “Grocer” (engrosser) around 1370. By 1373 they numbered 124 members and had established a Court of Assistants; civic Ordinances of 1386 granted Wardens a power of search. The primary charter (February 16, 1428–9, 7 Henry VI) established perpetual succession and the right to hold lands to 20 marks yearly; by 1447 their search rights were extended kingdom-wide, covering spices, drugs, medicines, oils, ointments, confectionery, and later tobacco. The Great Fire devastated their City holdings; recovery took over a century. Their Irish manor, acquired 1617–22, was sold in 1872 for £157,256; in 1675 they faced Quo Warranto threats but were restored to fuller privileges under William and Mary in 1690.

The Drapers (lines ~9176–9342): Edward III’s patent of 1364 recognised an ancient cloth trade and prescribed four Wardens. The first formal charter (November 30, 1453, 17 Henry VI) created “the Master, Wardens, Brethren, and Sisteren of the Gild or Fraternity of the blessed Mary the Virgin of the Mistery of the Drapers.” A unique 1479 patent protected them against the Shearmen’s charter. The charter of 1607 reorganised government as a Master, four Wardens, and twenty-four Assistants for life — an unusually oligarchical structure — and the 1619 charter confirmed vast property holdings enumerated in 31 separate parcels including messuages, gardens, wharves, and an annuity. The earliest recorded ordinances date to 1322; subsequent codes (1405, 1418) established apprenticeship conditions and ceremonial protocols.

The Fishmongers (lines ~9867–10025): The Fishmongers were unique among early Fraternities in that their charters lacked explicit religious or charitable motives, concentrating solely on commercial protection. The craft is traceable to 1154 (Henry II’s amercement for trading without licence), with traditional confirmations under Edward I, II, and III. Their patent of 1364–5 mandated that only enfranchised fishmongers could trade for fish to Gascony and that Gascons must sell in gross; it conferred search rights on four annually presented persons (required to be non-tavern-keepers). In 1298, the Company celebrated Edward I’s return from Scotland with a spectacular procession featuring four gilded sturgeons, four silver salmon, and forty-eight knights in pageantry. Salt fish and fresh fish occupied distinct quarters at Billingsgate and Fish Wharf before Henry VIII unified them; the Great Fire of 1666 destroyed nearly all muniments.

The Goldsmiths (lines ~10601–10799): Concentrated in Foster Lane from the 14th century, the Goldsmiths held peculiar importance through their monopoly on precious metals and their assay function. In 1278, a mass arrest occurred following currency manipulation discoveries — many Goldsmiths were condemned to drowning and hanging. The statute 28 Edward I vested assay rights in the Company and mandated the leopard’s head stamp on all gold and silver vessels. The earliest charter of 1327 elected a governing body, fixed all precious metal work to Cheapside or the King’s Exchange, and conferred a licence in mortmain (20l. yearly); the charter of 1393 (16 Richard II) confirmed perpetual succession and fixed Wardens at four. The charter of 20 Henry VII (1504–5) most significantly granted powers of fining, imprisoning, seizing, and condemning unlawful work — comprehensive authority maintained through subsequent sovereigns. Richard de Bettoyne, as Mayor (1327), appeared at Edward III’s coronation with 360 servants in uniform livery.

The Skinners (lines ~11354–11550): The Skinners received their first charter March 1, 1327–8 (1 Edward III), empowering overseers to search for deceitful furs. Two ancient Fraternities (Corpus Christi and the Virgin) predated incorporation; their ordinances of 39 Edward III mandated separation of old and new peltry, prohibited fraudulent stuffing of grey Flanders furs with chalk, and restricted members to Walbrook, Cornhill, and Budge Row. Henry VII’s charter (16 Henry VII, 1500–1) named the merged body “Corpus Christi of the Skinners”; Elizabeth’s charter (2 Elizabeth, 1560–1) regulated fur imports by calculations of “beasts, tiers, and bellies.” James I’s 1606 charter restricted coney-skin exportation and mandated that half of Assistants be professional skinners; its democratic clauses provoked Company resistance, and the Privy Council revoked the controversial provisions in February and March 1606–7. The Skinners’ notorious alternating precedence with the Merchant Taylors at positions six and seven was settled by Lord Mayor’s arbitration and continues to this day.

The Merchant Taylors (lines ~11901–12100): The Merchant Taylors began as the Gild of Pilgrims and Linen-Armourers; their books commence 1299–1300, though a licence from Edward I names them “Taylors and Linen Armourers,” indicating their dual function in both lining armour and making ordinary apparel. A legend survives of the devil visiting their haunts at Birchin Lane and being expelled by linen-armourers and their apprentices with Spanish needles. The first formal charter (March 1326–7, Edward III) limited the craft to freemen; Henry VII’s 1502 charter introduced the title “Merchant Taylors” and granted a monopoly on working and cutting men’s apparel within bounds. Their School — where Edmund Spenser was educated, as was Thomas Kyd; Richard Mulcaster served as headmaster — was a significant civic institution. The Company maintained boys’ performances at Court and was prominent in civic pageantry.

The Haberdashers: Two Brotherhoods, of St Katherine the Virgin and of St Nicholas, existed before formal incorporation; the Haberdashers of hats and small wares (Hurers/Cappers and Hatters) were combined into one society by 1448. In 1311, haberdashers and hatters were convicted of selling false hats made of wool and flocks, and the goods were burned in Cheapside. The earliest bye-laws (1371) prescribed seven-year apprenticeships; a 1378 inventory of a haberdasher’s goods reveals the breadth of the trade: laces, nightcaps, children’s caps, combs, inkhorns, gaming tables, paxbreads, quires of paper, pepper querns, purses, eyeglasses, and miscellaneous small wares. Subsidiary brotherhoods included the Hatband-Makers (incorporated 13 Charles I, December 12, 1638) and the Hatters. The Company was prominent in the Ulster Plantation, subscribing significant sums; the Skinners sublet their Irish estate shares to the Haberdashers among others.

The Salters (lines ~13203–13310): The Salters received their first charter under Richard II in 1394, assembled in Bread Street. Their Hall history is one of successive disasters: a fire in 1539 required rebuilding; Ambrose Nicholas’s will described the “common Hall of the said Wardens and Commonalty, called Salters’ Hall in Bread Street,” which was totally destroyed by fire in 1598. The Company relocated in 1641 to Oxford House/London Stone (formerly a residence of Sir Richard Empson and Edmund Dudley); that Hall perished in 1665, replaced by a small brick structure in 1673, and a final Hall completed 1821–27. Almshouses were relocated to Watford in 1863. A Literary Notice includes George Peele’s Descensus Astraæ (1591), a civic pageant device for Lord Mayor William Web featuring a sea nymph presentation and a pinnace on the water.

The Ironmongers (lines ~13325–13507): The iron trade, initially supplied from the Forest of Dean and later from the Wealds of Sussex, Surrey, and Kent, had sophisticated organisation by 1300: three Wardens (one of London Bridge supervising unlading, two of the Market) and a standard rod sealed by the Chamber to verify all iron-work for cart-wheels. Edward IV granted their parent charter in 1464, naming Richard Flemming as first Master and Nicholas Maxhall and Robert Toke as first Wardens, with perpetual succession, a licence in mortmain (10 marks yearly), and a common seal. The Articles of 1455 (23 Henry VI) established thirteen persons plus Wardens as a quorum; yeomanry paid quarterly; Wardens conducted annual searches of weights and measures. Unusually, the Ironmongers never possessed the right of search and required the Court of Aldermen to initiate action against trade abuses. In 1882, the Livery numbered 52 and Freemen 156.

The Vintners (lines ~14021–14199): The Vintners’ origin is ancient and obscure; four earlier societies (New Meyne, the King’s, the Shipup, and another) sought charters opposed by Vintners but eventually merged. Their letters patent of July 15, 1364 (37 Edward III) forbade any but enfranchised Vintners to trade for wine to Gascony, mandated that Gascons sell in gross, and conferred search rights on four annually presented non-tavern-keeper persons. The Assize of 1311 (4 Edward II) fixed selling prices at 5d., 4d., and 3d. per gallon by quality. A 1364 exemplar punished a vintner selling unwholesome wine by forcing him to drink it and pouring the rest over him, followed by disfranchisement. Stow described the Vintry as the Thames bank where Bordeaux merchants craned wines from lighters and sold within forty days; distinctive measures — gallon, pottle, quart, pint; pipes, tuns, and hogsheads — were strictly regulated.

The Clothworkers (lines ~14823–14963): The Clothworkers emerged from the union of Shearmen and Fullers under a grant giving the Executive a common right of search over all cloth and fustian work; this was confirmed by inspeximus of 4 & 5 Philip and Mary (June 4, 1558) and 2 Elizabeth (July 8, 1560). Charles I’s charter (April 24, 1633) reconstituted the Company as “Master, Wardens, and Commonalty of Freemen of the Art or Mistery of Clothworkers.” The earliest Ordinances (1531–2) were approved by Sir Thomas More as Lord Chancellor and renewed in 29 Elizabeth (1587) and 15 Charles I (1639); they regulated officers, livery, poor relief, apprentices, journeymen, and foreigners. Sisters were admitted to freedom; children of freemen succeeded by inheritance. The Company contributed 5,000l. to the Ulster Plantation; their Irish estate was ultimately sold for £150,000. The Court in the 1890s comprised a Master, Wardens, and 44 Assistants, with aggregate Company numbers around 500.

The Minor Companies (lines ~15500–31305; pp. 285–670)

Alphabetical chapters on all incorporated Minor Companies surviving in 1892. Each chapter follows the same structural format as the Great Companies, scaled to available historical documentation.

The Apothecaries (lines ~15489–15637): The Apothecaries separated from the Grocers by charter in 1617; King James I personally supported the separation, stating “I myself did devise that Corporation, and do allow it.” Governance by a Master, two Wardens, and twenty-one Assistants; the Company — along with the Stationers and Brewers — admitted only members of the vocation, making it a true Craft Gild. The Hall, acquired under the 1633 charter as Cobham House (formerly Lady Howard of Effingham’s property, extending to the Thames on Water Lane), perished in the Great Fire; the 1695 successor measured 59 by 28 by 26 feet with an Irish oak screen and orchestra surmounted by royal arms. The Physic Garden was leased for sixty-one years from 1673 (three acres, one rood, thirty-five perches) and given in perpetuity by Sir Hans Sloane in 1731 at 5l. quit-rent, on condition of presenting 2,000 well-cured plant specimens to the Royal Society annually. Notable members: Tobias Smollett, William and John Hunter, Edward Jenner, Sir Humphrey Davy, Dr Sydenham, Oliver Goldsmith, George Crabbe, and John Keats.

The Armourers and Braziers (lines ~15732–15855): Two initially separate crafts — Armourers (Ordinances from 1322, first charter as Fraternity of St George 1453) and Braziers (eight men in the 1469 watch muster versus Armourers’ thirty-four) — were formally united by Queen Anne’s charter in 1708 as “Company of Armourers and Braziers.” James II’s 1685 patent had extended search and presentment powers over edge tools, armour, and all copper/brass work within the City and five miles; the 1708 Queen Anne charter remains the governing instrument. The Hall at London Wall, adjoining the Leathersellers’ Hall, dates to the early 15th century; the Founders hired it for two days in 1497. In 1882, pictures, plate, furniture, and armour were valued at nearly £12,000; the Company subscribed 78l. 15s. toward technical education.

The Bakers (lines ~15858–15986): The United Gild of White and Brown Bakers was chartered by Henry VIII (22 July 1509) as “one Master and four Keepers of the commonalty of freemen of the Mistery of Bakers.” A second charter (26 May, 11 Elizabeth) granted a mortmain licence (40l. yearly) and power of search. The White and Brown Bakers remained formally separate through 1629 (assessed differently for City levies and Ulster contributions — 480l. and 90l. respectively), before a single charter under 2 James II comprehended both with one Master, four Wardens, and thirty Assistants. Since the Act 3 George IV (cap. 16) altered bread supply law, the Bakers ceased active trade connection; women are now admissible to freedom. The Ulster Plantation sub-share was sold to the Skinners in 1877 for £40,000. In 1882, approximately 250 freemen and 216 liverymen.

The Barbers (lines ~15989–16156): The Barbers’ earliest vestige predates their 1461 incorporation; in 1308, they presented Nicholas Le Barbour as Supervisor. Uniquely, a 1388 return to Richard II’s gild inquiry survives (transferred from the Tower in 1634, copied to vellum in 1648 — preserved through this procedural accident). The letters patent of 1 Edward IV (February 24, 1461–2) created the first formal incorporation as “one body and one perpetual community” with search rights over freemen barbers and foreigners. In 32 Henry VIII, Parliament united Barbers and Surgeons under one government as “Masters or Governors of the Mistery or Commonalty of Barbers and Surgeons,” with the Act prohibiting barbers from practising surgery (except tooth-drawing) and surgeons from barbery. The 1469 watch muster assessed the Company at 120 men; they held exemption from jury and assize service since 1357.

The Basket-makers (lines ~16258–16322): The Basket-makers claimed existence by prescription; no charter survived (papers deposited at Guildhall were destroyed in the Great Fire). An Edward IV record restricted them to the Manor of Blanche Appleton, Fenchurch Street. The 1569 Court of Aldermen settlement established them as an ordinary Association, confirmed January 10, 1585–6 and March 27, 1610, with authority to elect two Wardens for two-year terms sworn before the Mayor. The Company appeared among fifty-five companies assessed for corn and money in 1602–3 and subscribed to the Ulster project in 1615 (a small Fishmongers’ Manor sub-share). Livery was limited to thirty; women were admitted. In 1892, income was 102l.; they had no Hall of their own, occasionally using the Parish Clerks’ Hall in Silver Street.

The Blacksmiths (lines ~16331–16473): The Blacksmiths represent a craft formerly of much wider consequence, with medieval practitioners also serving in horology — clock-making eventually separating into its own Gild. Bye-laws of 1372 directed all work to Gracechurch Street or Tun on Cornhill; articles of 1394 fixed labour hours and prohibited copying locks without original keys. No charter is likely prior to Elizabeth’s (April 20, 1571), which united them with the Spurriers; renewed by Charles I (February 15, 1639–40) and extended to a four-mile radius. Their dispute with the nascent Clockmakers Company (1627–38) was acrimonious — when the Clockmakers were incorporated in 1631, the Blacksmiths used every effort to hamper them through protests and legal proceedings. Hall on Lambert Hill near St Mary Mounthaut; in 1882, Livery numbered eighty-one, Freemen eighty-three.

The Bowyers (lines ~16496–16574): Incorporated May 25, 1621, and confirmed November 17, 1668, the Bowyers were constituted to revive the long bow — the craft existed as a “remote Fraternity but then in decay.” Governance comprised one Master, two Wardens, and thirteen Assistants. James Wood’s will (August 1, 1625) bequeathed the manor of Isley Walton, Leicestershire, to fund Oxford and Cambridge exhibitions with preference for Bowyer freemen, plus annual charitable distributions in kind (30s. or three yards broadcloth) to six poor brethren. The Company now supports seven exhibitions of 5l. at each University and a school at their expense. Former Hall in Noble Street; total income approximately 600l. with very little remaining after charges.

The Brewers (lines ~16576–16955): The Brewers trace a continuous site at Addle Street (Wood Street turning, Cheapside) from at least 1420, when the governing body assembled there every Monday. Henry IV’s ordinance (7 Henry IV) bound freemen to elect eight inspectors (four east, four west of Walbrook). The Company nearly uniquely admitted none but trade members or brewing partners; the Executive comprised a Master and three Wardens (Upper, Middle, Renter) and twenty-six Assistants. In 1627, a petition objected to alum manufacturing near St Mary Matfellon (Whitechapel) whose urine boiling polluted wells used for brewing; the Lords of Council ordered removal. The Great Fire destroyed the Hall almost completely; it was rebuilt in 1673 partly by subscription and partly by pawning plate (never redeemed). The kitchen, the oldest surviving portion, preserves original fireplace spits; a 1671 leaden cistern bearing the Brewers’ arms was preserved. Notable benefactors: Alderman Richard Platt (1528–1609), Dame Alice Owen (1547–1613), and Samuel Whitbread (1720–96). Corporate revenue reached 17,500l. in 1892; Livery numbered 75 in 1882.

The Broderers (lines ~17213–17332): The Broderers existed at least three centuries before their incorporation in 1561, representing the original Telarii (Tapestry-Weavers) commemorated by Chaucer. The 3 Elizabeth charter (October 25, 1561) established them with perpetual succession, a licence in mortmain (30l. yearly), and search rights within London, Westminster, St Katherine’s, and Southwark. A 1634 petition to Charles I stated the trade was “so much decayed and grown out of use” that many Company members had become porters and water-bearers; the mortmain licence was augmented to 100l. The Ulster Plantation subscription was 150–200l. (a Mercers’ Manor sub-share). Thomas Carleton, Broderer (d. 1382), bequeathed to the Merchant Taylors’ Chapel; Thomas Foster/Forster, Citizen and Broderer (will 19 Henry VIII), conveyed Gutter Lane property and likely gave his name to Foster Lane. In 1882, the Livery numbered 31; they retained their small Gutter Lane Hall from early Henry VIII.

The Butchers (lines ~17547–17665): The Butchers had two ancient principal City markets: East-Cheap and St Nicholas Shambles (Newgate Street). The 1277 ordinance restricted them to appointed localities; a 1369 royal order called for discontinuation of slaughtering at St Nicholas Shambles near Baynard’s Castle, as offal thrown into the river at Butchers’ Bridge provoked Crown pleas from prelates. A 1371 order directed large animal slaughtering at Stratford or Knightsbridge; a 4 Henry VII Act forbade slaughtering within the City, yet the nuisance persisted under Elizabeth. Nathaniel Lee the dramatist drank his last at the Bear and Harrow in Butchers’ Row, St Clement’s. A 1414–15 indenture delimited the East-Cheap stall area from the Pye on the Hoop to the Saracen’s Head, east to west from Crooked Lane to William Ivor’s tenement. In 1343, Sea-Coal Lane was granted to St Nicholas Shambles butchers on condition of sending the Lord Mayor a boar’s head annually at Christmas.

The Carpenters (lines ~17892–18009): Medieval master carpenters were estimating contractors furnishing complete building works, from cathedral erection to cottages; by 1271, London had two Master Carpenters surveying buildings, likely members of the “great fraternity” described by Chaucer (c. 1388). Patent office of Master Carpenter to the Crown dates to 1484 (Edward Graveley, 1s./day); a continuous series followed into the Surveyorship of Works. Humphrey Coke, Master Carpenter at Savoy Hospital, served as Warden 1507–8 and 1511, commemorated by Walpole in Anecdotes of Painting. A surviving 1472 Bristol indenture details a domestic building contract: shop, hall, chamber, and upper chamber (19 ft. 5 in. × 10 ft. 4 in.) for 6l. 18s. 4d. The Company formed a distinct Association probably from mid-14th century, ranked thirty-ninth in the 1418–1440 City crafts catalogue between Masons and Pewterers.

The Clockmakers (lines ~18332–18450): The Clockmakers were constituted in 1631, arising directly from tensions within the Blacksmiths’ Company. Foreign competition in the English clock trade (1627) prompted anxiety; alien manufacturers were protected by privy-seal warrant, aggrieving freemen Blacksmiths who practised clock-making. A 1628 proposal for union with the Blacksmiths was declined. The petition for an independent charter was agreed at trade meetings 1629–30, with 72l. guaranteed by sixteen persons (Roger Johnson contributing most, at 10l.); Commissioners referred the matter to the Court of Aldermen with a favourable report. The charter of August 22, 1631 founded “The Master, Wardens, and Fellowship of the Art or Mistery of Clockmaking of the City of London” — unusually comprehensive, admitting all clockmakers whether freemen, foreigners, or other persons. Tensions with the Blacksmiths continued through protests and legal proceedings into the late 1630s.

The Cooks (lines ~18855–18943): The Cooks were incorporated by 27 Edward IV patent as “one body and one commonalty perpetual” with two Masters/Governors, two Wardens, and Assistants, with search jurisdiction over London, Westminster, suburbs, and a four-mile radius. A 1614 new charter was immediately challenged by the Court of Aldermen as “secretly and surreptitiously” obtained; Lord Chief Justice Coke reported it against common law and City charters, and it stood in abeyance until lapsing. The Hall in Aldersgate Street (escaping the Great Fire) was used by Saddlers, Stationers, and Painter-Stainers during their rebuilding 1668–70; it is now commercial premises (nos. 10–12 Aldersgate Street, let 1875 on eighty-year lease at 800l./year). A 1567 City lottery entry preserves the Cooks’ motto: “We Cooks of London, which work early and late, If anything be left, God send us part.” In 1882, 249 freemen (98 Livery).

The Coopers (lines ~18945–19060): The Coopers are traceable to Edward II (beyond formal documentation), with the earliest specific trace a 1310 debt seizure (Robert le Cuver and Alice le Cuver, two great cuves valued 19s.). Their early history reveals chronic fraud in barrel manufacture: a 1396 Court of Aldermen agreement sought to suppress oil and soap barrels being sold to brewers for ale storage; an Ordinance renewal of 1409 noted that provisions against unsound wood were habitually ignored. In 1413, a fishmonger was discovered with 260 vessels of impure, middle-sawn wood of untrue measure — burned at Guildhall. A marking system from 9 Henry IV (1407–8) obliged all Coopers to bring goods to the Chamber of London before output, with duplicate marks entered on record. The Coopers’ reform was closely associated with Sir Richard Whittington’s representations.

The Cordwainers (lines ~19616–19734): The earliest Cordwainer Ordinances (1271–2, 56 Henry III) addressed spurious material, apprenticeship, and stranger admission — notably lacking any reference to a governing body, requiring the Corporation’s direct involvement. Bye-laws of 1375 addressed the fraud of selling shoes of bazen as cordwaine or Cordovan leather, and limited sales to Soper Lane. In 1439 (17 Henry VI), for 50 marks, the King conferred a charter on “freemen of the Mistery of Cordwainers” as one body with perpetual succession, one Master and four Wardens, and search rights over tanned leather within the City and a two-mile radius of Cornhill. The Cordwainers and Cobblers were perpetually litigious — distinguished as new-leather and old-leather workers respectively — meriting the sobriquet “the Gentle Craft.”

The Curriers (lines ~20002–20120): The Curriers’ corporate evolution can be traced in successive stages: Wardens from a 1415 petition to the Court of Aldermen; a Hall site (1516, Thomas Sterne’s bequest of the Boar’s Head messuage with St Alphage tenements, on condition the site serve as a common Hall in perpetuity); Assistants (1581); a grant of arms (1583); and a full Livery, Clerk, and Beadle (1587 Elizabethan Ordinances) — yet no charter was found until James I accorded one on April 30, 1606, the first and only known. A 1567 Crown licence enabled Wardens and freemen to buy and sell leather at all markets within forty miles of London. The 1597 constitutional controversy over Warden election was settled by the Lord Mayor: outgoing Wardens should elect two successors — one from Assistants, one not — with the Fellowship nominating the junior. The Hall perished in 1666.

The Cutlers (lines ~20211–20384): Overseers of the Cutlery trade were appointed by the Court of Aldermen in 1344, with bye-laws prescribing seven-year apprenticeships, day-only manufacture, and Mayor court appeal on contested Warden judgements. Regulations of 1380 prohibited silver-handled knives unless sterling and required natural-coloured wooden handles. An 1408 dispute with the Sheathers established that knives underwent manipulation by four separate trades before sale: blade-smith, cutler, sheather, and furbisher. Concurrently, a complaint addressed foreigners selling Cutler articles bearing forged English bladesmith marks. Stated charters from Henry V (1415) through Anne (1703) reduce substantially to Henry V and James I, since others were inspeximus confirmations; the governing instrument is James I’s 1607 charter. The Hall was acquired in Cloak Lane in 1451.

The Distillers: Incorporated August 9, 1638 under Charles I, founded under the patronage of Theodore de Mayerne (physician to the King) and Dr Thomas Cademan, who became first Master; de Mayerne is listed as Founder with nineteen initial Assistants. Despite repeated royal commands, the Company was not enrolled in the Chamber of London until March 17, 1658. A new charter was granted May 12, 1687; confirmed with Rules in 1690; Livery granted October 21, 1672. The Company never erected a Hall and owned only a freehold site originally purchased for that purpose. Literary notices on distillation span Jerome of Brunswick (1527), Gesner’s Treasure of Evonymous (1559, 1565), The Distiller of London (1639, 1668), and French’s Art of Distillation (1651–1667).

The Dyers: The Dyers ranked first among the Minor Companies. They acquired a charter from Henry VI (1471) and subsequently from James I (June 30, 1607), with earlier Elizabeth bye-laws (1587). John Croson was first Master under the 1663 charter; governance comprised a Prime Warden, Renter Warden, and Assistants. Their Hall at Upper Thames Street near Ebgate/Old Swan Lane was lost in the Great Fire; a second Hall was built on Dowgate Hill. The Dyers shared swan-upping duties on the Thames with the Vintners, employing a Bargemaster and four swan-herd assistants — a practice continuing to the present day. Livery: 19 (1501–2), 200 (1699), 66 (1892).

The Fanmakers: The youngest of all London companies, chartered under Queen Anne (April 19, 1709). Governance comprised a Master, two Wardens, and seventeen Assistants; Livery was limited to 200 (only 120 in 1882). Peter Motteux — translator of Don Quixote — kept an emporium in Leadenhall Street at the sign of Two Fans. The Company was founded at a juncture when repeal of the duty on foreign imports proved fatal to its commercial basis; an Exhibition held in 1878 cost 750l. Lady Charlotte Schreiber was admitted as a member. The Fanmakers’ Grievances broadside survives as a literary notice concerning East Indies imports.

The Farriers: The earliest reference is Walter le Brun (1272), granted a forge-site at St Clement’s Parish in exchange for six horseshoes annually. Civic regulations in 1350 fixed prices: 1d. for a six-nail shoe, 2d. for an eight-nail, 3d. for a charger; Ordinances of 1356 required two Masters to supervise all forges. The formal charter was granted January 17, 1684–5, establishing a Master, three Wardens, and nineteen Assistants. The Company offered three competitive prizes for farriery advancement, including freedom, a gold medal, and 20l. for an essay on the treatment of horses. Literary notices: Gervase Markham’s Master-peece (1615, 1636) and Complete Farriar (1639, 1670); Andrew Snape Junior’s Anatomy of an Horse (1683). Livery: 88 (1882), 96 (1892).

The Feltmakers: The Feltmakers sought a charter as early as 1576, opposed by their parent company the Haberdashers. They were finally incorporated in 1604 under James I as “Master and Wardens and Commonalty of the Art or Mistery of Feltmakers,” regulating felt hat manufacture. Occurring as Feltmongers in 1501–2 (forty-seventh of seventy-four bodies) with a Livery of two members. New charters followed from Charles II (1667, 1670) and George III (1772). Livery was limited to 120; notable later members included the late Earl of Iddesleigh and W. H. Smith MP. Livery: 53 (1882), 65 (1892).

The Fletchers: The Fletchers existed by prescription only — no charter or Act of Parliament survived. Mentioned in a 1371 treaty with the Bowyers regarding trade distinctions; Ordinances of 1403 appointed Wardens with the right of search over arrows, arrowheads, and quarels. A coat of arms was granted under 7 Edward IV, with a crest in augmentation under Henry VII. Earliest extant Company records date from 1775. Governance comprised Upper and Renter Warden and ten Assistants; their Hall was in St Mary Axe. Property income from the ground-rent of the old Hall site yielded about 149l. annually. Livery: 25 (1739, paying 10l. fine).

The Founders (Coppersmiths): The earliest mention of “Good Men of the Mistery of the Founders” is 1365, when they petitioned for ordinances to regulate candlesticks, buckles, spurs, stirrups, lavers, pots, ewers, and basins; Ordinances of 1389 dealt with light solder abuse. The Company “began with twenty-four poor honest men” in 1472. The charter of September 18, 1614, cost 115l. 14s., itemised down to Sir Francis Bacon’s clerk’s fee (11s.). Their Hall site between St Margaret’s Church and Coleman Street was purchased in 1534; a new Founders’ Hall was erected in 1877 via an 8,000l. loan. Ben Jonson’s The Alchemist depicts Lothbury founders as background colour. Livery: 90 (1699), 136 (1724), 100 (1892).

The Framework Knitters: William Lee (St John’s College, Cambridge) invented the stocking-frame c.1599; the Company was chartered under Cromwell (June 13, 1657, enrolled July 14) and given a new charter by Charles II (August 19, 1663). Governance: Master, two Wardens, and fifteen-plus Assistants; John Croson served as first Master. Livery was granted June 9, 1713; bye-laws were approved in 1745 (Parliament ruled in 1753 that Ordinances governed only Gild members). The Hall on Red Cross Street was purchased in 1678 and completed in 1720; the Master’s chair was a gift in 1681. A surplus of 500l. was recorded in 1880.

The Fruiterers: Not listed in 1501–2 (though already existing), ranked last (forty-eighth) in 1515. Incorporated under James I (1603) as “Master, Wardens, and Commonalty of the Mistery of Fruiterers of London,” with a Master, two Wardens, and five-to-twenty Assistants; election day January 25. An ancient toll was commuted into an annual Lord Mayor fruit presentation on October 7. The Ulster Plantation subscription (1613) was a Vintners’ Bellaghy Manor sub-share. Literary notices: N.F.’s Fruiterers Secrets (1604, 1609), Ralph Austen’s Treatise (1653), and Fruit-Walls Improved (1699). Livery: 37 (1699), 99 (1882), 101 (1892).

The Girdlers: Ordinances regulating tissue size, metal garnishing, and apprenticeship were framed in 1344; Edward III confirmed ancient regulations in 1327 and Edward IV in 1377. The Brotherhood of St Laurence was incorporated in 1448 (27 Henry VI) as “Master and Wardens of Mistery of Girdlers”; Elizabeth’s 1570 charter united the Girdlers, Wireworkers, and Pinners into one body. The Hunt estate (1431–2) gave the Hall site in Old Jewry; the Flycke estate (Hammersmith, 1579–82) added income. The Hall was rebuilt 1877–80 at a cost of 10,000l. Governance: Master, two Wardens, seventeen Assistants. Thomas Sutton, founder of Charterhouse, was an eminent member. Livery: 85 (1699 and 1724), 91 (1881).

The Glaziers: An Act of James I (1616) required all glaziers to be subject to Masters’ control; the first manual of glazing was Walter Gedde’s work of 1615. The charter under 13 Charles I established “Wardens and Commonalty of the Mistery or Art of Glaziers and Painters of glass.” The Wardens were committed to Newgate in 1627–8 for loan default. James II’s charter is the governing instrument. The Company’s estate derived from a Thames Street/Queen Street property (purchased 1671 for 490l., producing 3,700l. in stock). Bye-laws of 1749 (thirty-two articles) were agreed at the Loriners’ Hall. Literary notices: Walter Gedde’s Booke (1615), Antonio Neri’s Art of Glass (1662), Robert Boyle’s Essays (1673). Livery: 76 (1699), 100 (1724), 32 (1882).

The Glovers: Ordinances of 1349 detailed terms of the craft; a 1350 case saw seventeen pairs of “vamped false” gloves burned at Stone Cross. A coat of arms was granted in 1464. In 1502 the Glovers were incorporated with the Leathersellers on June 7, but recovered independence by Charles I’s charter of September 5, 1638 as “Master, Wardens, and Fellowship of the Company of Glovers.” Governance: Master, four Wardens, thirty Assistants; Hall in Beech Lane from 1739. James Peele, author of the first English book-keeping manual (1553) and writer of City pageants, is noted — possibly related to George Peele the dramatist. Livery: 120 (1699), 90 (1892).

The Gunmakers: Henry Rowland, gunmaker, and sixty-two others were chartered on March 14, 1637–8 as “Master, Wardens, and Society of the Mistery of Gunmakers”; Henry Rowland was first Master. Jurisdiction extended over London plus ten miles; gunpowder proof of every gun was specified. Livery was granted in 1670. The Proof House at Whitechapel remains their only owned premises. The Gun Barrel Proof Act of 1868 recognised the charter, and the Company is unique among Livery Gilds in maintaining independent regulatory status through modern legislation. Estate accumulated approximately 30,000l. from proof business profits. Livery: 32 (1882), Freemen 58.

The Horners (and Bottlers): Horn Fair at Charlton, granted by Henry III in 1268, was held in Trinity Week and later on St Luke’s Day (October). The 50 Edward III listing placed Horners among forty-eight misteries with two Common Council members; a 4 Edward IV statute protected them from foreign competition and regulated horn export. The charter of January 12, 1638 (13 Charles I) incorporated them as “Master, Wardens, Assistants, and Fellowship of the Mistery of Horners.” Warehouse and sheds on Wentworth Street, Whitechapel, were leased in 1604 and purchased on a 1,000-year term at 4l. annually; demolished in 1881, they yielded 72l. annual Consols income. An Exhibition held at Mansion House in 1882 attracted 7,000 visitors at a cost of 200l. Bottlers — makers of leather bottles — were earlier merged into the Company. Livery: 17 (1892), Membership: 13 (1882, including only three freemen).

The Innholders: Ordinances were approved July 31, 1483; the Company was incorporated December 21, 1515 (6 Henry VIII), ranked between Plumbers and Founders among the forty-eight misteries. The 1663 charter (15 Charles II) dissolved the older body and reconstituted it as “Master, Wardens, and Society of Art and Mistery of Innholders,” with three Wardens and twenty Assistants. A Hall property on Dowgate Hill was acquired in 1613; it was destroyed in 1663 and rebuilt, and a third Hall erected by 3,000–4,000l. in subscriptions. Literary notices: Articles of Direction (1607–09), Ale-house Licence forms (James I), John Taylor’s Circular Perambulation (1636). Livery: 16 (1501), 150 (1724), 122 (1892); aggregate revenue 1892: 1,385l.

The Joiners: Ordinances of 1309 set six Wardens/Overseers to examine and stamp goods; two Common Council members in 1376 and thirty men-at-arms in 1469. The charter of 13 Elizabeth (April 14, 1570) incorporated them as “Master, Wardens, and Commonalty of Faculty of Joiners and Ceilers or Carvers.” A controversy 1613–21 over whether executives must be practising craftsmen was resolved by arbitration. Stephen College, “the Protestant Joiner,” wainscoted Stationers’ Hall in 1674 for 300l. and was hanged at Oxford in 1681 for high treason — Hazlitt notes him as Grinling Gibbons’ predecessor. The Thames Street/Friar Lane Hall was demolished in 1850. Livery: 190 (1699), 286 (1724), 90 (1882).

The Leathersellers: The earliest document is a 1372 petition for leather regulation; four searchers were chosen. The Glovers and Pursers merged with the Leathersellers in 1502, the Pouchmakers in 1517; incorporated trades by 1635 included Fellmongers, Leatherdressers, Parchment-makers, Glovers, Pursers, Pouchmakers, Leather-dyers. The charter of August 19, 1444 (22 Henry VI) granted perpetual succession, common seal, and mortmain licence; the 1604 charter (James I) reconstituted with four Wardens, a Master, and twenty-four Assistants. The contentious monopoly patent of Edward Darcie (1591–95) cost the Company 4,000l. to revoke. The Company moved to St Helen’s Bishopsgate in 1543, purchasing the former Nuns’ Hall of the priory (founded 1210, by goldsmith William Fitzwilliam) from Sir Richard Williams/Cromwell for 380l.; the current Hall dates from 1878. Roger Daniel, King’s Printer, served as Master and left the Bishopsgate estate. Livery: 33 (1501–2), 169 (1699), 141 (1892); Trust income 1882: 2,333l.

The Loriners: Ordinances of 45 Henry III (1260) appointed four Wardens with eight articles: no night work or Saturday afternoon, no repair of old lorinery, foreigners to pay fees. They appeared as copper and iron workers in 1327; in 1469 they furnished fourteen men-at-arms. Despite this antiquity, their only charter dates from 1711: Master, two Wardens, and twenty-one Assistants, with an unusually large mortmain licence (500l. yearly). Freemen: approximately 1,391 (1882); Livery: 438. The Hall at London Wall opposite Basinghall Street was described as “small, convenient” in 1739; from 1767 the Company engaged Founders’ Hall in Lothbury at 10l. yearly. Literary notice: Blundevile’s Four Chiefyst Offices (1566, 1580, 1597, 1609).

The Masons: The Act of 5 Elizabeth (1562–3) first specified the trade distinctly. Ordinances of 1356 described two classes: hewers (rough work) and light masons/setters (finer work). William de Rameseye (master-mason at St Paul’s, 1332) was exempted from jury service. Arms were granted by Clarencieux in 1473, rediscovered in 1871 (purchased for 7l. 7s. and presented to the British Museum). Despite this long history, the first known charter dates only from September 17, 1677 (29 Charles II), granting unlimited mortmain and search/control within seven miles. Bye-laws followed in 1678. Governance: Master, Warden, Renter Warden, and seven Assistants. The Hall was in Masons’ Alley, Basinghall Street (now Masons’ Hall Tavern). The designation “Free Mason” appears in documents of 1655–56, changed to “Mason” thereafter. Livery: 11 (1501–2), 63 (1724), 45 (1892).

The Musicians (Minstrels): The original charter of April 24, 1472–3 does not survive; James I’s charter of July 8, 1604 re-established them as “Master, Wardens, and Commonalty of Art or Science of Musicians of London” with a Master, two Wardens, and fourteen Assistants. Bye-laws approved in 1606 included jurisdiction over dancing and prohibited playing under nobles’ windows without Court leave. The Company contributed to the Ulster Plantation alongside Bowyers, Fletchers, and Woolmen (20l. each). Property included Clapton freeholds and Government Consols; income: 336l. (1880), 400l. (1892). Livery: unlimited; 17 (1699), 50 (1892). Literary notices: instruction books from 1558, John Case’s Praise of Musicke (1586), Playford’s Brief Introduction (1654, often reprinted), Simpson’s Division-Violist (1659).

The Needlemakers: Hazlitt includes a Needlemakers chapter in the Minor Companies section; their trade had connections to the pin-making and wire-working industries within the Girdlers’ orbit. Detailed content for this chapter was not extracted in the present review.

The Painters (Painter-Stainers): Nicholas Bacon, painter, purchased sinople, vermilion, varnish, and verdigris (20s.) in 1284. Ordinances of 1467 established two Wardens, six Assistants, and a beadle, with quarterly dues (16d.) and Livery rules; clauses prohibited tinfoil work without oil colours and sign-painting with fine gold. The Stainers were independent in 1469 (fourteen men-at-arms); “Painter-Stainers” first appears as a combined title in 1501–2 (Livery 18). The charter of July 19, 1581 (13 Elizabeth) designated “Master and Wardens and Commonalty of Freemen of Mistery or Art of Painters, commonly called Painters Stainers,” with thirty-seven articles of bye-laws. Richard III had prohibited imported beaten gold and silver papers for painters. The Hall was a gift in 1531. Livery: ranked twenty-ninth (1515), thirty-ninth (1531–2 with five representatives, two messes).

The Pewterers: Earliest bye-laws from 1348 specified alloy proportions for pots, saltcellars, esquelles, and platters; by 1350 the ratio was fixed at 16 lbs. lead to 112 lbs. tin. The charter of January 20, 1473–4 (Edward IV), renewed 1478, granted inquisition rights throughout the realm; subsequent confirmations by Henry VII (1505), Henry VIII (1513), Philip and Mary (1555), Elizabeth (1559), James I, Charles I (1639), Charles II (1674), and Anne (formal bye-laws right 1704, last bye-laws confirmed 1766). The Fraternity was founded in honour of the Virgin Mary and included brothers and sisters plus voluntary members. Governance: Master, two Wardens, Commonalty. Lydgate’s London Lickpenny (c. 1425) mentions pewter pots clattering in East Cheapside.

The Plumbers: Ordinances of 1365 governed gutters, conduit-pipes, tap-troughs, furnaces, and belfries; a 1371 furnace dispute at East Cheap established forty-year custom requiring a shaft higher than neighbouring roofs. Lead was used for roofs and cisterns as tiles replaced thatch. In 1501–2 the Company ranked thirty-second with a Livery of twelve. Their only charter was granted in 1611: “Master, Wardens, and Commonalty of Freemen of Mistery of Plumbing,” with common seal, mortmain licence, and a council-house requirement. John Haselwood’s 1543 bequest included a lead sow of 11 cwt. three quarters. Post-Reformation ecclesiastical foundations yielded quantities of lead as abbeys were dissolved.

The Poulters: Claiming a voluntary Association from 1345, the Poulters were incorporated by Henry VII (19 Henry VII, February 23, 1504), with renewals under Elizabeth, Charles II (June 13, 1663), and William and Mary (May 6, 1692). In 1357 they were forbidden Leadenhall with rabbits and fowls; in 1375, foreign poulterers were directed to sell before St Nicholas Flesh-Shambles. A 1381 case saw William Fot pilloried for exposing eighteen putrid pigeons (four Bread Street cooks testified). A printed tariff dates from 1572. Income approximately 600l. yearly. Thomas Nepton’s will (May 6, 1718) and Robert Warden’s trust (June 3, 1609) established charitable distributions to the poor of Barking, St Botolph Aldgate, and Shoreditch School. Governance: Master, two Wardens (Upper, Renter), Assistants; election on Ash Wednesday. Livery: 127 (1724), 40 (1892).

The Saddlers: The Saddlers claim a 1154 document testifying to a compact with the monks of St Martin le Grand, providing two masses weekly for living and departed members in exchange for church privileges and funeral rights (8d. for reception in churchyard and bell ringing). Home was originally under the east boundary of St Martin le Grand; the old Hall was lost in the Great Fire; the modern Hall is on Foster Lane. By 1272, Edward III granted powers of search over saddle-girdles throughout the realm; a 1356 dispute erupted when the Girdlers obtained search power over leather saddle-girths (suspended until Parliament). St Martin le Grand disappeared at the Reformation, replaced by the General Post Office.

The Shipwrights: The Brotherhood of SS. Simon and Jude, with Ordinances of 1428, 1456, and 1483, preceded formal incorporation. The charter of April 23, 1605 (3 James I) established “Master, Wardens, and Commonalty of the Art or Mistery of Shipwrights, London” with a Master, two Wardens, and sixteen Assistants. The Company relocated from the Surrey side of the Thames to Ratcliffe, establishing their Hall near Ratcliff Cross. A dispute with foreign shipwrights lasted 1613–83, draining Company funds despite favourable decisions from the Court of Aldermen and the Admiralty. George Whitehead, the celebrated Quaker, is noted as a member; Thomas Ward, Citizen and Shipwright, served as Sheriff in 1829. Livery: 245 (1892).

The Spectacle-makers: Chartered in 1629 with a Master, two Wardens, and eight Assistants. By Common Council Act of July 1, 1658, all trade practitioners were required to take up freedom. Livery was granted in 1809, limited to 400. Spectacle-makers are documented as early as 1563 in Newbery’s Dives Pragmaticus; magnifying lenses were introduced as documented in A New Invention; Or A Paire of Cristall Spectacles (1644). Sir William Tite left the Company 2,100l. in railroad stock in 1863; the trust estate totalled 11,667l. In 1882, Freemen numbered 1,300, Livery 400.

The Tallow Chandlers: First documented in letters patent of Henry VI in 1426 as “Master and Wardens of the Mistery or Craft of Tallow Chandlers”; their formal charter came March 8, 1462 (2 Edward IV), with rights to hold realty, a common seal, and a mortmain licence (10 marks yearly). In 1469 they supplied sixty men to the City Watch; by 1515 they ranked twenty-first in Company precedence. Successive charter renewals: November 15, 1517 (8 Henry VIII), February 18, 1549 (2 Edward VI), June 7, 1557, March 3, 1561 (3 Elizabeth), March 6, 1606 (3 James I). Tallow Chandlers’ Hall on Dowgate Hill, referenced in bye-laws of 1639, was destroyed in the Great Fire and rebuilt in 1672.

The Tin-plate Workers: Believed an offshoot of the Girdlers, operating under the designation “Wire Workers” from Edward IV’s reign, associated with the Pinners. In 1569 the bipartite body sought incorporation under the Girdlers’ charter; by 1670 (22 Charles II) the Wire Workers were formally incorporated with bye-laws by 1678. A Livery fine of 31l. 10s. is payable on election to the Court. The Company has organised two Exhibitions of Tin and Wire Work since 1878. Trust income derives from the wills of John Miers and John White (1,145l. in total). Livery: 77 (1882), 71 (1892).

The Turners: Several named Turners are documented in 1310 as sworn to make only gallons, pottles, and quarts; by 1347 further restrictions regulated material and marks for liquid measures. In 1418, the Company received 4s. per hundred for 2,500 wooden cups supplied for the siege of Rouen. The first and only known charter, granted June 12, 1604, conferred a licence in mortmain (20l. annually), search rights within five miles, and the power to mark vessels — powers since lost to municipal action. No bye-laws were drawn until 1823. Governance: Master, Upper and Renter Warden, and twenty-four Assistants, with unlimited Livery. Annual Exhibitions of Turners’ Work at Mansion House have been sponsored since 1870 by Lady Burdett Coutts and Samuel Morley MP. Livery: 112 (1699), 239 (1882), 193 (1892).

The Tylers and Bricklayers: After the Fire of 1212 mandated tiles and slate instead of reed and rush roofs, the Tyler trade developed; orders in 1245 required principal thoroughfares to use slates or tiles. In 1302, Thomas Bat contracted to tile roofs in St Laurence Parish. Their charter came late in 1568, with a second Elizabeth charter in 1571 authorising search for defective bricks and tiles, and a third James I charter from Gorhambury (April 20, 1604). Ben Jonson’s connection to the Company as a working bricklayer is probably inaccurate; Hazlitt suggests he became free by patrimony through his stepfather’s membership, which may have facilitated contact with architect Inigo Jones. The former Hall, near Aldgate Ward, is now occupied by City of London College. Livery: 78 (1882), 73 (1892).

The Upholders (Upholsters): Originally dealers in second-hand clothes (Fripperers), concentrated in Cornhill shops vacated by Drapers in the fourteenth century, the Upholders evolved into storekeepers selling cushions, cupboards, curtains, feather beds, sheets, and ironmongery. A 1356 inventory of Stephen le Northerne reveals this mixed commerce; Lydgate’s London Lickpenny (c. 1425) describes them settled in Cornhill. In 1465 they obtained a grant of arms, and in 1469 contributed twenty-four guards to the City Watch. Sir Bartholomew James (son of Edward James, Citizen and Upholder) became Lord Mayor in 1479. Their charter dates to 1626 (Charles I). In 1882, the Livery numbered 38; income derives from 8,350l. invested (320l. yearly from Hall sale proceeds) plus Jackson’s Charity distributing 20l. annually.

The Wax Chandlers: Bye-laws extending to 1358 regulated supervisors and the manufacture of torches, cierges, prikets, and candles; the 1371 renewal detailed pricing and funeral torch hire. A charter from Richard III in 1483 reflected the Company’s humble standing with only five marks mortmain. In 1469 they supplied twenty-one men to the City Watch; by 1502, held a Livery of seventeen; by 1515, ranked twentieth in precedence. Supporters were added to their arms by William Camden in 1602. The Hall, originally in Ingane Lane (Maiden Lane) by 1493, stood in what became Gresham Street; destroyed in 1666 and replaced by a modern structure facing Haberdashers’ Hall. Governance: Master, two Wardens (Upper and Renter), twenty Assistants; Livery unlimited. Income 1892: 1,600l. (230l. from trusts).

The Weavers: The Telarii or Weavers claim to be the most ancient Anglo-Norman Gild, with a charter from Henry II (1154–89) confirming grants of Henry I that authorised the Gild in exchange for two marks of gold at Michaelmas. A second Henry II charter (31st year, 1184–5) specified that cloth from Spanish wool mixed with English must be burned by the Portgrave. By 14 Edward II (1320–1) a Scire facias required demonstration of authority for election of bailiffs and exclusion of strangers. Edward III (1330) invited foreigners to settle and teach English subjects. Multiple ordinances governed cloth composition, weight, and breadth, as well as fulling and dyeing; the Staple’s removal from Calais (1361) to Westminster and English towns is documented. Notable business was in Hosier Lane and Cordwainers’ Street, Holborn. Livery: 178 (1892).

The Wheelwrights: Incorporated by charter of February 3, 1670, constituting wheelwrights within the City and five miles’ compass as “Master, Wardens, Assistants, and Commonalty of the Art and Mistery of Wheelwrights.” Bye-laws of October 12, 1670 imposed a 5l. penalty on members refusing to furnish waggons or artillery for His Majesty’s train, and prohibited making wheels in brewers’ or coachmakers’ shops. Searches divided jurisdiction into North and South sides, conducted twice yearly until 1750. Livery (applied for 1773, limited to 100, enlarged to 150 in 1792 and 250 in 1817) numbered 105 in 1882. Annual income: 300l. (1892), with no actual trusts save nominal sums from Sir William Leighton and William Leedham.

The Woolmen: The Woolmen claim descent from Wool-staplers traceable to Edward I’s reign; Gregory de Rokesley, eight times Mayor, headed a list of fifty-seven licensed Staplers in 2 Edward I, combining his goldsmith and Assay-master offices with substantial wool export commerce. The Woolmen are identical with Woolpackers and Woolwinders; under Charles II’s proclamation, Wool-combers were placed under their jurisdiction. The Statute of 27 Edward III cap. 23 vested in the body rights to appoint licensed wool-winders. Sir John Crosby, described by Stow as “Grocer and Woollman,” built Crosby Place (1466). Ordinances of 1587 survive (the claimed charter lost in the 1666 Fire), detailing governance by Master, two Wardens, and eleven Assistants. In 1825, the Court of Aldermen granted a Livery of forty; by 1892, only twenty-four liverymen remained. The Company met at Founders’ Hall by arrangement (from April 3, 1672) at 40s. yearly.

The Scriveners (lines ~26818–27337)

Hazlitt traces the Scriveners from their original designation as “the Fraternity or Mistery of the Scriveners or Writers of the Court Letter of the City of London,” associated at one time with the Limners who constituted “the germ of the Stationers’ Company.” Their exemption from jury service (1357), their Ordinances of 1373–4 (requiring every Scrivener to set his name to documents he copied; livery rules; Whitsuntide gowns), 1390, 1440 (apprenticeship, examination of all craftsmen), 1497 and 1557. The growth of the Scriveners’ avocation in conveyancing and finance from the 15th through the 17th century; frauds and pillory punishments (1367, 1376, 1391). Note: all ancient papers of the Scriveners except the Minute Book were lost in the 1666 Fire.

The Stationers (lines ~27337–27902; pp. 625–641)

The origin of the name from the “stations” — fixed market-points at Cheapside crosses — at which dealers stood. As early as 1379 the Corporation leased a set of stations to eleven named women. The Scrivener and the Stationer had a common early history but diverged; “the Stationers gained and preserved an ascendency, because the most lucrative features in the Scriveners’ earlier business — conveyancing and financing — passed away from them.”

The 1403 Corporation Ordinance for “the Writers of Text-Letter, Limners, and others, who bind and sell books”: two Wardens appointed. Pynson and Wynkyn de Worde were probably members of the pre-incorporation Fraternity; Wynkyn de Worde is specifically cited; the custom of registering copies is believed to predate the 1556 charter.

The 1556 Charter under Philip and Mary: the charter preamble is quoted in full, identifying censorship of “seditious and heretical books, rhymes, and treatises” as the explicit motive. Cost to the grantees: just over £20 (including 9s. for a rejected first draft). Thomas Dockwray first Master; John Cawood (royal printer) and Henry Cooke first Wardens. Elizabeth’s confirmation: 10 November 1559.

Quo Warranto proceedings of 1663 and 1684: the 1663 writ arose not from ordinary hostility but from “a belief that the Company was not sufficiently vigilant and strict in its capacity as a curb on the press, and connived at the publication of inflammatory or scurrilous literature.” Charter restored by Act of 2 William and Mary (1690). Power of search was a prominent feature of the original charter concession.

Patent invasions c.1620: the Company’s sole printing rights were challenged by a monopoly created for three individuals, one of whom was an alien, who did not print themselves but assigned work to others at a price.

Common Stock system: Latin stock, Irish stock, ballad stock, Bible stock. About 1601 a Crown grant consolidated these into the English Stock. In 1882 the English Stock had capital of £41,000–42,000 among 306 members; £400 per year distributed to the poor.

Membership: Livery of 226 (1629), 214 (1724–5), 312 (1882), 282 (1892). Freemen: 445 (1882). About 100 apprentices annually bound at the Hall.

Hall history — five locations: (1) Clement Court, Milk Street, Cheapside (probably mid-15th century); (2) Peter College at the south-west angle of St Paul’s churchyard, formerly converted by William Seres into a printing office after the Dissolution — the Company moved here c.1550; (3) frontage of Peter College let to Edward Kynaston (vintner) in 1606, who made it the Feathers Tavern; (4) Abergavenny House acquired 1611; (5) rebuilt after the 1666 Great Fire (completed 1674), wainscoting contracted with Stephen College, the “Protestant Joiner,” for £300.

Great Fire losses: Hall “reduced to a wreck, with the contents and even the common seal, the registers excepted.” The registers survived because they were with the Clerk at his house in Clerkenwell Green. All plate — including a silver-gilt spoon presented by first Master Thomas Dockwray — was sold in 1643 to Nowell, a goldsmith in Foster Lane, to meet liabilities of about £1,500; “Mr. Hulet’s standing cup” alone was retained.

Court Records suppressions: French books burned by the Primate (1595); Raleigh’s History of the World directed to be suppressed by the Archbishop of Canterbury (1614); Lithgow’s Travels (1616); Prynne’s Histriomastix erased from the books (1634); MS and whole impression of Buchanan’s History of Scotland seized. John Stow: awarded £3 and forty copies for his Survey of London (1602) and 20s. and fifty copies for his Brief Chronicle.

Notable members: Thomas Berthelet (whose wife gave the Company 13s. 4d. at his burial), Reginald Wolfe (four times Master), John Cawood, Richard Jugge, Richard Tottell, the Harrisons, the Days, the Nortons, the Barkers, the Rivingtons. Richard Field is named as “son of Henry Field, of Stratford-upon-Avon, tanner, Shakespear’s fellow-townsman, and the publisher of his youthful productions. Field married the daughter of Thomas Vautrollier, and the poet is supposed to have profited by some of the classical or historical books which proceeded from both presses.” Later members: Samuel Richardson (novelist, Master 1754), Jacob Tonson (great-nephew of Dryden’s Tonson), Henry Sampson Woodfall (printer of the Letters of Junius, Master 1797).

Illustrations: Stationers’ barge and waterman; Stationers’ Hall in 1670 and 1842; devices and monograms of Hugh Singleton, Reginald Wolfe, Christopher Barker (from Gascoigne’s Glasse of Government 1575), Robert Wyer, Richard Smith, Richard Jones, Richard Tottell, William Seres; original common seal (lost in 1666); mark of Richard Tottell; mark of John Day (1548–71); title-page of Lydgate’s Vertue of the Masse (Wynkyn de Worde); Autographs of Eminent Stationers of the 16th Century.

Literary Notices: Orders, Rules, and Ordinances (1678, 1682); Charters and Grants (1741); 1553 Proclamation against seditious books; 1558 Proclamation against Diuers Books; Ordinances decreed for reformation of diuers disorders in printing (29 June 1566); Patent to Ralph Bowes and Thomas Beddingfield for playing cards (1571); Stationers’ Hall entry for French card mould (1588); booksellers’ case against trading beyond the sea (James I); general note on prices for binding (1619, 1646); Patent to Roger Wood and Thomas Symcocke for sole printing on paper and parchment (1620); Bookbinders’ complaint against Goldbeaters’ monopoly [1621]; Journeymen Printers’ petition to Parliament [c.1630]. ^[source: hazlitt_livery_companies_raw.txt]
