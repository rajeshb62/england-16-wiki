Lyman Ray Patterson, Copyright in Historical Perspective. Vanderbilt University Press, 1968. First paperback edition 2000. ~15,031 lines extracted from PDF. Legal history of copyright from the Stationers’ Company (1557) through the Statute of Anne (1710) and into American copyright law.

Patterson’s central argument: copyright developed not as an author’s right but as a private guild monopoly — the stationer’s copyright — that was sustained for 150 years by government censorship policy, which shielded it from judicial interference. When censorship lapsed in 1694, the guild’s monopoly collapsed, and the Statute of Anne (1710) was enacted as a trade-regulation measure — not an author’s rights statute. The persistent confusion between publisher’s right (stationer’s copyright) and author’s right is Patterson’s explanation for continuing legal discontent with copyright down to his own time. ^[source: copyright_patterson_raw.txt]

Contents — Chapter-by-Chapter

Overview (lines 159–1010)

Patterson opens with the landmark American case Wheaton v. Peters (1834) and works backward to identify the English lineage of American copyright: the Statute of Anne (1709) → the Star Chamber Decree of 1637 → the Stationers’ Company charter (1557). His starting date for the study is 1557. He identifies three principal features of the pre-Statute of Anne period: the stationer’s copyright, the printing patent, and government press control.

The overview summarises Patterson’s thesis about the Statute of Anne: it was not the “first English copyright act” (as commonly stated), since the Star Chamber Decrees and the Licensing Act of 1662 were also copyright acts. It was the first parliamentary copyright act without censorship provisions. Its statutory copyright was modelled on the stationer’s copyright and the Licensing Act of 1662, with modifications aimed at the problem of monopoly. The statute is best understood as a trade-regulation measure rather than an author’s rights statute. ^[source: copyright_patterson_raw.txt]

Prelude: Early Government Press Control (lines 1011–1386)

The introduction of the printing press into England (Caxton, 1476) and the government’s response. Henry VII appointed Peter Actors as the first royal printer in 1485 in a grant “remarkably free of any restrictions.” The government moved from encouraging printing to controlling it as religious and political controversy intensified.

Patterson argues that copyright was not a product of censorship, nor would its development have been prevented by the absence of censorship — but censorship did protect the early stationer’s copyright from judicial interference. He surveys the Tudor censorship regulations up to 1557: Henry VIII’s proclamations banning Lutheran books; Edward VI’s use of proclamations for press control; the reign of Mary I and the Stationers’ Company’s charter issued by Philip and Mary in 1557. The chapter also covers the book-trade context: Caxton’s monopoly position at court, the absence of significant piracy in the early decades, and why the concept of property rights in publishing was left to printers and publishers rather than authors. ^[source: copyright_patterson_raw.txt]

The Stationers’ Company (lines 1387–2304)

The Stationers’ Company received its charter from Philip and Mary on 4 May 1557. Patterson traces the company’s earlier existence as a craft guild (the stationers appear in London records from the fourteenth century), the absorption of bookbinders, illuminators, and booksellers, and how the charter transformed this informal trade grouping into a chartered monopoly of the London book trade.

The chapter covers the company’s governing structure: the Court of Assistants; the Wardens; the system of apprenticeship and freedom; the registers (Register A, B, C etc., transcribed by Arber, covering 1554–1708). The company’s principal records are described in detail, including the hiatus in book entries between 1571 and 1576.

Patterson analyses the company’s control over printing through its monopoly: only members of the company (or holders of royal patents) could legally print. The company served government interests as a self-regulating police of the press — a role that gave it its monopoly power — while also serving member interests by protecting their copyrights from each other. The gradual shift of power from printers to booksellers (well advanced by Christopher Barker’s 1582 report, which complained that booksellers “being growen the greater and wealthier nomber haue nowe many of the best Copies and keepe no printing howse”) is tracked through the company records. ^[source: copyright_patterson_raw.txt]

The Stationer’s Copyright (lines 2305–4320)

The stationer’s copyright was a publisher’s right, not an author’s right. It was created by the practice of entering a title in the company’s register before printing — “entrance” — which gave the registering stationer the exclusive right to print that work. Patterson analyses the mechanics:

Printing and publishing arrangements. Four basic forms: (1) printer-publisher-bookseller as single person (early period, when printers were dominant); (2) trade printer working for bookseller-publishers (growing through the 16th century); (3) author-publisher arrangements; (4) combinations. The shift toward bookseller dominance had displaced printers from the most profitable copyrights by the late 16th century.

Entrance procedure. The stationer paid a fee (usually fourpence for a small book, sixpence for a large one) and the title and author were entered in the register. Patterson analyses whether entrance was required to acquire copyright (it was practically required for enforcement within the company but not legally required to establish the right).

The printer’s right. A right analogous to copyright, held by the printer in his investment of type and labour — distinct from the publisher’s stationer’s copyright. Patent and copy interacted in complex ways when a printer held a patent for a category of books.

Author’s rights under the stationer’s copyright. None, in the formal sense: the stationer’s copyright was entirely the publisher’s, and the author had no legal interest once he sold his manuscript. Patterson traces early instances of authors receiving payments, but finds no legal mechanism for authors to claim rights in their own works. ^[source: copyright_patterson_raw.txt]

The Printing Patent (lines 4321–6243)

The royal prerogative to grant printing patents — monopoly rights to print specific categories of books (psalters, primers, law books, ABCs) or specific works — existed from the beginning of the English press and operated alongside the stationer’s copyright throughout the 16th and 17th centuries.

Patterson explains the legal basis: the prerogative was of the common law, not above it, but its indefiniteness gave the sovereign broad scope. He traces patents from Henry VII’s grant to Peter Actors (1485) through Elizabeth I’s extensive use of patents in the 1560s–70s. The major patents covered the most profitable categories: law books (Tottel), psalters (Day), primers (Seres), catechisms (Day), almanacs and prognostications.

The conflict between patents and the Stationers’ Company monopoly: individual patents created monopolies within the book trade that disadvantaged company members without patents. The crisis of the 1570s, when a group of poorer stationers petitioned against the monopoly of patents, is analysed in detail. Resolution came through the English Stock (1603), a joint investment scheme through which the company collectively held the most profitable patents, distributing income to members proportionally.

The printing patent contributed to copyright development the idea that copyright is a government grant — a concept Patterson argues has had lasting effect, particularly in American law where the patent and copyright clauses were joined in the Constitution. ^[source: copyright_patterson_raw.txt]

Copyright and Censorship (lines 6244–7913)

The relationship between the stationer’s copyright and government censorship policy. Patterson opens with Milton’s Areopagitica (1644) distinguishing legitimate copyright from its use as an excuse for censorship and monopoly.

Three major censorship instruments under the Tudors and Stuarts:

Star Chamber Decree of 1586. Issued by the Star Chamber under Archbishop Whitgift. Restricted printing to London (except one press each at Oxford and Cambridge), required pre-publication licensing, regulated the number of presses, and empowered the Stationers’ Company to search for and seize unlicensed books. It was primarily beneficial to the company: it gave the company enforcement powers backed by Star Chamber authority.

Star Chamber Decree of 1637. Extended and tightened the 1586 decree. Thirty-three clauses. Strengthened licensing requirements; added provisions specifically desired by the stationers. Patterson quotes the decree’s detailed provisions on printer selection, controlled by the Archbishop of Canterbury and Bishop of London.

Licensing Act of 1662. Enacted after the Restoration, extending press control into the Stuart period. It lapsed in 1679, was renewed, and finally lapsed permanently in 1694. Its lapse ended the legal basis for the company’s private copyright, creating the crisis that led to the Statute of Anne.

Patterson traces how the stationers lobbied successfully for each successive censorship measure by arguing the need for order in the book trade, using censorship to reinforce their monopoly, and why the courts did not interfere with copyright during the censorship period — the government’s own enforcement apparatus made judicial challenge impractical. ^[source: copyright_patterson_raw.txt]

The Statute of Anne (lines 7914–8308)

The Statute of Anne (8 Anne, c. 19; enacted 1709, effective April 1710). Patterson analyses it as a trade-regulation statute directed at three monopoly problems: the company’s general monopoly; the booksellers’ existing copyrights in old works (addressed by giving those works a 21-year term only); and future monopoly (addressed by limiting new copyrights to 14 years with one renewal).

He analyses the statute’s language closely: “author” in the statute is always used alternatively with “purchaser of copy,” “proprietor,” “bookseller,” or “assignee.” The title (“An Act for the Encouragement of Learning, by vesting the Copies of printed Books in the Authors or Purchasers”) names both authors and purchasers, making clear the statutory copyright was available to anyone, not just authors. The radical change was not giving authors rights but opening rights previously limited to Stationers’ Company members to all persons.

Three provisions directed specifically at monopoly: Section III (alternative Gazette registration if the clerk refused to register); Section IV (price controls); Section VII (exclusion of foreign-language books from the prohibition). ^[source: copyright_patterson_raw.txt]

Copyright in England from 1710 to 1774 (lines 8309–10629)

The booksellers’ campaign to preserve their monopoly after the Statute of Anne by arguing that authors possessed a perpetual common-law copyright which they could convey to booksellers — thereby circumventing the statute’s term limits. Patterson traces the legal and political battle: the Society for the Encouragement of Learning (1736), formed to oppose bookseller control; the booksellers’ parliamentary lobbying; and eventually the two decisive cases: Millar v. Taylor (1769, affirming common-law copyright) and Donaldson v. Beckett (1774, House of Lords, reversing Millar and holding that the Statute of Anne superseded any common-law copyright). Donaldson settled the question: copyright in England was statutory, not perpetual. ^[source: copyright_patterson_raw.txt]

The Early American Copyright (lines 10630–10906)

The first federal copyright act (1790) modelled on the Statute of Anne. Patterson analyses its seven sections: the two categories of copyright (works already printed and works to be printed); the term (14 years plus one renewal); the infringement provisions (printing, importing, or selling with knowledge). The act’s designation of persons entitled — “the author … being citizens or residents of the United States … his or their executors, administrators or assigns” — follows the Statute of Anne in treating authors and purchasers equivalently. ^[source: copyright_patterson_raw.txt]

The Wheaton Case (lines 10907–11358)

Wheaton v. Peters (U.S. Supreme Court, 1834): whether Henry Wheaton, the Supreme Court’s official reporter, retained a common-law copyright in his published volumes of Supreme Court reports. The Court held he did not — statutory compliance was required, and he had not complied fully. The decision adopted the English Donaldson position: American copyright is statutory, not common-law perpetual. Patterson traces the personal history behind the case and its effect in establishing the statutory-grant model as bedrock American copyright doctrine. ^[source: copyright_patterson_raw.txt]

The Continuing Discontent (lines 11359–11739)

Despite Wheaton, legal discontent with copyright persisted. Patterson surveys the evidence: Justice Story’s inability to “lay down any general principles applicable” to all copyright cases; Justice McLean’s acknowledgment of “painful uncertainty in the authorities”; three general revisions of the copyright statute (1831, 1870, 1909); over two hundred copyright bills introduced in Congress by 1904; the Register of Copyrights’ complaint in 1904 that the laws “fail to give the protection required.”

He gives examples of individual authors who suffered under the statutes: Harriet Beecher Stowe lost an infringement case when a German translation of Uncle Tom’s Cabin was held not to be a copy; Oliver Wendell Holmes lost copyright in Autocrat of the Breakfast Table and Professor at the Breakfast Table through serial publication without copyright notice before book publication.

Patterson’s diagnosis: the confusion of the stationer’s copyright (publisher’s right in the act of publication) with the author’s copyright (author’s right in the work) has led courts to treat the statutory copyright as encompassing all of the author’s rights rather than only the right of first publication — producing a concept that serves no single interest cleanly. ^[source: copyright_patterson_raw.txt]

Copyright in Historical Perspective (lines 11740–12199)

Patterson’s concluding chapter. He summarises the historical development as a sequence: copyright began as a device for maintaining order among Stationers’ Company members (16th century); supported by censorship laws, it functioned as a private monopoly for 150 years; when censorship lapsed the monopoly collapsed; the Statute of Anne restored order as a trade-regulation measure; the booksellers captured the concept of “author’s copyright” to perpetuate their monopoly; the Donaldson and Wheaton decisions settled copyright as statutory, but the conceptual confusion between publisher’s right and author’s right remained embedded in the law.

His prescription: separate the creative interest and the economic interest of the author, which have been conflated under copyright — a proposal he argues would allow development of a federal common law of literary property alongside statutory copyright, and provide sounder principles for dealing with modern copyright problems. ^[source: copyright_patterson_raw.txt]

Appendix I — Printing Patents (lines 12204–12799)

Full transcriptions of selected printing patents: the letters patent to William Seres (3 June 1559) for psalters and primers, and the letters patent to John Day (also 1559) for ABCs and catechisms. Both are transcribed from Arber’s Transcript of the Registers of the Company of Stationers. Primary source texts in original Elizabethan spelling. ^[source: copyright_patterson_raw.txt]

Appendix II — Star Chamber Decrees (lines 12800–end)

Full transcription of the Star Chamber Decree of 1586 (and possibly 1637 — text continues beyond the 15,031-line extraction). The 1586 decree is given clause by clause in original spelling, including: the restriction of printing to London plus Oxford and Cambridge; the appointment and presentment of new printers before the Archbishop of Canterbury and Bishop of London; the prohibition on printing without authority; the allowance of books by the High Commissioners. ^[source: copyright_patterson_raw.txt]
