Joseph Ames (begun); William Herbert (considerably augmented), Typographical Antiquities: or an Historical Account of the Origin and Progress of Printing in Great Britain and Ireland. Vol. II. London: Printed for the Editor, 1786. ~37,377 lines extracted from a zip of 640 OCR’d page files. Bibliographical history of the English and Irish book trade from 1471 to 1600, organized printer by printer. This volume (the only one in the Anna’s Archive zip) covers printers active in the Elizabethan period, starting at book page [685].

The work is a reference compendium, not a narrative history. Each entry gives: a brief biography of the printer, drawing on Stationers’ Company register records, court-of-assistants orders, and contemporary accounts; followed by a chronological catalogue of books known to have been printed by that printer, with exact title transcriptions, format (folio, quarto, octavo, sixteens), approximate date, and the library or private collection where a copy was located at the time of writing. Herbert substantially expanded Ames’s original 1749 compilation with new biographical material and additional book entries. ^[source: typographical_antiquities_ames_raw.txt]

Quality Assessment

OCR from a University of Virginia library copy of the 1786 printed volume. The body text is generally readable, but has several artifact types:

  • Long ‘s’ as ‘f’ — 18th century typography renders the long ‘s’ as ‘f’ throughout (e.g. “ftationers” for “stationers,” “prefs” for “press,” “licenfe” for “license,” “confiftants” for “assistants”). This is a systematic OCR artifact from the period typeface, not an OCR error per se, and is consistent throughout
  • Marginal footnotes inline — The original has marginal notes and footnotes that appear as interruptions within the main printer entries; these are captured mid-sentence in the OCR output
  • Page headers inline — The printer’s name appears as a running header on each page and is captured inline (e.g. “CHRISTOPHER BARKER.” or “RICHARD TOTTEL.” appearing mid-entry)
  • Page numbers inline — Book page numbers appear as isolated numbers within the text flow
  • Some OCR noise in borders/decorations — Occasional character-noise from ornamental borders around section breaks

Title transcriptions within each entry are in original Elizabethan spelling as printed on the title pages.

Contents — Printer Entries

This volume covers approximately 80 printers active in the mid-to-late 16th century. Entries vary greatly in length: minor figures receive a paragraph; major figures like Christopher Barker or Thomas Vautrollier receive several pages. The following are the most substantial or most relevant entries.

Richard Tottel (lines ~6766–)

Tottel was “a very considerable printer of law, and an original member of the Stationers’ company.” He dwelt in Fleet Street within Temple Bar at the Sign of the Hand and Star. He held a succession of patents for printing common law books: first granted under Edward VI (7 Edw. VI, 12 April); renewed under Philip and Mary (1 May, Pat. 2 & 3 Phil. & Mary); and extended for life under Elizabeth (12 Jan., Pat. 1 Eliz.). A draft patent for cosmography books was prepared but apparently not signed. He served as renter 1559–60; under-warden 1561; upper warden 1567, 1568, and 1574; master 1578 and 1584. In his final years he withdrew to the country for health, causing business delays; the Court of Assistants noted his “continuall absence” and he was removed from the Assistantship in 1589. His career continued until 1593. The entry lists his complete known output including law reports, statutes, and Tottel’s Miscellany (a foundational anthology of English verse). ^[source: typographical_antiquities_ames_raw.txt]

William Seres (lines ~142–)

Seres began in partnership with John Day, though Herbert notes they did not form a strict partnership and were also each “occasionally concerned with” other printers. He held a patent for psalters and primers. His entry records books printed jointly with Day and separately under his own imprint across several decades. ^[source: typographical_antiquities_ames_raw.txt]

Henry Denham (lines ~14462–)

A substantial entry for Denham, who printed across the 1560s–80s. The books listed include a wide range of Protestant devotional, historical, and literary works. ^[source: typographical_antiquities_ames_raw.txt]

Henry Bynneman (lines ~15696–)

Bynneman held a printing privilege covering certain categories of books including chronicles. His entry covers his output in the 1570s–80s. ^[source: typographical_antiquities_ames_raw.txt]

Thomas Vautrollier (lines ~21394–)

Vautrollier was “a Frenchman, a scholar and printer from Paris or Rouen, who came into England about the beginning of Q. Elizabeth’s reign” and was admitted a brother of the Stationers’ Company on 2 Oct. 1564. He set up his press in Blackfriars. He printed Giordano Bruno’s works in 1584 and fled to Edinburgh thereafter, where he introduced quality printing to Scotland; he stayed there until friends interceded to obtain his pardon. He was absent from London from at least July 1581 and was in Scotland in 1584 and 1585, though London books continued to bear his name. He returned to London and died before 4 March 1587-8. He used the device of an anchor with two sprigs of laurel and the motto Anchora Spei. He married his daughter Jakin to Richard Field, who succeeded to his house and business. His entry lists extensive output including Beza’s New Testament, handwriting manuals, and works in French, Latin, and English across the 1570s and 1580s. ^[source: typographical_antiquities_ames_raw.txt]

Christopher Barker (lines ~22048–)

Barker was “of an ancient family.” Elizabeth granted him “a very extensive patent” on 28 Sept. 1577 (19 Eliz.) “in consideration of his great improvement of the art of printing.” He dwelt first in Paternoster Row at the Sign of the Tiger’s Head and kept a shop in St. Paul’s Churchyard at the Sign of the Grasshopper; Herbert notes the Tiger’s Head was also the crest of Sir Francis Walsingham, “his patron.” He had a printing office in Bacon House near Foster Lane where Acts of Parliament were printed. Originally a member of the Drapers’ Company, he was translated to the Stationers on 4 June 1578, served as upper warden 1582 and 1585, and from 1588 carried on business wholly through deputies. He died 29 Nov. 1599, aged about 70, and was buried at Datchet near Windsor; his memorial inscription in Latin survives. His 1582 report to the Privy Council on the state of the book trade — in which he described how booksellers “being growen the greater and wealthier nomber haue nowe many of the best Copies” — is not quoted at length here but his career context illuminates it. The entry lists his extensive Bible-printing output as Queen’s Printer and other Elizabethan publications. ^[source: typographical_antiquities_ames_raw.txt]

John Charlewood (lines ~23167–)

Charlewood printed across the 1570s–80s. His entry records output including dramatic and literary works. ^[source: typographical_antiquities_ames_raw.txt]

Robert Waldegrave (lines ~26306–)

Waldegrave’s entry records his printing output in the mid-1580s. A marginal note in the text notes that his house was searched and his goods seized — the Marprelate note (below) connects this to John Wolfe’s role as enforcer. His books in this period include Protestant devotional and sermon literature. ^[source: typographical_antiquities_ames_raw.txt]

George Bishop (lines ~26663–)

Bishop was a significant Elizabethan stationer. His entry covers multiple pages of output across the 1580s–90s, including chronicle history and legal and theological works. ^[source: typographical_antiquities_ames_raw.txt]

John Wolf(e) (lines ~28247–)

Wolfe’s entry covers his printing output from the early 1580s, including Italian-language publications and Protestant theological works. A marginal note records that he was active “at the search of Waldegrave’s house for such [unlicensed books],” and quotes Martin Marprelate’s epithet for him: “John Woolfe (alias Machivill) most tormenting executioner of Waldegrave’s goods.” The entry lists works printed jointly and separately across the 1580s. ^[source: typographical_antiquities_ames_raw.txt]

Roger Ward (lines ~29630–)

A printer active in the later Elizabethan period. Entry records output across the 1580s–90s. ^[source: typographical_antiquities_ames_raw.txt]

John Windet (lines ~31579–)

Windet was active in the 1580s–1600s. His entry records a range of output. ^[source: typographical_antiquities_ames_raw.txt]

Edward Allde (lines ~32556–)

Allde was active in the later Elizabethan and early Jacobean period. His entry records output from the 1580s onward. ^[source: typographical_antiquities_ames_raw.txt]

Richard Field (lines ~33477–)

Field was “son of Henry Field of Stratford on Avon, Tanner.” He was apprenticed to George Bishop for seven years from Michaelmas 1579, made free 6 Feb. 1586-7, and came on the livery 1 July 1598. In 1588 he married Jakin, daughter of Thomas Vautrollier, and succeeded to Vautrollier’s house and business in Blackfriars, using the same devices and printing the same copies. He was fined in May 1589 for printing a book contrary to order. His entry lists output from 1589 onward, including Puttenham’s Arte of English Poesie (1589), Ovid’s Metamorphoses, a Drake voyage account, and works in French and English across the 1590s. ^[source: typographical_antiquities_ames_raw.txt]

Index of Printers (lines ~36948–37377)

Alphabetical index of all printers covered in this volume with their page numbers. Covers approximately 80 printers from Adams through Yetfweirt. ^[source: typographical_antiquities_ames_raw.txt]
