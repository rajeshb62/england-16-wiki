David Hume, The History of England Under the House of Tudor, Vol. II. First published A. Millar, London, 1759. Digital edition: Oxford Text Archive, 2011. ~7.1 MB extracted from mobi via HTML. Covers the reign of Elizabeth I in full (1558–1603) across 7 chapters.

Hume’s Elizabeth is a figure of genuine greatness but also of vanity, dissimulation, and occasional meanness: a ruler whose prudence was extraordinary but whose womanhood (on Hume’s accounting) introduced irrationalities — jealousy of Mary, coquetry with suitors, the Essex infatuation — that a man would have suppressed. The volume covers Philip II’s character, the French Wars of Religion, the Dutch Revolt as Elizabeth experienced it, and the strategic logic of the Anglo-Spanish conflict. ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol2_raw.txt]

Contents and Coverage

Chapter I — The Elizabethan Settlement and Scotland (lines 125–1923)

The accession. Elizabeth enters London through cheering crowds; Hume describes her declining to fall on her knees in the Tower as another prisoner (a Marian one) had done, but instead giving thanks for her deliverance. She receives the Bible from a boy on a triumphal arch and presses it to her bosom — Hume calls this an “innocent artifice” and does not mean it disparagingly: Elizabeth’s management of public affection was a political art.

Philip II’s proposal is declined deftly. Hume gives Elizabeth’s reasoning in full: marrying Philip would confirm her illegitimacy (a parallel to her father’s marriage to Katherine), would place her on a “precarious” throne dependent on Spanish power, and would alienate the nation. She keeps Philip hoping anyway, which is treated as prudent dissimulation.

The religious settlement. Extended account of Cecil’s advice to Elizabeth on the political logic of restoring Protestantism: the pope cannot ratify her title without destroying his own authority; Philip and France can never combine against her; English Catholics will conform to their new sovereign as they have conformed to every previous one. Hume treats the Elizabethan settlement as a political calculation brilliantly executed, not a religious conviction. The Acts of Supremacy and Uniformity pass against an episcopate that refuses to cooperate — most bishops are deprived. ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol2_raw.txt]

Scotland and the Reformation. John Knox, Mary of Guise (the regent), the Lords of the Congregation, French troops in Scotland, English intervention, and the Treaty of Edinburgh (1560). Hume’s Knox is a bigot of genius — indispensable to the Protestant cause, deeply hostile to monarchy, and personally insulting to every queen he encounters. Mary Queen of Scots arrives in Scotland: Hume introduces her as a figure of beauty, charm, and political naivety — not yet a threat, but surrounded by enemies.

Elizabeth’s government. Hume closes the chapter with a long appreciation: open in address, “rejoiced in the concourse of her subjects,” expert at managing Parliament and the people, and possessed of “masculine spirit.” He acknowledges the irony that the woman he called “masculine” ruled for 45 years more successfully than most kings. ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol2_raw.txt]

Chapter II — Mary Queen of Scots and the French Wars (lines 1924–4530)

Philip II’s character. Opens with one of the finest portrait passages in the volume: Philip is “endowed with great industry and sagacity, a remarkable caution in his enterprizes, an unusual foresight in all his measures… ever cool and unmoved by passion.” But “prejudices had on him a more pernicious effect than ever passions had on any other monarch.” His vow after the tempest — to dedicate his life to extirpating heresy — is Hume’s pivot: an intelligent man converted by religion into an engine of destruction. The Elizabeth-Philip opposition is framed as the collection’s central geopolitical conflict: the more virtuous sovereign was “happily thrown into the more favourable cause.” ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol2_raw.txt]

France. The French Wars of Religion as background: Catherine de’ Medici’s failed balancing act, the Huguenots and the Guises, English occupation of Havre de Grace (1562) and its humiliating loss, the Massacre of Vassy. Hume’s account of French religious violence is sustained and horrified: “in a nation renowned for polished manners” — the comparison with civilisation is always implicit.

Mary Queen of Scots: the catastrophe. Darnley, the most detailed sequence in the chapter: Rizzio’s murder (Mary’s Italian secretary, stabbed in her presence, Darnley a participant), Darnley’s own murder at Kirk o’Field (explosion; Hume treats Mary’s complicity as highly probable), Mary’s marriage to Bothwell within months, the Scottish nobles’ insurrection, Mary’s imprisonment at Lochleven, her escape into England. Hume’s assessment: Mary was not a plotter by nature but was used by others — Bothwell, the Guises, the seminary priests — and her passions (love, resentment, pride) consistently overrode her judgment. The conferences at York and Hampton Court: Elizabeth refuses to decide, keeps Mary in limbo — Hume calls this “a species of refined and complicated policy.” ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol2_raw.txt]

Chapter III — Plots, the Duke of Norfolk, and St. Bartholomew’s (lines 4531–7061)

The Puritans. Hume opens with a character sketch of Puritanism: not a coherent theology but a disposition — opposition to ceremony, hostility to bishops, embrace of private judgment — that naturally tends to republicanism. He is more sympathetic to the Puritans as civic actors than as theologians, but hostile to their theocratic ambitions in Scotland under Knox.

The Duke of Norfolk’s conspiracy. Norfolk as Mary’s proposed husband: the “Ridolfi plot” is traced in detail, with Hume noting that Walsingham’s intelligence penetrated it thoroughly. Norfolk is executed (1572). Elizabeth’s reluctance to sign his death warrant: Hume sees this as neither sentiment nor policy but a kind of royal paralysis when confronted with the execution of a peer. The pattern will recur with Mary.

St. Bartholomew’s Massacre (1572). Among the most powerful passages in the volume — Hume’s account of the Paris murders is controlled fury: “that signal of massacre was given by the great bell of the palace; and Guise himself led on the perpetrators.” Catherine de’ Medici and Charles IX’s complicity. The scale of the killing across France. Hume draws the line between the Massacre and England’s accelerating anti-Catholic legislation: the seminary priests trained at Reims were responding to this as much as causing it.

The Low Countries. Don John, Parma, the Pacification of Ghent, Elizabeth’s agonised and reluctant support for the Dutch rebels: she hates the precedent of subjects resisting their sovereign, but cannot afford a Spanish-controlled Channel coast. ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol2_raw.txt]

Chapter IV — Drake, the Anjou Courtship, and Escalation (lines 7062–8716)

Sir Francis Drake. Hume’s account of Drake’s circumnavigation and knighthood is compact and strategic — he is interested in the diplomatic fallout and what Drake’s success signals about the Anglo-Spanish balance, not the adventure narrative. “This man, sprung from mean parents in the county of Devon, having acquired considerable riches by some depredations on the Spaniards in the isthmus of Panama…” — Hume’s prose efficiently places Drake in the context of Elizabethan state piracy. Use alongside sir-francis-drake for the full account.

The Anjou courtship. Elizabeth genuinely considered marrying Anjou, Hume argues, and was half in earnest. Walsingham and the council opposed it; the people’s hostility alarmed her. The courtship dragged on, giving England diplomatic cover with France, until Anjou died in 1584. Hume sees the whole affair as Elizabeth at her most womanlike — not a compliment in his register.

The tightening net. Morton’s execution in Scotland; the young James VI under French influence; Spanish troops in Ireland under San Josepho (massacred by Lord Gray after surrender — Elizabeth expressed displeasure); the assassination of William of Orange (1584); the Bond of Association. Hume traces the escalating logic by which England moved from covert to open war with Spain. ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol2_raw.txt]

Chapter V — Babington, Mary’s Execution, and the Armada (lines 8717–11164)

The Babington Plot. Detailed reconstruction: Ballard’s mission from Rheims, Savage’s assassination vow, Babington’s recruitment of a circle of Catholic gentlemen, their letter to Mary proposing assassination of Elizabeth and her liberation, Mary’s reply. Walsingham’s penetration of the correspondence via a mole in the beer-barrel delivery. The conspirators’ capture, trial, and executions (September 1586).

Mary’s trial. Hume’s extended analysis of the commissioners’ difficulty in persuading Mary to submit to trial: she refused to acknowledge the jurisdiction of a court that was trying a queen. She eventually appeared, made her defence, and was condemned. Hume weighs Elizabeth’s four months of indecision before signing the death warrant with characteristic balance: it was partly political calculation (would James retaliate?), partly genuine horror at the precedent, partly — he suggests — the women’s rivalry for legitimacy that had structured the entire reign.

Mary’s character assessment. Hume gives Mary one of his most sustained and ambivalent portraits: beauty, charm, courage, political intelligence — all real. But also recklessness, passion, the inability to sustain any consistent policy, and the fatal pattern of placing herself in the hands of men (Bothwell, the Guises, Babington’s circle) who used her. He is not the hostile Protestant Hume of legend; he is the Enlightenment historian who cannot forgive irrationality.

Drake at Cadiz (1587). “Singeing the king of Spain’s beard”: Hume places Drake’s Cádiz raid here as the prelude to the Armada, giving it military and psychological weight. Philip’s Armada plans; English preparations; Howard as Lord Admiral, Drake as vice-admiral.

The Armada (1588). Hume’s account is strategic and compressed: the fireships at Gravelines, the Spanish flight north around Scotland, the storms. He emphasises English discipline and Spanish tactical failures rather than providential deliverance (Hume never invokes Providence). The Portugal expedition (1589): its failure and Drake’s disgrace. ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol2_raw.txt]

Chapter VI — France, Henry IV, and Essex Ascending (lines 11165–12711)

The assassination of the Duke of Guise (December 1588): Henry III has Guise killed in the Château de Blois; Hume calls it “cruel” but concedes “the necessity of it.” The friar Jacques Clément assassinates Henry III (1589) — Hume’s note on the century’s spirit of religious violence: “that bloody spirit of bigotry which distinguishes this century, and a great part of the following one, beyond all other ages of the world.”

Henry IV. The King of Navarre’s accession: the League under Mayenne, Philip’s ambitions to dismember France. Elizabeth’s subsidies to Henry: £22,000, “a larger sum than, as he declared, he had ever seen before.” The campaigns: Coutras, Ivry, the siege of Paris. Parma’s relief of Paris. Henry IV’s conversion to Catholicism (1593): “Paris is worth a mass.” Hume treats this pragmatically — a rational concession to political reality, not apostasy.

Essex ascending. Robert Devereux, Earl of Essex, appears here as Elizabeth’s new favourite — young, charismatic, militarily ambitious, politically reckless. Hume establishes the psychological dynamic early: Elizabeth indulged Essex where she would have contained other men. The pattern anticipates the catastrophe of Chapter VII. Naval enterprises: the Islands Voyage, the attack on Cádiz (1596 — a success that inflamed Essex’s ambitions). ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol2_raw.txt]

Chapter VII — Ireland, Essex’s Fall, and Elizabeth’s Death (lines 12712–end)

Ireland. Hume opens with a long and remarkably harsh analysis of English colonial failure in Ireland: four centuries of nominal dominion maintained by insufficient force, laws that excluded the Irish from English protections, private conquest that produced a lawless settler class that “degenerated into mere Irish.” The argument is Enlightenment in structure — institutional analysis of why a colonial system produces the violence it claims to suppress. Tyrone’s rebellion (1593–1603) is the climax.

Essex in Ireland. Elizabeth sends Essex to Ireland with a large army and high expectations; he wastes it in marches, signs an unauthorised truce with Tyrone, and returns to England without leave. Hume’s account of Essex’s dash from Ireland, his unexpected appearance in Elizabeth’s bedchamber before she had dressed, and her controlled response — gracious on the day, punitive within weeks — is one of the volume’s most vivid scenes. Essex’s disgrace, his intrigues with James VI of Scotland, his insurrection (February 1601): a hundred horsemen in the streets of London who find no followers. His trial and execution. Hume is colder on Essex than most later historians: he sees a man of charm and military talent destroyed by self-dramatisation and an inability to calculate odds.

Mountjoy and Tyrone. Charles Blount, Lord Mountjoy, succeeds where Essex failed: systematic campaign, scorched-earth in Ulster, breaking Tyrone’s support. Spanish troops land at Kinsale (1601) and are defeated. Tyrone submits just days after Elizabeth’s death — she never knows the Irish war is won.

Elizabeth’s death and character. She declines into silence after Essex’s execution, refusing food, refusing to go to bed, sitting on cushions on the floor of her chamber for days. She dies on 24 March 1603.

Hume’s full character assessment occupies several pages and is the volume’s centrepiece. He catalogues her gifts at length: “masculine understanding,” prudent government, management of Parliament, use of talented ministers, the creation of a prosperous and confident England. He then qualifies systematically: vanity about her appearance and her suitors; excessive parsimony with money and with honours; jealousy of capable women (Mary Queen of Scots, her own ladies); indecision at crisis moments (Norfolk, Mary, Essex). His conclusion: “on the whole, she possessed the virtues of kings, with some of the weaknesses of women.” ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol2_raw.txt]

The survey of arts, manners, commerce, and learning that closes the chapter covers the Elizabethan economy, overseas trade and the merchant companies, the state of the navy, printing and literacy, the Elizabethan literary flowering (Spenser, Sidney, Shakespeare mentioned by name), and the condition of the universities. ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol2_raw.txt]
