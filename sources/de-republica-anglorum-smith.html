De Republica Anglorum: A Discourse on the Commonwealth of England

Author: Sir Thomas Smith (1513–1577) Editor: L. Alston (with a Preface by F. W. Maitland) Publisher: Cambridge University Press, 1906 Original composition: 1562–1565 (written while Smith was Elizabeth’s ambassador in France) First published: 1583 (posthumously) Source type: Primary source (constitutional/political treatise)

Raw text: de_republica_anglorum_raw.txt

Overview

Sir Thomas Smith’s De Republica Anglorum is a foundational description of the English constitution and commonwealth as it existed under Queen Elizabeth I. Written while Smith was serving as ambassador to France (1562–1566), the work offers a contemporary’s account of how England was governed — its monarchy, parliament, laws, courts, and social hierarchy. It is one of the most cited primary sources on Tudor governance.

Smith was a distinguished scholar and statesman: a Cambridge regius professor of civil law, fellow of Queens’ College, public orator, secretary of state under both Edward VI and Elizabeth I, ambassador to France, and a privy councillor. He was a key figure in the introduction of Erasmian Greek pronunciation and the reform of English spelling.

The book is divided into three books. Book I describes the forms of commonwealths and the social orders of England. Book II describes the institutions of government — Parliament, the monarchy, courts, and legal procedures. Book III covers appeals and their absence in English criminal procedure.

Contents

Book I — Of the Diversities of Commonwealths and the Social Orders of England

Chapter 1 — Of the diversities of commonwealths. Smith opens with the three classical forms of government drawn from Aristotle and Plato: monarchy (rule by one, for the common good), aristocracy (rule by the best few), and democracy or politeia (rule of the multitude). Each is defined by its purpose — governance oriented to the common good — as distinct from its corrupt counterparts discussed in Chapter 3. Smith frames the entire work within this ancient taxonomy before applying it to England.

Chapter 2 — What is just or Law in every commonwealth. Smith engages Thrasymachus’s challenge from Plato’s Republic — that justice is merely whatever the stronger party ordains — and rejects it. His own definition: law is what the whole body of a commonwealth has established for the common good, not what any single faction imposes for its own advantage. This sets the constitutional standard against which England’s specific institutions are then measured.

Chapter 3 — An other division of commonwealths. The six forms completed: each of the three good forms has a corrupt counterpart. Monarchy degenerates into tyranny (the monarch ruling for himself alone); aristocracy into oligarchy (the few ruling for their own benefit); democracy into mob rule or ochlocracy (the multitude exploiting the rest). Smith follows Aristotle’s schema closely.

Chapter 4 — Example of changes in government. Illustrated through Roman history — kings giving way to consuls, consuls to dictators, dictators to emperors. The lesson is that constitutional change is natural and follows from the accumulating pressures of circumstance. No form is permanent; all are subject to internal decay and external shock.

Chapter 5 — Of the question what is right and just. When is disobedience against unjust government warranted? Smith examines Dion against Dionysius of Syracuse, and Brutus against Caesar. His conclusion is characteristically cautious: rebellion against a tyrant may be theoretically justifiable but is “alwayes a doubtfull and hasardous matter.” He stops well short of endorsing resistance theory — the practical statesman’s hedge.

Chapter 6 — That commonwealths are not simple but mixed. No pure form of government exists in reality. Like the four Aristotelian elements, all real governments are compounds. England is not a pure monarchy but a mixed constitution combining monarchical, aristocratic, and democratic elements — a claim Smith will substantiate through the rest of the book.

Chapter 7 — The definition of a King and of a Tyrant. Three differences distinguish king from tyrant: how authority was obtained (just succession vs. usurpation), how it is administered (by law vs. by will), and to what end (the common good vs. self-interest). Smith notes that these three criteria produce four permutations: a man can be a tyrant by entry but rule justly (Octavius/Augustus), or come to power legitimately but rule tyrannically (Nero). The full tyrant scores badly on all three; the good king scores well on all.

Chapter 8 — Of the absolute king. Absolute power — power unchecked by law — is legitimate in one sphere only: war, where the speed of decision makes legal process impossible. In peace, absolute power corrupts. Smith cites Plato: no man can bear unchecked authority without becoming unjust, which is why Rome limited its dictators to six months. The English king is not absolute in peacetime; the Privy Council, Parliament, and common law all constrain him.

Chapter 9 — Of the name King and the administration of England. The etymology of “cyning” from the Saxon. The English monarch holds authority directly from God, without investiture by any external power — no pope, no emperor. Smith argues that King John’s submission to the papacy in 1213 was never binding on England, because it was not enacted by Parliament. Parliament’s consent is the constitutional threshold that makes obligations binding on the realm.

Chapter 10 — What is a commonwealth. A society of free men, governed by common accord, for the common good. Slaves are excluded: they are instruments, not members, of the polity. Smith questions whether Ottoman despotism qualifies as a true commonwealth at all — if the subjects are not free, the polity is merely a household writ large.

Chapter 11 — The first sort or beginning of a house or family (oikonomia). The family as the first and most natural society: husband and wife in complementary roles, parents and children, master and servants. Smith follows Aristotle’s dictum that man cannot live alone without being either a beast or a god. The household (oikonomia) is the foundation from which larger political arrangements grow.

Chapter 12 — The first and natural beginning of a kingdom (basileia). As the household grows into a tribe, the patriarch continues to rule absolutely — basileia is the natural political consequence of family governance extended over time and distance.

Chapter 13 — The first and natural beginning of aristocracy (aristokratia). When the patriarch dies, his sons deliberate together and take turns in authority. From this fraternal consultation emerges the aristocratic form — deliberative rule among equals who share a common standing.

Chapter 14 — The first original beginning of democracy (demokratia). Growing equality among men makes oligarchic exclusion unsustainable; free men refuse to be shut out of honour and profit. Democracy arises not from a deliberate design but from the pressure of social equality asserting itself against a restricted ruling group.

Chapter 15 — That the commonwealth must be according to the nature of the people. Smith’s shoe-to-foot metaphor: government must fit the governed. A people accustomed to liberty will not endure tyranny without becoming servile or rebellious; an undisciplined people without strong authority falls into disorder. There is no universal best constitution — the question is always which form fits the particular people in question.

Chapter 16 — The division of parts and persons of the commonwealth. Women are excluded from governance of the commonwealth — except when royal authority is attached to blood and title, in which case a queen absolute has the same authority as a king. England is divided into four sorts: nobilitas maior (lords temporal and spiritual), nobilitas minor (knights, esquires, and gentlemen), citizens and burgesses, and yeomen. A fourth sort — labourers and artisans — are outside the active governing structure. Lords and Commons in Parliament represent the first three sorts.

Chapter 17 — Nobilitas maior: dukes, marquises, earls, viscounts, barons. The titled peerage: created by the king or inherited. Smith gives income requirements (baronage needs £1,000 per year minimum) and compares the English peerage to Roman senators and patricians. All bear the title “Lord” and have voice in the upper house of Parliament.

Chapter 18 — Of the second sort of gentlemen: knights. Knights are made, not born. Smith describes the dubbing ceremony — the formula “sus ou sois chivalier au nom de Dieu et de Saint George” — and notes that knights may be compelled at coronations if they hold income of £40 per year or more. He covers the Order of the Garter (founded by Edward III, 26 knights), knights bachelor, and the distinction between knights banneret (made in the field under the royal standard) and ordinary knights.

Chapter 19 — Of esquires. Esquires bear coats of arms and are not strictly separated from gentlemen; originally they were armour-bearers for lords. Smith treats them as the upper stratum of the gentry, not a formally distinct order.

Chapter 20 — Of gentlemen. Gentlemen are defined by blood, lineage, and the continuity of “old riches or prowesse remaining in one stock.” Smith uses the “gilded copper” metaphor for families whose quality has declined: they still pass as gold at a distance but are not the genuine article. The king can create new gentlemen as a husbandman plants new trees — replacing what time has degraded.

Chapter 21 — Whether the manner of England in making gentlemen so easily is to be allowed. Smith defends England’s relatively permeable gentry: any man who studies law, attends university, professes the liberal sciences, or can live without manual labour and bear the outward charges of gentility (dress, servants, liberality) may call himself a gentleman and be called “master.” A herald will grant him arms. Smith argues this costs the crown nothing (unlike France, where fiscal distinctions between estates matter), because in England gentlemen pay more toward public charges, not less, and bear the costs of military service and household display themselves.

Chapter 22 — Of citizens and burgesses. A third estate between gentlemen and yeomen. Citizens and burgesses are freemen of incorporated towns, of sufficient substance to bear public charges. In the shire they have little standing, but in Parliament they are essential: ancient cities return four burgesses, lesser boroughs return two, to give consent in the name of their communities.

Chapter 23 — Of yeomen. Smith calls yeomen the most important commoners in the commonwealth — more travailed in service than any other group. A yeoman is a legalis homo who holds at least 40 shillings of yearly revenue from free land. They do not call themselves gentlemen but carry more prestige than labourers. Yeomen are the backbone of military service — the English foot soldiers who made France “afraid”; the French gentleman on horseback was England’s superior, but the English yeoman on foot was France’s superior. Smith traces the word “yeoman” to the Dutch “yonker man” (a lesser gentleman), adopted in mockery during the wars and domesticated over time. Cato and Aristotle both praised this middling, land-owning, self-sufficient sort as the best citizens.

Chapter 24 — Of the fourth sort who do not rule. Day labourers, poor husbandmen, copyholders, merchants without free land, and all artisans — the Roman capite censi. They have no voice in the commonwealth and are governed rather than governing. But they are not entirely neglected: in cities, for lack of yeomen, they serve on juries; in villages they become churchwardens, alecunners, and constables. Smith closes Book I with a summary: England is governed by three active parts — the Prince, the gentlemen (in two degrees: barons and lesser nobility), and the yeomanry.

Book II — Of Parliament, the Monarchy, Courts, Trials, and Legal Procedures

Chapter 1 — Of the Parliament and its authority. Smith’s central constitutional claim, in language he intends to be definitive: “The most high and absolute power of the realme of Englande, consisteth in the Parliament.” Parliament can abrogate old laws, make new ones, legitimate bastards, establish forms of religion, define succession to the crown, appoint subsidies, restore attainted persons. “All that ever the people of Rome might do either in Centuriatis comitijs or tributis, the same may be doone by the parliament of Englande.” Every Englishman is taken to be present in Parliament — by procuration if not in person — and the consent of Parliament is taken to be every man’s consent.

Chapter 2 — The form of holding Parliament. The Prince sends writs to every lord spiritual and temporal, and to the sheriffs of every shire to return two knights of the shire elected by all gentlemen and yeomen (those with 40 shillings of free land). Ancient cities return four burgesses; boroughs two. On the first day the Lords assemble in robes in the upper house; the Chancellor sits under the Prince as the Prince’s voice. On woolsacks sit the judges (to advise on points of law, but without a vote) and the Secretaries of State. The Commons assemble separately, elect a Speaker, present him at the bar of the upper house, where he disclaims ability, is confirmed by the Chancellor, and then makes four petitions: freedom of speech within the house, freedom from arrest during Parliament, power to discipline their own members, and right to confer with the Lords or the Prince if needed. Bills are read three times in each house and may be amended by committee. The upper house votes individually — “content” or “not content” — with each lord also casting votes by proxy for absent lords. The lower house votes by voice (yea or no), with division by physical separation if the result is doubtful. No bill is law until both houses agree and the Prince gives assent in person on the last day of the session: “Le roy le veult” — or withholds it: “Le roy s’aduisera,” which kills the bill entirely.

Chapter 3 — Of the Monarch, King or Queen of England. The Prince has absolute power in war and peace — declaring war, making alliances, receiving ambassadors — with advice only from the Privy Council, which he himself appoints and dismisses. He proclaims the coin of the realm (controlling weight, fineness, and value) by proclamation alone. He appoints all great officers and judges, both temporal and spiritual. He holds wardship over those holding land in chief. Martial law in the field is absolute: the Prince’s word is law. All writs and criminal prosecutions run in the Prince’s name. England’s king is, Smith argues, “farre more absolute” than the Duke of Venice or the Spartan kings — because the Privy Council is entirely his creature, not an independent check.

Chapter 4 — The chief points wherein commonwealths differ. Five functions distinguish one government from another: lawmaking, war and peace, money provision, officer appointments, and justice. Parliament handles lawmaking and taxation jointly with the Prince; the Prince alone handles war and appointments; and the administration of justice remains to be described.

Chapter 5 — Three forms of trial or judgment in England. Three modes of definitive judgment exist: by Parliament (the highest), by battle, and by the great assize of twelve men. Each is considered in turn.

Chapter 6 — Trial by Parliament. Parliament may judge between private parties or between the Prince and a subject, in criminal or civil matters, following the same bill procedure as for legislation. In practice it is rare: Parliament, occupied with public affairs, is reluctant to involve itself in private quarrels.

Chapter 7 — Trial by battle. Technically still available and not formally abrogated. Rarely used, partly because the clergy always condemned it as unlawful, partly because customs change with time. Smith says he could not learn that it was ever repealed — it simply fell into disuse.

Chapter 8 — Trial by assise or twelve men. Smith introduces the English jury as England’s defining legal institution, contrasting it explicitly with the civil law countries of France and Italy, where judges decide. In England, the decisive verdict is given not by the judge but by twelve laymen — and this applies to civil matters as much as criminal.

Chapter 9 — Of pleas or actions. Criminal pleas are “pleas of the crown” — the Prince is party to every criminal prosecution because the life of his subjects is held only under the Prince’s protection. When a subject is robbed of life or limb, the Prince is damaged and demands an account. Civil pleas are personal (for contract or injury) or real (possessory or writs of right). A “writ” (breve) is the English equivalent of the Roman actio.

Chapter 10 — Of the chief tribunals, benches, or courts of England. Westminster Hall contains three permanent tribunals, fixed there by parliamentary agreement after it proved cumbersome for courts to follow the king. At the entry on the right: the Common Pleas, for civil matters of land and contract. At the upper end on the right: the King’s Bench, for pleas of the crown and criminal matters. On the left: the Chancery, presided over by the Chancellor and masters. Each has its distinct jurisdiction and procedure.

Chapter 11 — Of terms and the Chancery. The common law sits only four times a year (Michaelmas, Hilary, Easter, Trinity terms) — fewer than a third of the year’s days. Smith defends this by reference to Cato’s argument that excessive litigation is a social vice: those who love pleading should feel the cost; industrious men settle their matters at home by neighbourly judgment. The Chancery fills the gap that the common law’s rigidity creates: as the Roman praetor moderated jus summum, the Chancellor provides equity — ex aequo et bono, according to good conscience rather than the strict letter of the law. The Chancery operates by civil law procedure (written bills, witness examination) rather than jury trial.

Chapter 12 — Of judges in common law and the manner of trial. The Prince appoints two chief justices (King’s Bench and Common Pleas) and six or more puisne judges, all drawn from the senior counsellors and serjeants-at-law. They sit at term in their courts, hearing pleadings from counsel on both sides until the case reaches its “issue” — the precise legal or factual question on which everything turns.

Chapter 13 — Of the two manner of issues. Two kinds of issue exist. If both parties agree on the facts but disagree on the law (demurrer), the judges decide — and if the point is difficult, all the judges meet in checquer chamber to pronounce. If the parties disagree on what happened (issue of fact), the twelve men decide. Smith explicitly contrasts England’s procedure with civil law: in England, counsel argue to a legal question first, and fact is then tried by the jury; on the continent, fact is established by witnesses and torture before advocates argue the law. Smith praises the English system as “the most wise, most just, and most well devised meane found out” in Europe.

Chapter 14 — Of the sheriff and the Court of Exchequer. The sheriff (reeve of the shire, vice-comes in Latin) is the key executive officer of the shire, responsible for executing the commands of the courts. The Exchequer (fiscus principis) is the royal treasury and accounting court, where customs, subsidies, and revenues are received. Barons of the Exchequer hear questions that arise under its jurisdiction, decided by twelve men as in common law. The sheriff collects fines and amercements ordered by the courts; particular receivers handle routine revenues.

Chapter 15 — Of the twelve men. The sheriff summons twenty-four to ensure twelve can be assembled after absences and challenges. Either party may challenge individual jurors (for insufficient income, kinship with the other party, enmity, etc.); two of the panel are empanelled first to try the challenges. Once twelve are sworn, sergeants declare the issue from each side, witnesses give evidence openly in public — before judges, justices, parties, and all present; cross-examination is permitted. When the judge has heard enough, he recapitulates briefly for the jury, gives them the written evidence, and bids them retire. A bailiff keeps them without bread, drink, fire, or light until they agree unanimously — all twelve must agree, not merely a majority. The verdict is delivered in the fewest possible words (“guilty” or “not guilty”). The winning party customarily gives the jury dinner; that is all they receive.

Chapter 16 — Of hundreds, lathes, rapes, and wapentakes. The subdivisions of the shire — each named differently by region. Hundreds were originally the 100 townships (or the 100 men) that composed them; lathes and rapes derive from harvest service; wapentakes from Danish weapon-inspection musters. Each hundred has high constables appointed by the Justices of Peace, and its own bailiff appointed by the lord (if a liberty) or by the sheriff.

Chapter 17 — Of the court baron. Every owner of a manor may hold a court baron from three weeks to three weeks for his tenants. Tenants form the “homage” rather than an enquest. Court barons handle admissions of copyholders, deaths of tenants, intrusions by tenants against each other or the lord, and small disputes. Any party who prefers can remove a case to Westminster by writ. The court baron serves those who prefer neighbourly settlement over the delay and expense of the common law.

Chapter 18 — Of the leete or law day. A manorial court with special Crown grant or long prescription, held twice yearly. Its original function was broader: swearing men to the Prince, taking pledges, inquiring into conspiracies, affrays, and murders, and overseeing measures of bread and ale. It may reach those outside the manor’s homage. Smith traces the name from the Saxon lant (law) and “day.”

Chapter 19 — Of the proceedings in causes criminal; justices of the peace. Before criminal procedure can be understood, Smith explains three key officers. Justices of the Peace are chosen from the nobility and gentry of the shire and from learned lawyers; they hold commission at the Prince’s pleasure. Numbers grew from four to eight and now commonly reach thirty to forty per shire. A subset (the “Quorum”) must be present for certain acts. At quarter sessions (four times yearly) they inquire into felonies, riots, vagabonds, and misdemeanors. Each year or alternate year the Privy Council sends articles from penal laws to the Justices for local enforcement — a mechanism Smith praises as “the most benefitte that can be devised for the common wealth of Englande,” if honestly executed.

Chapter 20 — Of hue and cry. Every Englishman has a duty to join the hue and cry when theft or murder is reported. Constables raise the parish; the pursuit goes from parish to parish by chain. The parish that fails to pursue pays the victim’s damages to the king as a fine. Suspects are bound by recognizance to appear at the gaol delivery; witnesses are similarly bound to give evidence.

Chapter 21 — Of the coroner. If any person dies violently and the murderer is unknown, the body may not be buried before the coroner views it. The coroner — a gentleman learned in law — empanels a jury from bystanders and inhabitants, who may range at large and return a verdict after twenty to thirty days as the evidence allows. The verdict is given openly in public, in the street. Smith argues the title derives from the Crown’s interest in every violent death of a subject — the Prince’s strength “doth stande and consist in the force of his people.”

Chapter 22 — Of constables. Originally custodes pacis with authority closer to that now held by Justices of Peace; today they are primarily the executors of the Justices’ commands. They may command the peace in affrays, pursue suspects, enter houses to search, and hold men in the stocks until a Justice examines them. Made at leet courts and called headboroughs or tithingmen in different localities, they are commonly artisans or labourers of limited education and authority.

Chapter 23 — Of sessions of gaole delivery and definitive criminal proceedings. Twice yearly — in Lent and summer vacation — the Prince commissions judges from Westminster Hall to circuit the shires and deliver the gaols. The full machinery of justice is assembled in each town: judges, Justices of the Peace, the sheriff with his under-sheriff, and five inquests of twenty-four yeomen each. Prisoners are called by name and arraigned. The arraignment formula is exact: “Thou art endicted of such a felonie… What sayest thou, art thou guiltie or not guiltie?” If the prisoner refuses to answer, he is judged “mute by contumacy” and condemned to peine forte et dure — pressed to death under stones and weights. Some strong men chose this death to preserve their heirs from the forfeiture of goods that a felony conviction would bring. If the prisoner pleads not guilty, the formula is “I will be tryed by God and the Countrie” — and the clerk replies that the twelve honest men present are his country. Evidence is given publicly; the judge summarises; the jury retires; the verdict — “guilty” or “not guilty” — is final.

Chapter 24 — Certain orders peculiar to England touching punishment. For felony, manslaughter, robbery, murder, and rape — hanging until dead. Nothing more: no heading, dismembering, breaking on the wheel, impaling, or other forms of bodily torture used on the continent. Judges cannot aggravate or mitigate punishment; only the Prince and Privy Council can, and they rarely do. Special cases: a wife who kills her husband is burned alive (petit treason); a servant who kills his master is drawn on a hurdle (petit treason); a poisoner is boiled (by Henry VIII’s act). Then Smith’s most quoted passage: “Torment or question which is used by the order of the civill lawe and custome of other countreis to put a malefactor to excessive paine, to make him confesse of him selfe, or of his felowes or complices, is not used in England, it is taken for servile.” The argument against torture is both moral and practical: a tormented man will confess to anything, even to having killed his own father; the twelve-man verdict is unaffected by confession; and “the nature of English men is to neglect death, to abide no torment.” An ancient law makes it a felony for a gaoler to torture a prisoner. England achieves “as few murthers committed as any where” without it.

Chapter 25 — Of treason and the trial of the higher nobility. Treason follows the same jury procedure as felony, but the punishment is worse: hanged, drawn down alive while still breathing, bowels removed and burned before the face, then beheaded and quartered, the parts set up in diverse places. A duke, marquess, or other baron of Parliament charged with treason is tried by his peers — the Lords of Parliament rather than a yeoman jury — each giving his vote separately, beginning from the youngest lord. A single Lord is appointed Constable of England for the day; at the end of judgment he breaks his staff of office and abdicates the role.

Book III — Of Appeals and Their Absence in England

Chapter 1 — Of what in other countries is called appeal or provocation. In England there is no appeal from a jury verdict of guilty or not guilty, unlike civil law countries where sentences may be challenged in higher courts. If the judges think the jury went against the evidence, the judge may “reprive” (remand) the prisoner and petition the Prince for a pardon — but no formal appeal mechanism exists. Smith presents this not as a deficiency but as a strength: the finality of the twelve-man verdict is precisely what gives it authority.

Significance for the 16th Century England Wiki

De Republica Anglorum is the single most important primary source for understanding how Elizabethan governance actually worked from the inside. It is valuable for:

  • Parliamentary sovereignty: Smith’s articulation of Parliament’s absolute authority — the source from which all later constitutional arguments derive
  • Social hierarchy: the division into four sorts, with precise income qualifications and the mechanics of social mobility through the gentry
  • The jury system: the only detailed contemporary description of how twelve-man verdicts were reached in both civil and criminal cases
  • Torture’s absence: Smith’s explicit prohibition and reasoned argument against it — a primary source for claims about English criminal justice’s distinctiveness
  • The Chancery’s role: how equity supplemented the common law’s rigidity
  • Justices of the Peace: the mechanism by which the Privy Council’s commands were executed across the shires
  • The constitution as mixed: Smith’s synthesis of monarchy, aristocracy, and democracy in England’s actual institutions
