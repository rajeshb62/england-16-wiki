John Sugden, Sir Francis Drake. Random House, 2012. ~1.25 MB, 23,038 lines extracted text. The standard modern biography of Drake, based on extensive archival research across English and Spanish sources including the Conway Papers (Cambridge), the Public Record Office, and Spanish colonial documents printed in I. A. Wright’s English Voyages to the Caribbean.

Comprehensive life from Drake’s origins in Devon through the Hawkins slaving voyages, his private war against Spain in the Caribbean, the circumnavigation (1577–1580), the knighthood, the raid on Cádiz (“singeing the King of Spain’s beard”), the Armada campaign of 1588, and the final ill-fated Caribbean expedition where he died in January 1596. Corrects the two dominant academic traditions that preceded it: the Corbett school (Drake as founder of naval empire) and the revisionist school (Drake as mere filibuster). Sugden presents Drake as an unusually complex social climber — privateer, civic leader, MP, property magnate — who rose from near-poverty to knighthood in a society built on inherited status. ^[source: sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

Sugden’s preface states his purpose plainly: to rescue Drake from “the extremes of the prevailing interpretations” and to write a rounded biography rather than “the conventional collection of voyages.” His use of Spanish sources gives the biography its distinctive double vision — we see every English raid through Spanish eyes too, and Philip II emerges as almost an equal protagonist. ^[source: sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

Contents — Chapter-by-Chapter

Preface (lines 180–313)

Sugden opens with Pope Sixtus V’s rueful admiration — “Just look at Drake! Who is he? What forces has he? And yet he burned twenty-five of the King’s ships at Gibraltar…” — and with the simple fact that Drake’s name, four centuries later, remains a household word that has inspired Montgomery, Churchill, and Thatcher. He surveys the two academic traditions he is writing against (Corbett’s naval empire thesis vs. the revisionist privateer thesis), explains his use of English and Spanish sources together, and announces that his subject is not just the sea raider but also the social climber: Drake the businessman, property owner, parliamentarian and magistrate. ^[source: sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

Ch 1: ‘By God’s Faith!’ (lines 315–840) — Origins in Devon

Drake’s family was long settled near Tavistock on the edge of Dartmoor. The dissolution of the monasteries (1539) is the first historical context: the Tavistock abbey lands had passed to the Russell family, and Francis Drake’s father, Edmund, was a Protestant lay reader of the new dispensation, farming land once owned by monks. Edmund’s religion was militant: when the Prayer Book rebellion of 1549 swept Devon in the Catholic cause, Edmund fled east with his family. He eventually became a lay minister serving sailors at Chatham — and it was among the sailors and their ships at Chatham that young Francis first learned the sea.

Drake’s apprenticeship was with the Hawkins family of Plymouth. John Hawkins was the dominant figure in Devon’s maritime world, and his family’s connections to the Drakes were close. Francis served first on small coastal and Channel vessels, learning the trade of a mariner, before becoming captain of a small bark on a trade voyage to the Bay of Biscay. He was still a young man, barely out of his teens, when Hawkins chose him for the third slaving voyage in 1567. Sugden is careful about Drake’s age — the records are uncertain — but places his birth probably around 1540–1545. ^[source: sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

Ch 2: Beyond the Line (lines 841–1459) — Spain’s claim to the Americas and Philip II

Sugden suspends Drake’s story to give a full portrait of his adversary. Philip II — who had been briefly King of England as Mary I’s consort — is presented as the central fixed point around which the entire book orbits. Philip is almost entirely unlike Drake: reclusive, indecisive, agonizing, ruled by papers and self-doubt, yet capable of “almost pathological resolution” when committed. The contrast is structural: Drake bold, gregarious, decisive, forward-pressing; Philip cautious, secretive, reactive, desk-bound. Both men understand themselves as instruments of God; both understand the war as religious as much as political.

The chapter explains the Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) which divided the non-Christian world between Spain and Portugal, and the Spanish doctrine of “beyond the line” — that violence committed in the Americas did not count as war in Europe, allowing Philip to attack English ships in the Caribbean while remaining nominally at peace with Elizabeth. This legal fiction governs Drake’s entire early career: his raids are technically piracy, not warfare, because England does not recognize Spain’s monopoly claim. ^[source: sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

Ch 3: The Troublesome Voyage of John Hawkins (lines 1460–2354) — The Slaving Voyages and San Juan de Ulúa (1568)

The pivot of Drake’s life. Hawkins fitted out six ships at Plymouth in September 1567 — including two of the queen’s warships, the Jesus of Lubeck (flagship) and the Minion — for what was the fourth Hawkins slaving voyage to West Africa and the Spanish Main. Drake commanded the small bark Judith. Sugden is unflinching about the nature of the enterprise: raiding Portuguese vessels for enslaved Africans and selling them illegally to Spanish colonists is described as it was, piracy and human trafficking, dressed up in extreme Protestant piety (the Jesus’s quartermaster was “a notorious Lutheran, the greatest that existed in England,” who held compulsory services and preached against the Pope with a rope’s end at hand).

The voyage ends in disaster at San Juan de Ulúa (Mexico), where a Spanish flota arrived while the English were refitting in the harbour. The new Viceroy, Don Martin Enriquez, negotiated a truce — and then attacked. The English lost four of their six ships. Hawkins escaped on the Minion with overcrowded, starving survivors; Drake escaped on the Judith and — controversially — did not return for Hawkins in the morning. Hawkins held this desertion against Drake for years (“that bark, the same night, forsook us in our great misery”). Sugden gives Drake the benefit of the doubt: the offshore winds were dangerous, the Judith overcrowded, and the Atlantic crossing unknown territory for Drake. But he notes that Drake’s “resolution, for the first time, failed him.”

The aftermath: the men left behind faced the Inquisition. Sugden names them, one by one — William Orlando who died in Seville dungeons, Robert Barrett of Saltash burned at an auto da fe, Michael Morgan tortured and sentenced to the galleys — making the human cost of the slaving voyages vivid. The chapter ends with Drake, returning to Plymouth after four months at sea, having made his decision: “Francis Drake declared war upon the King of Spain.” ^[source: sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

Ch 4: Drake’s War (lines 2355–3210) — Building the Vendetta, 1569–1572

Drake returned to an England where diplomatic calculation worked against him. Elizabeth and Philip were moving toward the Convention of Bristol (1574), which required her to discourage her corsairs. Drake was advised to keep a low profile, and for two years after his return nothing is known of his activities. When he reappears it is on the margins of the dismal English colonization project in Ireland — he served briefly under Walter Devereux, Earl of Essex, providing naval support for what Sugden treats as a squalid affair that tells us little about Drake’s abilities but something about his willingness to do ugly work for powerful patrons.

The chapter develops the international backdrop: France in civil war, Spain consolidating in the Netherlands under the Duke of Alba, the gradual shift of English attention from France to Spain as the primary threat. Drake’s personal decision after San Juan de Ulúa aligns with this national reorientation. His private war is not politically eccentric — it anticipates what England’s strategic position will require. ^[source: sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

Ch 5: ‘The Mouth of the Treasure of the World’ (lines 3211–3883) — Nombre de Dios and the Panama Silver Train (1572–1573)

Drake’s first independent command of any significance. He left Plymouth on Whit Sunday 1572 with seventy-three volunteers in two small ships, the Pasco and the Swan, with three prefabricated pinnaces stored in pieces and provisions for a year. He was aiming at Nombre de Dios, the Atlantic port where silver from the Potosí mines was gathered for shipment to Spain — “the mouth of the treasure of the world,” as Drake called it.

The raid on Nombre de Dios: Drake’s assault force of seventy-three men attacked a town of several hundred. Drake himself was shot in the leg and refused to let his men know he was wounded, continuing to direct the attack until he fainted from blood loss and the English withdrew. They had reached the treasure house; they had not been able to carry it away. Sugden’s account is detailed on tactics — drums and trumpets, six shields and twelve pikes, the timing of the assault at 2–3 a.m.

The Panama silver train: having allied with the cimarrones (escaped African slaves who lived as guerrillas in the isthmus jungle and were at permanent war with Spain), Drake marched forty-eight men across the isthmus of Panama to intercept the mule-trains carrying silver between Panama City and Venta Cruces. He climbed a great tree that allowed him to see both the Atlantic and the Pacific simultaneously — “the first Englishman, or at least the first that we have exact notice of, that saw that sea.” He returned to England in August 1573 with enough plunder to make him wealthy, and a reputation across the West Country. ^[source: sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

Ch 6: ‘Despite … All the Spaniards in the Indies’ (lines 3884–4569) — Consolidating the Caribbean Campaign

The cimarrone alliance is developed at length: Drake’s relationship with Pedro and the other cimarron leaders is the most interesting moral dimension of the Caribbean campaigns. The cimarrones had every reason to hate the Spanish — Sugden quotes their account of how their town had been sacked, men, women and children killed or captured. Drake treated them with genuine respect and they trusted him. His fleet of pinnaces and alliances with displaced, stateless people created a mode of warfare the Spaniards could not easily counter: light, fast, intelligence-driven, operating from hidden harbours and forest paths.

The chapter also follows the fate of Drake’s brothers. His brother Joseph died of fever in Panama. His brother John was killed in a skirmish. By the time Drake sailed home he was one of the only survivors of his original company. Sugden uses this to establish something about Drake’s psychological formation: he was a man acquainted with large and repeated personal loss who learned to press forward regardless. ^[source: sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

Ch 7: Services Ashore and Afloat (lines 4570–5094) — Ireland and the Political Interlude, 1573–1577

Drake’s triumphant return to Plymouth coincided with the worst possible diplomatic moment: Elizabeth was moving toward accommodation with Spain. The most dangerous corsair on the ocean had to disappear for a while. The chapter covers his brief involvement in Irish operations under Walter Devereux, Earl of Essex — Sugden notes the ugly character of the Irish campaign without dwelling on it. He is more interested in what the interlude reveals about Drake’s social ambitions: his cultivation of Essex, and through Essex of the Devereaux family connection, anticipates his later cultivation of Leicester and finally of Essex’s son as patrons. Drake understood from early in his career that a man of his birth needed powerful sponsors, and he worked systematically to find them. ^[source: sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

Ch 8: The South Seas Project (lines 5095–5806) — Planning the Circumnavigation

Sugden reconstructs the planning of the circumnavigation from fragmentary evidence, since the backers required secrecy. The investors included Leicester, Walsingham, Hatton, and almost certainly the queen herself, though Elizabeth maintained deniability. The original plan, carried in documents never intended for Spanish eyes, was probably to raid the Pacific coast of Spanish America — not specifically to circumnavigate. The cover story told to most of the crew was that they were bound for Alexandria.

The Doughty problem begins here. Thomas Doughty was a gentleman, a friend of Drake’s, and a servant of Hatton. He joined the expedition as a senior figure, but from the beginning showed a disposition to undermine Drake’s authority with hints that the backers expected Doughty to share the command. Sugden traces this carefully, using the depositions of the carpenter Edward Bright, who heard Doughty’s private boasts at Plymouth. Doughty is presented not as a villain but as a man whose expectations of shared authority in a venture that required absolute command were structurally incompatible with Drake’s leadership style. ^[source: sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

Ch 9: The Atlantic Voyage (lines 5807–6591) — Departure 1577; Magellan Strait; Doughty’s Execution

The Pelican (later renamed the Golden Hind), Elizabeth, Marigold, Swan, and Benedict left Plymouth on 13 December 1577. The seizure of the Portuguese pilot Nuño da Silva in the Cape Verde Islands — taken with his ship, his charts, and his knowledge of South American waters — is treated as necessary piracy: Drake needed the charts and the expertise. The Mary (Drake’s prize, renamed after his wife) becomes the site of the Doughty problem’s first open flare: Doughty was appointed captain of the prize and immediately began creating trouble with the men.

Doughty’s execution at Port St Julian (Patagonia): Drake convened a jury of forty men, was examined himself, presented evidence from Bright and others of Doughty’s private undermining, and had Doughty tried, found guilty, and beheaded. He communed with him before the end and dined with him afterward — “we are yet friends,” Drake told him. Sugden’s treatment is measured: Doughty was probably guilty of something, possibly of sharing information about the voyage with Burghley, who was not one of the backers and would have opposed it; but whether the offence merited death under any proper legal standard is another matter. Drake had no royal commission and his authority to execute a gentleman was extremely doubtful. He knew he would one day have to answer for it — hence the renaming of the Pelican as the Golden Hind, honouring Hatton (Doughty’s patron) with his family’s emblem.

The Magellan Strait passage: fourteen days, the fastest of the century (Magellan took thirty-seven days). Drake emerges into the Pacific to face an immediate catastrophe — the worst storm the fleet had ever encountered drove them south for weeks, destroying the Marigold with all twenty-nine aboard and separating the Elizabeth (whose captain, Winter, eventually turned back to England). Drake was left with one ship in the South Pacific. ^[source: sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

Ch 10: War Comes to the Pacific (lines 6592–7536) — Raiding the Pacific Coast of Spanish America

Drake’s solo raid on the entirely undefended Pacific coast — the Spanish had never imagined anyone could reach it from the west. He struck north along the Chilean and Peruvian coast, taking ship after ship, none of them armed, because the Spanish had never needed to arm ships in what they regarded as a private sea. The climax was the capture of the Nuestra Señora de la Concepción, the great treasure galleon nicknamed “the Cacafuego” by her crew — she carried twenty-six tonnes of silver, thirteen chests of silver coins, eighty pounds of gold, and a cargo of jewels and precious stones. Drake spent six days transferring the cargo to the Golden Hind.

The chapter covers the pursuit mounted by the Viceroy of Peru — Pedro Sarmiento de Gamboa sent south to the Magellan Strait to intercept Drake, too late. Philip II in Madrid is shown receiving the reports of the Pacific raid with mounting consternation and beginning to endorse papers with “to be put with what relates to Drake.” Sugden notes that the Pacific raid, though it yielded treasure worth perhaps £140,000 in English money, was strategically more important as a demonstration: the entire system of Spanish imperial security was exposed as resting on the assumption that no one could reach the Pacific from Europe. ^[source: sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

Ch 11: The World Encompassed (lines 7537–8269) — The Route Home; California; the Spice Islands; Return 1580

Drake faced three possible routes home: back through the Magellan Strait (too dangerous, and the Spaniards were waiting); the hypothetical North-west Passage; or across the Pacific. He attempted the North-west Passage first, reaching 48° north along the California coast — further north than any European had previously explored in the Pacific — before cold, fog and the failure to find any strait entrance persuaded him to give up. He careened the Golden Hind in a quiet harbour in northern California (its exact location still disputed between Drake’s Bay, Bolinas Bay, and the San Francisco Bay entrance) and made contact with the coastal Miwok people, who performed ceremonies of welcome that the English interpreted as an offer of sovereignty and Drake claimed the country for Elizabeth as “Nova Albion.”

The trans-Pacific crossing: the Spice Islands (Moluccas), where the Sultan of Ternate — engaged in his own war against the Portuguese — made Drake welcome and arranged the loading of six tonnes of cloves. A near-disaster on a reef that held the Golden Hind fast for twenty hours. The return around the Cape of Good Hope and up the Atlantic to England, arriving at Plymouth on 26 September 1580 — nearly three years after departure. The investors’ shares of the treasure were enormous: the queen’s share alone may have been worth £160,000. ^[source: sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

Ch 12: The Golden Knight (lines 8270–8986) — Elizabeth’s Reception; Knighthood on the Golden Hind; Diplomatic Fallout

Philip II had filed a formal claim for restitution. Elizabeth listened to it, and then knighted Drake on the quarterdeck of the Golden Hind at Deptford on 4 April 1581, using a gilded sword — and making clear that the treasure would not be returned. The knighthood was the central fact of Drake’s social career: it transformed him from a corsair of uncertain legal status into Sir Francis Drake, with the full machinery of honour and respectability behind him.

The chapter is notably attentive to Drake’s anxiety about his coat of arms. His initial response to knighthood was to deny his humble origins and claim that his family “had borne arms of long time.” He borrowed a dragon crest from the Drakes of Ashe in Devon (a different family), fussed endlessly about the design, and only in the last years of his life settled into accepting the crest as originally granted by the queen — the globe with the Golden Hind riding it and a hand directing from the clouds. Sugden reads this as the social climber’s characteristic insecurity: Drake was not yet comfortable inside his own new skin.

He also bought Buckland Abbey (the converted Cistercian monastery four miles north of Plymouth) from Richard Grenville — through an intermediary, because Grenville would not sell to him directly — spending £3,400 to establish his country seat. ^[source: sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

Ch 13: ‘What Has Become of Drake?’ (lines 8987–10043) — The Years of Relative Inactivity, 1581–1584

A quieter chapter covering Drake’s adjustment to his new social position — and the years in which Elizabeth, hoping to avoid provoking Philip, kept her most dangerous instrument sheathed. Drake was elected mayor of Plymouth (1581–82), served as Member of Parliament, worked on Plymouth’s water supply, and continued to cultivate his network of patrons (Leicester above all, then Walsingham). He married again after the death of his first wife Mary. He was also involved in planning a Moluccas expedition under Edward Fenton that came to nothing.

Sugden uses this period to develop Philip’s counter-moves: the fortification of the Strait of Magellan (a settlement attempt under Sarmiento that ended in disaster and starvation — the survivors were found years later by Thomas Cavendish and called “Port Famine”). Philip was also buying Portugal’s throne (1580), annexing its empire, and beginning to think seriously about the enterprise of England. The chapter ends with Drake’s 1585 commission: Elizabeth had finally resolved on open support for the Dutch rebels and was prepared to use her most effective weapon. ^[source: sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

Ch 14: Playing the Dragon (lines 10044–10895) — The 1585 Caribbean Expedition; Sacking Cartagena and San Domingo

Drake’s largest Caribbean expedition: 25 ships and 2,300 men, a semi-official operation with the queen investing ships and Leicester investing money. The expedition sacked Santiago in the Cape Verde Islands (no treasure), crossed the Atlantic to Hispaniola, and took Santo Domingo — the oldest city in the Americas and the administrative capital of Spanish Caribbean — by landing soldiers at night on the beach west of the city and marching around the defences. The sack lasted a month; the Spanish governor negotiated a ransom (Drake’s demolition squads were systematically destroying one building a day until the ransom was paid). Net proceeds: about 25,000 ducats, far less than hoped.

Cartagena followed — the principal city of the Spanish Main, forewarned and better defended. Drake landed soldiers at the Caleta, the narrow neck of land forming the harbour entrance, after a diversionary attack by Frobisher’s ships on the harbour chain. The infantry assault on the Caleta met Spanish galleys, lance, and arquebuses; the men panicked initially but recovered. Cartagena fell after hard fighting. Again, a ransom negotiation rather than plunder — 107,000 ducats, less than a third of what Drake demanded. He demolished the city building by building for weeks.

The expedition failed financially but succeeded militarily: it demonstrated to Philip that Spain’s Caribbean empire was fundamentally indefensible against a large English force. In Madrid, Philip’s ambassador wrote: “Drake is so highly valued by the queen and these people that we must endeavour to get rid of him by other means.” The chapter ends with Drake touching at the fledgling Roanoke Colony in Virginia on the return — taking off the surviving colonists (including Ralph Lane) and effectively ending the first Roanoke settlement. ^[source: sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

Ch 15: ‘A Fearful Man to the King of Spain!’ (lines 10896–11532) — The Cádiz Raid and Cape St Vincent (1587)

Philip II began assembling his Armada in late 1586. Elizabeth authorized Drake to “impeach” the Spanish preparations — a deliberately ambiguous commission that he used to the fullest extent possible. He sailed for Cádiz in April 1587 with four queen’s ships and twenty-three private vessels and arrived to find thirty-seven Spanish ships in the harbour, several of them large, loading provisions and stores for the Armada.

Drake attacked immediately, over the protests of his vice-admiral (William Borough, who wanted a formal council of war first). He spent thirty-six hours in the harbour, sinking, burning, or capturing thirty-seven ships and enormous quantities of stores — “singeing the King of Spain’s beard.” Borough wrote a formal protest against Drake’s high-handedness, and Drake had him court-martialled on the return. Elizabeth quietly dropped the charges against Borough — she was unimpressed by Drake’s treatment of a subordinate — but kept Drake in command.

Cape St Vincent: Drake seized the cape as a base, ravaged coastal shipping, and destroyed the barrel staves stored in the area. The stave destruction had strategic significance: without seasoned barrel staves, the Armada’s provisions would spoil in the casks cut from green wood. This may have contributed to the fleet’s provisioning problems in 1588. Drake’s own estimate of the raid’s importance was grandiose — he wrote to Walsingham that it would delay the Armada by a year. He was roughly right. ^[source: sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

Ch 16: ‘Stop Him Now, and Stop Him Ever!’ (lines 11533–12352) — Spanish Armada Preparations; Mary Queen of Scots

Mary Queen of Scots was executed at Fotheringay in February 1587, removing Philip’s last reservation about the enterprise of England: while Mary lived he feared that overthrowing Elizabeth would benefit France. With Mary dead, Philip himself came forward as the legitimate Catholic heir. His leading admiral, Santa Cruz, died in February 1588 and was replaced by Medina Sidonia — who famously protested his own appointment in terms of comic self-deprecation (“he would certainly make a mess of it”) before accepting.

Sugden gives Elizabeth’s strategic hesitations full weight. She understood that Spain was the only counterweight to France; she had already spent most of her reserves on the Netherlands and on Drake; she feared war’s costs. Her refusal to strike preemptively was not cowardice but a calculation about sustainability. Drake’s view — that only an aggressive offensive could save England — and Elizabeth’s view — that she must husband her resources — represent a genuine strategic debate, not simply boldness versus timidity. ^[source: sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

Ch 17: ‘The Sooner We Are Gone, the Better’ (lines 12353–13307) — English Preparations; Drake as Vice-Admiral

Howard as Lord Admiral, Drake as Vice-Admiral: the joint command structure. Sugden is good on the English fleet’s composition and on the debate about strategy — Drake wanted to take the fleet to Spain to intercept the Armada before it left port; Howard was more cautious; Elizabeth at one point ordered them back to their home ports. The wait at Plymouth while the wind held them bottled in and the Armada approached was agonizing.

The Armada itself is described from the Spanish side: 125 ships, 19,000 soldiers and 8,000 sailors, massed prayers and confessions, their standard inscribed “Arise, O Lord, and vindicate thy cause.” Medina Sidonia’s plan (link with Parma’s army in the Netherlands for an invasion of Kent) is described alongside its fatal assumption: that flyboats could convey Parma’s men from the shallow Flemish ports to the Armada’s deep-draft ships without being destroyed by the Dutch. ^[source: sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

Ch 18: ‘It Was Impossible to Come to Hand-Stroke with Them’ (lines 13308–14292) — The Armada Channel Battles

Sugden opens by demolishing the bowling-on-Plymouth-Hoe story: it dates from a Dutch pamphlet of 1624, was originally a Spanish boast about the Armada’s approach rather than an English anecdote about Drake’s sangfroid, and was not attributed to Drake until 1736. “On such flimsy foundations rests the most famous of all yarns about Sir Francis Drake.”

The seven days of Channel fighting: the English could not close with the Armada (which kept tight defensive formation) and the Armada could not detach transports to seize a port (Medina Sidonia’s orders forbade unnecessary engagements until the junction with Parma). The tactical problem for both sides is described precisely — the Spanish formation was too strong to break, the English guns too light to penetrate Spanish hulls at long range. One notable incident: Drake extinguished the lantern that his ships were following during a night pursuit and went off to capture a straggling Spanish ship for himself, leaving Howard and the rest of the fleet in darkness and confusion. He later said his lantern had failed. Sugden treats this as characteristic — Drake’s instinct for personal profit operating even during a fleet action. ^[source: sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

Ch 19: ‘God Hath Given Us So Good a Day’ (lines 14293–14870) — The Fireships at Gravelines; Spanish Flight

The Armada anchored off Calais on 27 July 1588, waiting for Parma. Howard and Drake sent eight fireships into the anchorage at midnight. No ship was actually burned — the Spanish cut their anchor cables and scattered into the North Sea — but the panic had destroyed the Armada’s tight defensive formation, and it was never re-formed. Drake offered his own ship, the Thomas Drake, as one of the fireships.

The Battle of Gravelines (29 July): the only full day of close action, the English having finally exhausted their ammunition and powder supply in the process of bringing the Armada to near-destruction. The Rosario (captured earlier by Drake — his personal trophy), the San Salvador, and eight other Spanish ships were wrecked or sunk. The Armada fled into the North Sea without the ability to return south, pursued by Howard and Drake as far as the Firth of Forth before the English turned back for lack of provisions.

Philip II received the news in his monastery at the Sierra de Guadarrama: “I hope that God has not permitted so much evil, for everything has been done for His service.” His annotated dispatches trace his reluctance to accept the magnitude of the disaster. ^[source: sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

Ch 20: ‘This Will Cause an Evil Summer’ (lines 14871–16106) — The Portugal Expedition (1589); Failure and Disgrace

The counter-armada of 1589 is Drake’s most important failure and the one Sugden analyses most critically. The plan had three objectives: destroy Armada survivors in Spanish ports; take Lisbon for Dom Antonio (Portuguese pretender); seize the Azores. These were sequenced badly and the vital first objective (destroying ships at Santander) was missed — Drake and Norris went south to Corunna instead, wasting time in a siege that achieved nothing militarily. By the time they reached Lisbon the army was weakened by disease and Dom Antonio’s promised Portuguese uprising failed to materialize. The Azores attempt was abandoned for lack of provisions.

Sugden’s verdict: if Lisbon had been dropped from the plan, and Drake had been sent simply to destroy the Armada survivors and then take the Azores, the result could have been decisive. The Azores as a base would have allowed England to intercept the treasure flotas. But Elizabeth had been persuaded by Dom Antonio’s fantasies, and Drake had allowed himself to be carried along. “He had always reached for the stars, realized ambitions that some might have considered chimerical, and probably it was only a matter of time before he overreached himself. That time had finally come.” ^[source: sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

Ch 21: Last Years Ashore (lines 16107–17153) — Plymouth; Parliament; Rehabilitation, 1589–1595

Contrary to the common picture of disgrace and idleness, Sugden argues that these were among Drake’s busiest years: Member of Parliament (Plymouth), acting Deputy Lord Lieutenant for Devon, military engineer (Plymouth’s defences), entrepreneur, prize commissioner, and civic leader. His most lasting legacy from this period may be the Plymouth water supply — the leat he engineered to bring fresh water from Dartmoor to Plymouth, sixteen miles of open channel, completed in 1591.

The broader war continued at low intensity: privateering fleets (the Lanzarote expedition, Cumberland’s Atlantic raids), Hawkins’s proposed blockade of the Azores trade routes that was never fully implemented. Drake’s name retained its terror value — Spanish coastal towns would panic at any rumour of his approach. Philip was reviving his navy and planning further armadas; England’s strategic position remained precarious. ^[source: sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

Ch 22: ‘Into a Waste and Desert Wilderness’ (lines 17154–17835) — The Final Caribbean Expedition; Death, January 1596

The last voyage was compromised from the start by divided command: Drake and Sir John Hawkins received joint commissions with equal authority. Sugden quotes Thomas Maynarde’s portrait of the two old admirals as they embarked: Drake still full of spirit and fit “to conduct forces and discreetly to govern,” but “self-willed and peremptory”; Hawkins “old and wary, entering into matters with so leaden a foot that the other’s meat would be eaten before his spit could come to the fire.” The two commanders maintained effectively separate squadrons.

Puerto Rico was the first objective. Drake failed: the Spanish had been alerted by the capture of a straggler that carried the fleet’s plans, and San Juan was now fortified. Hawkins died at Puerto Rico on 12 November 1595. Drake pressed south to the Main, took Río de la Hacha, sacked Santa Marta, took Nombre de Dios — and then sent an army under Baskerville across the isthmus to attack Panama. The army was turned back by a Spanish force, the first time Drake’s Panama strategy had failed. Drake’s health collapsed: dysentery, possibly compounded by despair. He died aboard the Defiance off Buena Ventura (Panama) on 28 January 1596, and was buried at sea in a lead coffin west of Puerto Bello. ^[source: sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

Ch 23: Afterword (lines 17836–18500) — Drake’s Legacy and Philip’s Death

Baskerville brought the fleet home. FitzGeffrey wrote a full-length epic. English seamen followed Drake’s wake — Puerto Rico, Puerto de Caballos, Puerto Bello — for decades. Captain William Parker, taking Puerto Bello in 1601, anchored east of the castle of Saint Philip “under the rock where Sir Francis Drake, his coffin, was thrown overboard,” in deliberate salute.

Philip II’s response to the news of Drake’s death: “It is good news, and now I will get well.” Lope de Vega wrote La Dragontea (1597) celebrating Drake’s death as a defeat of the Devil. A Spanish soldier who had fought against Drake, Don Alonso de Sotomayor, gave him this epitaph: “one of the most famous men of his profession that have existed in the world, very courteous and honourable with those who surrendered, of great humanity and gentleness, virtues which must be praised even in an enemy.”

Philip himself died in September 1598, his empire intact but his war unresolved. The conflict wound up by the Treaty of London in 1604. Sugden’s final judgement: the profits of privateering, in experience and treasure, constituted England’s principal legacy from the later war years — not decisive naval victory but the accumulation of maritime skill that would eventually generate genuine sea power. ^[source: sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]
