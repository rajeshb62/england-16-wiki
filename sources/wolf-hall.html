Hilary Mantel, Wolf Hall (The Rise and Fall of Thomas Cromwell, Vol. 1). Fourth Estate, 2009. ~1.25 MB, 25,584 lines extracted text. Booker Prize-winning historical novel narrated in close third person from Thomas Cromwell’s point of view.

Covers 1500–1535: Cromwell’s origins in Putney as a blacksmith’s son, his formation in Europe, his rise as lawyer and fixer under Cardinal Wolsey, and his ascent through Henry VIII’s court after Wolsey’s fall. Ends as Cromwell has consolidated power and the court moves to Wolf Hall to visit the Seymours. ^[source: wolf_hall_raw.txt]

Quality Assessment

Clean born-digital epub, fully readable throughout. Close-third-person narration uses present tense and refers to Cromwell throughout as “he,” which requires care when extracting quotations — sentences beginning “he” always mean Cromwell. Author’s Note distinguishes historical fact from fiction. ^[source: wolf_hall_raw.txt]

Contents — Chapter-by-Chapter

Part One

Ch I — Across the Narrow Sea. Putney, 1500 (line 329)

Opens with Cromwell as a boy on the cobblestones of his father’s yard, beaten by Walter Cromwell, a blacksmith and brewer. His sister Kat and her husband Morgan Williams take him in. The chapter establishes his origins: the Putney household’s poverty and violence, the riverbank setting, his physical toughness and early capability at figures and addition (he has taught Kat to write her name). He is described by his brother-in-law as a man who “could cripple the brute in a fair fight” if he chose. The chapter ends with the implied decision to leave. ^[source: wolf_hall_raw.txt]

Ch II — Paternity. 1527 (line 849)

1527: Cromwell is established as Wolsey’s man of business, returning from a mission to Yorkshire where monks have threatened to kill him over the cardinal’s monastic amalgamation project. He meets Wolsey late at night; Stephen Gardiner, the cardinal’s secretary, is present as Cromwell arrives, an antagonism established without ceremony. Wolsey relates that Henry wants a divorce — the emerald ring story is in the city: “not for the queen.” Cromwell describes his continental formation obliquely, through an anecdote about surviving an assassination attempt: the Italian estoc, the knife pushed up under the ribs. The chapter covers Wolsey’s plan to redirect dissolved monasteries’ revenues into his two colleges. ^[source: wolf_hall_raw.txt]

Ch III — At Austin Friars. 1527 (line 1482)

Cromwell’s household at Austin Friars: his wife Liz, their son Gregory at Cambridge, the multilingual clerks and merchants who flow through, the apprentice girls, the dog Bella. Liz reports city gossip — the king’s intentions are known, and her verdict is that “all women” of England will oppose it: women who have daughters but no sons, women who have lost children. Cromwell lies awake, thinking about what is coming. His European formation is sketched: soldiering with the French at Garigliano, trading in silk in Italy, his languages. He has a Lutheran book from Germany that Liz does not comment on. ^[source: wolf_hall_raw.txt]

Part Two

Ch I — Visitation. 1529 (line 1908)

Wolsey’s fall: Norfolk and Suffolk arrive at York Place and strip it for the king. Room by room, the king’s men catalogue and confiscate everything — gold plate, silver, vestments, tapestries, books, even the ink and quills. Cromwell manages the crisis: he stalls the dukes by arguing they need a written instruction from the king (gaining twenty-four hours), then organises the departure to Esher when the written order arrives the next morning. He watches the Solomon and Sheba tapestries rolled up, the hunting scenes taken down. His silent inventory of what is lost runs through the scene. He prevents the household treasurer from spreading rumours of the Tower. They depart upriver by barge. ^[source: wolf_hall_raw.txt]

Ch II — An Occult History of Britain. 1521–1529 (line 2607)

A retrospective chapter suspended from the main narrative. Opens with the mythological frame: the Albina legend (thirty-three daughters, a murder, a voyage to a foggy island, giants, demons); the Brutus legend (Trojan Brutus defeating Gogmagog, founding New Troy); the Tudor claim through Constantine and Arthur. The chapter then tracks the Boleyn affair from its origins: Anne’s first appearance at court at Christmas 1521 in yellow, dancing in a masque as Perseverance; the Percy affair (Harry Percy courting Anne, Wolsey’s brutal crushing of it in Cromwell’s presence); Thomas Boleyn’s cold diplomatic performance before the cardinal. Cromwell watches from the shadows. The chapter also extends the estoc anecdote: memory of a knife in a dark room, a shape against a wall, learning to see a shadow move before it kills. ^[source: wolf_hall_raw.txt]

Ch III — Make or Mar. All Hallows 1529 (line 6025)

Cromwell in grief at Esher on All Hallows. His wife Liz is recently dead; his daughters Grace and Anne died before her. He holds Liz’s prayer book, seeing her hands on the page and his daughter’s finger-marks. George Cavendish finds him apparently crying. His declaration to Cavendish: he is going to lose everything unless he secures a parliamentary seat, and he has already sent Rafe to Westminster. He then pivots: a plan to make the cardinal’s chaplains pay off the household servants with their own money, because “his servants love my lord more than his priests do.” He tells Cavendish he will “make or mar” before he returns — his own common saying, Cavendish confirms. The Simonides memory palace story follows: Simonides survives the collapse of a banqueting hall and identifies the crushed dead by their positions in his mental seating plan. Cromwell’s way of carrying memory. ^[source: wolf_hall_raw.txt]

Part Three

Ch I — Three-Card Trick. Winter 1529–Spring 1530 (line 6196)

Rafe has secured Cromwell a parliamentary seat at Taunton; the Duke of Norfolk has agreed to patronise him into Parliament, believing Wolsey has buried treasure and that Cromwell knows where it is. Cromwell’s long conversation with Norfolk: frank, almost equal, neither man pretending to more respect than he feels. Norfolk asks if he can shoe a horse, finds out he served with the French, says “wrong side, lad.” Cromwell: “I noticed.” Norfolk agrees informally that invasion of France is futile but says it can’t be said aloud. The forty-four charges against Wolsey are being drafted. Cromwell commutes between Esher (where the cardinal paces and asks Cavendish every hour when Cromwell will arrive) and Westminster, gathering petitions and messages too sensitive to write down. ^[source: wolf_hall_raw.txt]

Ch II — Entirely Beloved Cromwell. Spring–December 1530 (line 7698)

Cromwell now in Henry’s service. He goes to York Place — now being refurbished for Anne — and meets her for the first time. She is small, sharp-tongued, her little dogs rushing at him while she stands with rosemary shreds on her skirts. He lobbies her to restore Wolsey: “he is the only man in England who can obtain for you what you need.” She gives him five minutes. Their exchange establishes the dynamic: Anne abbreviating everything to its essentials (“Cremuel”), Cromwell matching her speed. At the end she says the cardinal failed her once and simply, and Cromwell says “you know it was not simple.” The king has privately called Cromwell “entirely beloved,” which is the chapter’s title and Cromwell’s first evidence of the royal regard. ^[source: wolf_hall_raw.txt]

Ch III — The Dead Complain of Their Burial. Christmastide 1530 (line 10647)

Henry summons Cromwell to Greenwich by barge before dawn. In the king’s chamber, Cranmer is present. Henry has dreamed of his dead brother Arthur, who seemed to reproach him: “you have taken my kingdom, and you have used my wife.” Cranmer offers the standard theological caution against heeding dreams. Cromwell takes the harder, more useful line: if Arthur visited you, it was not to reproach you but to remind you that you are vested with the power of both the living and dead; the dead grip the living (“le mort saisit le vif”), and Arthur’s power passed to you at the moment of his death. Henry must become the king Arthur could not be. Henry accepts this. Cranmer concedes “you may be right.” The exchange marks Cromwell’s entry into the king’s inner circle. ^[source: wolf_hall_raw.txt]

Part Four

Ch I — Arrange Your Face. 1531 (line 11095)

Cromwell at Windsor negotiating with Katherine and Mary — delivering the message that Henry will ride with Anne and that Katherine must go to the More in Hertfordshire. Katherine, forty-six, in her boned bodice, naming exactly what is being done to her: “You and Speaker Audley put your heads together by candlelight.” Mary is in pain, holding the back of her mother’s chair, sweating in the heat. Their exchange on the legislation: “as far as the law of Christ allows” — Mary says “it means nothing”; Cromwell says “it means everything.” Katherine’s final assessment: “The blacksmith makes his own tools.” ^[source: wolf_hall_raw.txt]

Ch II — ‘Alas, What Shall I Do For Love?’ Spring 1532 (line 13159)

Parliament breaking the church. Cromwell drafting the petition against the church courts, suspending annates to Rome, working through the night with Rafe and Wriothesley (Call-Me-Risley). Gardiner’s confrontation with the king — Henry screaming at his own Secretary about episcopal loyalty — ends with Gardiner collapsed against the painted panelling, a handkerchief knotted around his knuckles. Cromwell persuades the king to keep Gardiner rather than sacrifice him; the bill goes in now. Christopher St German, the aged jurist, confirms that if the church cannot reform itself, Parliament may do so. Thomas More’s position hardening invisibly in the background. The sick men in the Commons: Thomas Somer who died after release from the Tower, John Petyt with his iron hoop around his chest. Dr Cranmer is away in Germany seeking scholarly support for the divorce. ^[source: wolf_hall_raw.txt]

Ch III — Early Mass. November 1532 (line 16254)

A short chapter. Calais: Cromwell is told the king has gone to Mass. He pushes through the crowd to reach the church. Coming out, Anne is on the Governor’s arm, expression blank; Henry is large, benign, his glance settling on Cromwell. As Henry leaves the church, he puts on his hat. It is a big hat, a new hat. In that hat, there is a feather. Christophe — a French boy who has been looking for “milord Cremuel” — is acquired as a new household servant, having been beaten at the king’s lodgings for asking after Cromwell. ^[source: wolf_hall_raw.txt]

Part Five

Ch I — Anna Regina. 1533 (line 16294)

Back at Austin Friars, 1533. Cromwell takes in Helen Barre, a destitute woman with two small children, and sets her to work in the household. His coat of arms is being repainted on the wall: Wolsey’s choughs replaced by Cromwell’s lions and roses; a German painter lets Helen’s elder daughter apply a blob of blue. Cromwell describes himself as “always translating” — between languages, between Henry and Anne, managing their volatile dynamic day by day. The chapter encompasses the storms that kept the court in Calais for ten days after the chapter break. The Maid of Kent (Elizabeth Barton) is examined by Cromwell, Audley, Cranmer, and Sir Richard Riche in successive sessions: she treats Cranmer with condescension, tells Riche that God is sending a plague and Henry will be dead in six months. ^[source: wolf_hall_raw.txt]

Ch II — Devil’s Spit. Autumn and winter 1533 (line 18925)

Elizabeth’s birth announced to the assembled lords: the king receives it with perfect control — “Healthy? Then I thank God for his favour to us. Call her Elizabeth. Cancel the jousts.” In private with Cromwell and Cranmer he is desolate. Anne will wish the child away, he says. Then, on the long walk back, he turns himself around by an act of will: striding, expansive, “we are young enough, and next time it will be a boy.” Edward Seymour’s brutal comment — “it hardly matters, does it?” — goes unremarked. Cromwell orders the proclamations corrected: scribes who have written “prince” must squeeze in extra letters. The Maid continues to prophesy; Cromwell pursues the network of priests and monks behind her. ^[source: wolf_hall_raw.txt]

Ch III — A Painter’s Eye. 1534 (line 20583)

Holbein’s portrait of Cromwell arrives at Austin Friars. Cromwell examines it section by section — the hand holding a paper in a loose fist, the smooth skin, the heavy volume (Pacioli’s book on double-entry bookkeeping), the turquoise ring that was Wolsey’s. He stands before his own face and cannot read it. The household’s reactions: Alice says he looks stout; Richard Cromwell quotes Leonardo on curved surfaces and cannon balls; Helen Barre says that is not the expression on his face. Chapuys the imperial ambassador springs forward and back before the painting: “one would be loath to cross you.” When Gregory comes home, Cromwell shows him the portrait alone and tells him his mother always said she hadn’t picked him for his looks. Gregory stands looking at it. “Did you not know?” he says. ^[source: wolf_hall_raw.txt]

Part Six

Ch I — Supremacy. 1534 (line 20700)

Between Christmas and Epiphany, Cromwell is rereading Marsiglio of Padua (1324), whose forty-two propositions include: Christ came into the world not as a ruler but as a subject; Christ did not grant the Pope power over princes; all priests are subjects as Christ left them; the prince governs the bodies of his citizens and derives his power through a legislative body acting on behalf of the people. Cromwell presents these propositions to Henry on a brilliant, bone-chilling morning. Henry says “I never remember the cardinal spoke of this.” Cromwell: “Would you, if you were a cardinal?” The chapter covers the mechanics of compelling More and Fisher to take the oath of supremacy, and the legislative work of making silence a crime. ^[source: wolf_hall_raw.txt]

Ch II — The Map of Christendom. 1534–1535 (line 22672)

England diplomatically isolated, the Pope threatening, France and the Empire hostile. Cromwell managing the European Protestant contacts: correspondence with Schmalkaldic princes, the possibility of alliance. More in the Tower, examined by Cromwell: the problem of More’s silence — his refusal to give a reason for refusing is technically not treason, but Cromwell is working toward the legislation that will make it so. Fisher also imprisoned. The title refers to the redrawing of European politics by the Reformation: England’s new position as a schismatic kingdom. The chapter also follows Cromwell’s cultivation of Cranmer and the network of reformist scholars. ^[source: wolf_hall_raw.txt]

Ch III — To Wolf Hall. July 1535 (line 25098)

More executed. The court moves to Wolf Hall in Wiltshire to visit Edward Seymour and his family. Jane Seymour is present: still, modest, watching. Cromwell watches the king watching her. The novel ends in this house, with the next phase already implicit. ^[source: wolf_hall_raw.txt]
