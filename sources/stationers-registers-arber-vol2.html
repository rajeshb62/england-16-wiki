Edward Arber (ed.), A Transcript of the Registers of the Company of Stationers of London, 1554–1640 A.D. Volume II — Text. Privately printed, London, 1 October 1875. Reprinted New York: Peter Smith, 1950. ~45,409 lines extracted from a zip of 906 OCR’d page files. University of Minnesota Wilson Library copy.

This is the second of five volumes of Arber’s Transcript. Volume II contains the core primary-source record of the Elizabethan book trade at its height: Register B, the Stationers’ Company’s day-by-day register from 1576 onward, organized into four distinct sections — Apprentices, Book Entries (copyright registrations), Freemen, and Fines. Book entries run from 20 July 1576 to 25 June 1595; apprentice, freeman, and fine records run to 2 July 1605. Woven throughout the register sections are major illustrative documents from state papers and Company archives: the early printing patents granted to Seres, Day, and Vautrollier; the 1582 Star Chamber case of John Day v. Roger Ward; the full John Wolf controversy documents (1582–83); the Star Chamber Decree of 23 June 1586 — described by Arber as “the greatest enactment concerning the press in the reigns of Elizabeth or James I” — and the secret 1589 report to Lord Burghley naming the authors and printers of the Marprelate tracts. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_vol2_raw.txt]

Contents — Section by Section

Introduction (lines ~603–3879)

Arber opens with an essay on the history of the word “Stationer” — its Latin and continental origins in the 13th century (Toulouse, Paris, Castile), its definition in the 1440 Promptorium Parvulorum, and its establishment in English trade. He sets out the key regulatory documents whose full texts appear in this volume and describes the framework of State Papers, Star Chamber Bills, and Company archives from which the illustrative documents are drawn. The “vital and essential documents” he identifies as foundational to understanding the 1576–1595 period are: the first Star Chamber Decree (1566); Lambarde’s draft Act for the press (1580); Thomas Norton’s letter to George Goring about John Wolf (23 October 1582); Barker’s letter to Burghley (December 1582); the Company’s complaint against Wolf (March 1583); the report on printing-presses in London (1583); the Star Chamber Decree of 23 June 1586; and the Marprelate secret report (1589).

Before the register text begins, Arber inserts the full texts of the major early patents:

  • William Seres’s patent for psalters and primers (8 July 1559, lines ~3600): the Letters Patent granting Seres and his son the exclusive right to print all psalters, primers, and prayer books in English and Latin; the foundation of the longest-running and most contentious monopoly in the Elizabethan book trade
  • John Day’s 7-year licence (11 November 1559, lines ~3709): the Letters Patent granting Day a privilege for the Cosmographical Glass and all books he might produce for seven years at his own cost, subject to pre-publication allowance — an early prototype of the later licensing system
  • High Commission letter to the Stationers’ Company (25 May 1560, lines ~3757): the earliest evidence of surreptitious printing — the Commissioners report that “evill desposyd persons” have “secretly taken uppon them to prynte” Seres’s privileged psalters on “pryvye presses” and direct the Wardens to seize the books and punish the offenders
  • Archbishop Parker’s correspondence on the Bishops’ Bible (1566–68, lines ~3849): Bishop Cox’s recommendation of Bodleigh’s Geneva Bible (9 March 1566); Parker’s notice that the revision of the Bishops’ Bible has commenced (26 November 1566); Parker’s report of the revision’s conclusion (22 September 1568); his letter presenting the first copy to Queen Elizabeth (5 October 1568) — the documentary record of the authorised Bible project that would culminate in Barker’s editions of 1568–72
  • Vautrollier’s patent for certain books (19 June 1574): referenced in the contents table; the grant that established Vautrollier’s printing privileges before his flight to Edinburgh

Register B — Apprentices (lines ~3880–16007)

The complete enrollment record of apprentices presented to the Stationers’ Company from 20 July 1576 (20th year of Elizabeth’s reign) through 2 July 1605. Pages 31–292 of the original volume.

Entry format. Each entry gives: the date (in Latin, by feast-day and regnal year); the master’s name (in Clarendon type if a Company member); the apprentice’s full name; his father’s name, occupation, and place of origin (including the county); the term of years; the start date (by feast day); and the fee. From 1578 onward the enrollment fee rises from 6d to 2s 6d as a note marks (line ~4736). The county-of-origin data makes these records a geographical index of where Elizabethan stationers came from — apprentices from Yorkshire, Warwickshire, Kent, Lincolnshire, and London appear in the earliest entries.

The masters named in the earliest 1576 entries include William Seres, Richard Greene, Richard Gonneld, Richard Watkins, and William Norton. As the years progress, masters such as George Bishop, Henry Denham, John Charlewood, Robert Waldegrave, Richard Field, and Humphrey Lownes appear.

This section is the primary source for the apprenticeship careers of every known Elizabethan stationer: when an individual is identified as having been apprenticed to a particular master, the entry here gives the exact date, term, and geographical origin. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_vol2_raw.txt]

Register B — Book Entries / Entrances of Copies (lines ~16008–34489)

The copyright registration record — the central primary source for Elizabethan literary and print history. Every book, pamphlet, ballad, and broadside entered at Stationers’ Hall for which a fee was paid is recorded here, from 20 July 1576 to 25 June 1595. Pages 293–672 of the original volume.

Entry format. The standard form from 1576 onward: “[Printer’s or publisher’s name] Entred for his copie vnder th[e] handes of [warden(s)] and [licenser], [abbreviated title], [fee].” The licenser changes across the period: in the late 1570s entries are frequently under the wardens’ hands alone, or under “the bishop of [London’s]” hand; from 1586 the Star Chamber Decree requires the Archbishop of Canterbury’s representative or a bishop alongside the Wardens for most categories of work. Fees are typically 4d for a ballad, 6d for a pamphlet or small book, and higher for substantial works.

Coverage. The 20-year span 1576–1595 covers the peak of Elizabethan literary production. Entries in these pages include:

  • Sidney’s Arcadia — entered by William Ponsonby, 23 August [1590]: “Receaued of him for a booke of Sir PHILIP SIDNEYS makinge intitled Arcadia: aucthorised vnder the Archbishop of CANTERBURY [his] hand” (line ~25997)
  • Lodge’s Penelope’s Web — entered by Edward White, 26 June 1587: “Receaued of him for printinge a booke intituled PENELOPES Webbe Aucthorised vnder the bishop of LONDONS hand” (line ~24955)
  • Early play texts, verse collections, chronicles, devotional works, and the full range of Elizabethan print are present

The register also shows the mechanics of transfer, conditional entry, and disputed copies: entries note “PROVIDED that ye cumpanie shall haue them at his hands,” conditional licences, and the occasional notation that a book has been withdrawn or its entry cancelled.

Embedded within this section are the major illustrative documents relevant to book-trade disputes of the period:

  • The Star Chamber case John Day v. Roger Ward and William Holmes (7 February–10 July 1582, lines ~38336): Day’s bill of complaint against Ward and Holmes for printing his ABC and catechism contrary to his patent; the full Star Chamber proceedings including demurrers, examinations, and the decree. The case establishes that the 1566 Star Chamber Decree was the primary legal instrument against patent infringers, and that Ward’s defence — that the patent was contrary to common law — was rejected

^[source: stationers_registers_arber_vol2_raw.txt]

The John Wolf Controversy Documents (lines ~39330–39600)

The documentary record of the early 1580s crisis over unauthorized printing, centred on John Wolf (alias Wolfe), who had been apprenticed to John Day and who organized a group of journeymen printers and non-Company stationers to print patented and privileged copies in defiance of the Company’s authority.

Documents reproduced in full:

  • Thomas Norton’s letter to George Goring about John Wolf (23 October 1582): Norton — the barrister and government censor — reports on Wolf’s activities and the Company’s failure to contain them
  • The Wardens’ report to Lord Burghley that Roger Ward’s family refused to allow a search of his house (29 October 1582)
  • The appeal of householders using the trade of Stationers to Lord Burghley against Thomas Norton (October 1582): the journeymen printers’ side of the dispute
  • A similar appeal to Sir Francis Walsingham (undated, c. October 1582)
  • The supplication to the Privy Council of the Wardens of the Stationers on behalf of the ancients and patentees against John Wolf and his associates (c. March 1583): the Company’s formal complaint naming Wolf’s associates
  • The University of Cambridge’s appeal to Lord Burghley against the seizure of their printer Thomas Thomas (14 June 1583): the Cambridge press’s challenge to London Stationers’ jurisdiction outside the City
  • The final report of the augmented Commission from the Privy Council on the Stationers’ Company controversy (1583): the government’s resolution that settled the Wolf dispute — Wolf was eventually brought into the Company and given his own printing rights

These documents collectively show how the Elizabethan book-trade regulatory system was tested and renegotiated in the early 1580s. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_vol2_raw.txt]

The Star Chamber Decree of 23 June 1586 (lines ~41291–41400)

The full text of what Arber calls “the greatest enactment concerning the press in the reigns of Elizabeth or James I.” Written on vellum, this Decree — issued 23 June 1586, 28th year of Elizabeth’s reign — superseded all earlier orders and became the foundational press law for the next half-century.

Its key provisions: no person may print any book “against the fourme and meanyng” of any ordinance or statute; no press may be set up or kept except in London (plus the university towns of Oxford and Cambridge, by an earlier order); the number of presses in any printing house to be regulated by the Archbishop of Canterbury and the Bishop of London; no book to be printed until it has been “seen and perused” and allowed by the Archbishop or Bishop of London (or their nominees); a master printer who has been debarred may not exercise any branch of the trade even as a journeyman; any unlicensed books may be seized by any person whatsoever. The Act runs only for the life of the Queen.

The Decree is the direct legislative response to the Wolf controversy and to the perceived failure of the 1566 Star Chamber Decree to suppress unlicensed printing. It is the instrument that made the Archbishop of Canterbury’s imprimatur structurally necessary for all publishing — the requirement that appears in the book entries from 1586 onward. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_vol2_raw.txt]

The Marprelate Secret Report (lines ~41811–41870)

The full text of the secret report to Lord Burghley, dated 21 September 1589 in Burghley’s own hand, identifying the authors and printers of the Martin Marprelate tracts. Arber calls this “most valuable.”

The report identifies Robert Waldegrave as printer of the first three Marprelate books (printed in “a Dutch letter [type]” at Sir Richard Knightley’s house in Northamptonshire); names Henry Sharpe and John Hodgkins as printers of the last two books (in “a litle Romaine and Italian letter [type]”). It traces how Waldegrave’s press was searched at Easter 1588, how he saved his type “in a boxe vnder his Cloke,” brought the type to Mistress Crane’s house in London, and then to sites outside the City. It identifies the paper supply route through “a Spurrier [a maker of spurs] dwellinge about Pie Corner neere West Smythfeild.” The report also names Sir Richard Knightley and Mr Wiston as the gentlemen who harboured the press — both were later fined by the Star Chamber, though Archbishop Whitgift obtained remission of the fines. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_vol2_raw.txt]

Register B — Freemen (lines ~34490–43350)

The enrollment of freemen of the Company from April 1577 to July 1605. Pages 673–842 of the original volume. Each entry gives: date, freeman’s name, fee (initially 3s 4d + dinner paid), and master’s name. The fee rises progressively across the period. This section is the primary record of when each stationer took up his freedom — the moment he was legally entitled to publish (though not necessarily to print, absent a place in one of the twenty-two printing houses). ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_vol2_raw.txt]

Register B — Fines (lines ~43351–44404)

The record of fines levied by the Court of Assistants from 1576 to 1604. Pages 843–864 of the original volume. Entries record: date, the name of the person fined, the offence (printing without licence, breaking orders, working at an unlicensed press, etc.), and the amount. The fines section is a supplementary record to the book entries — where a book entry notes an infringement, the corresponding fine often appears here. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_vol2_raw.txt]
