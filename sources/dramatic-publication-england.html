Evelyn May Albright, Dramatic Publication in England, 1580–1640: A Study of Conditions Affecting Content and Form of Drama. Modern Language Association of America Monograph Series. D. C. Heath / Oxford University Press, 1927. ~1.1 MB extracted from zip (per-page OCR, 462 pages). University of Michigan library copy digitised via Internet Archive.

A scholarly study of the institutional, legal, and commercial conditions governing both performance and print publication of plays in the late Elizabethan and Jacobean periods. “Publication” is used in its technical sense to include both public performance and printed sale. Covers a range of companies, not only Shakespeare’s, and draws heavily on Henslowe’s Diary, Stationers’ Registers, Privy Council records, State Papers, and Herbert’s office-book. The study originated in a 1909 Shakespeare seminar at the University of Chicago under Professor Manly. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

Contents and Coverage

Preface (lines 68–108)

Albright defines her scope: “publication” covers both public performance and printed book sale, treating them as two distinct but related acts of making a play available to an audience. The study addresses the legal, commercial, and institutional framework — not literary interpretation of the plays themselves. Method is archival: Privy Council records, Stationers’ Registers, Henslowe’s Diary, Herbert’s office-book, State Papers. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

Chapter I: Organisation and Control of Dramatic Companies (lines 474–2751)

Status of actors. Albright argues the popular equation of actors with rogues and vagabonds applies to strolling players, not to members of the London patent companies. The 1572 statute required players to be retainers of “a baron of this realm or other honourable personage of greater degree” — this gave licensed company members a defined legal status. Richard Burbage’s company applied to the Earl of Leicester for a license in terms of being his “household servants.” Five actors from the King’s Men (including Shakespeare) marched in James I’s coronation procession and each received 4½ yards of red cloth from the royal wardrobe. Fifteen players were gowned in black at James’s funeral. The 1604 statute (1 Jas. I, c.7) shifted formal legal responsibility away from the patron and onto the individual actor. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

Companies and licences. The 1578 Privy Council order approved companies: Children of the Chapel, the Lord Chamberlain’s, the Earl of Warwick’s, the Earl of Leicester’s, the Earl of Essex’s, and the Children of Paul’s — and specified that no others should play. By 1622 Herbert’s office-book lists only five principal adult companies licensed. Provincial companies maintained separate touring licences, and touring records document dozens of appearances across England from 1558 to 1642. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

Actor contracts and economics. From Henslowe’s Diary: Thomas Heywood bound himself exclusively to the Admiral’s Men for two years with a £40 penalty for playing elsewhere. Robert Dawes’s contract specified payment by shares in proceeds rather than a fixed fee. The economic structure divided proceeds between actor-sharers (who received half profits from galleries, boxes, and tiring-house receipts) and housekeepers (who owned the theatre building and received the other half). Housekeepers acquired their share by money payment and by “merit and antiquity.” ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

Regulation: the Master of the Revels. Edmund Tilney served as Master of the Revels under Elizabeth; Herbert under James and Charles. By 1588 there were twelve licensers operating under Archbishop Whitgift’s authority. The Master of the Revels licensed plays for performance; the Lord Chamberlain stood between the companies and the Privy Council; the city of London’s Mayor had authority over performances within city boundaries. Companies in the liberties (Southwark, Shoreditch) were under Privy Council jurisdiction rather than the Mayor’s. In 1622, Herbert’s records show the theatres subject to closure when plague deaths exceeded a threshold (thirty per week under one patent, forty under later ones). ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

Chapter II: Literary and Dramatic Censorship (lines 2752–8820)

Part I: Censorship of Literature in General (lines 2752–4200)

Albright traces censorship from the 1382 Act of Parliament targeting Wycliffe’s followers, through Archbishop Arundel’s controls on Oxford manuscripts, to the first controls on printed books under the early Tudor monarchs. Cardinal Wolsey enforced Leo X’s bull against Lutheran books in 1520. A prohibitory index was established in 1526; a royal proclamation issued 1538. Mary I’s commissioners in 1557 extended controls. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

The officers of censorship across the period were: bishops and archbishops (with authority to license books in theology and divinity), the Star Chamber (with authority to decree press regulations), and the Mayor of London (with authority over printing within city boundaries). In practice these jurisdictions overlapped and competed. Whitgift’s 1599 proclamation specified “noe playes be printed excepte they bee allowed.” The Star Chamber decrees of 1566 and 1586 were the principal instruments of general press regulation. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

William Prynne’s Histriomastix (1633) resulted in a £5,000 fine, public pillory, loss of both ears, and imprisonment. Albright compares England’s censorship regime with France and Spain and concludes England operated with more freedom than either. She documents the persistence of both publishers and readers in circulating condemned books: Thomas Woodcock was committed to Newgate in 1578 for renewed sale of An Admonition to the Parliament; Raleigh’s History of the World circulated without a title-page after James condemned it. Albright quotes Calvert writing to Winwood in 1605 that a suppressed book on the peace negotiations was “not to be had” but he would send one when he found it. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

Part II: Censorship of the Drama (lines 4200–8820)

Albright identifies eight categories of offence in the eyes of the dramatic censor: (1) religious controversy — favorable or unfavorable exploitation of any creed or denomination; (2) sacrilege, and after 1606 use of oaths containing the name of Christ or God or obvious substitutes; (3) realistic portrayal of living kings, especially their weaknesses, vices, or policies; (4) satirical or hostile portrayal of foreign kings or high officials; (5) discussion of foreign politics or international relations; (6) cartooning of real persons of any rank, especially noblemen; (7) seditious sentiments or discussion of facts likely to produce popular discontent or desire for change of government; (8) excessive indecency. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

Religious discussion. Elizabeth gave an order before April 1559 prohibiting plays in derision of the Catholic religion, the mass, the saints, or God. A further proclamation of May 16, 1559 categorically forbade political and religious problems on stage, charging mayors and justices to permit no play “wherein either matters of religion or of the governance of the estate of the common weall shalbe handled or treated.” ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

Anti-Catholic portrayals ran throughout both reigns regardless of this prohibition. Bishop Gardiner had appeared as the type of the Catholic intriguer even in plays under Mary. Venetian and Spanish diplomatic correspondence documents protests about the English stage’s treatment of Catholicism. In 1618 Busino reported that “never represent any theatrical piece, not even satirical tragicomedy without larding it with the vices and iniquity of some Catholic churchman.” The Fortune players were fined £1,000 in 1639 for setting up an altar with basin and candlesticks on stage and bowing before it; they claimed it was an old play revived for a heathen gods scene. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

Puritans were ridiculed on stage throughout the period by Jonson, Marston, Middleton, and others. In the Martin Marprelate controversy (1589), the Martinists were satirized in plays by the Queen’s Men and Paul’s Boys. The Privy Council ordered a temporary suppression of all plays in London (November 8, 1589); Lord Strange’s Men refused to comply and two of their members were sent to the Counter. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

Treatment of contemporary matters: specific cases. Albright documents several named cases: ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

  • Richard II (Shakespeare): the deposition scene was reportedly suppressed or omitted during Elizabeth’s reign. The Essex faction commissioned a performance of an old play about Richard II on February 7, 1601, the day before the Essex rising. One player objected “the play was olde and they should have losse in playing it because fewe would come” but it was staged for £2 extra payment.

  • A Game at Chess (Middleton, 1624): depicted Philip IV of Spain and Count Gondomar allegorically in a chess game. Ran nine days before suppression. Albright documents it as the clearest case of systematic attack on a foreign king and his ambassador passing the censor.

  • The Isle of Dogs (Nashe/Jonson, 1597): caused the Privy Council to suppress all plays in London for the summer and to order demolition of The Curtain and The Theatre.

  • Eastward Ho (Chapman/Jonson/Marston, 1605): Chapman and Jonson imprisoned for passages mocking the Scottish at James I’s court.

  • Sir Thomas More (manuscript, Harley 7368): the manuscript shows Tilney’s censorship marks throughout; Tilney struck the riot scenes dealing with immigrants (“Ill May Day” scenes) and required their replacement. Albright’s account of the manuscript is that it was too sensitive for the stage in its submitted form.

  • Massinger’s plays: several referenced as suppressed or censored for criticism of royal policies; The Roman Actor (1626) includes a defence of free speech on the stage.

Chapter III: Authorship and Ownership of Plays (lines 8821–11458)

Albright opens by noting Sidney Lee’s estimate that only 600 of approximately 3,000 plays acted in the period reached print. She then documents the conditions of dramatic authorship that governed whether plays were published at all. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

Authorship as hack-work and revision. Playwrights routinely appropriated plots and whole lines from earlier plays. Henslowe’s Diary documents regular payments for “altering and mending of old plays,” for additions, prologues, and epilogues. Albright cites Davenport’s King John and Matilda as expanded from an earlier play; Shakespeare’s remodelling of old plays as common practice among best writers, not just second-rate ones. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

Company ownership. Once a playwright sold a play to a company it became company property; the playwright had no residual rights. Albright documents the legal mechanics of play ownership through an extended quotation from Richard Jones’s agreement of January 3, 1589 with Edward Alleyn, selling Jones’s share in “playing apparalles, play bookes, instrumentes, and other commodities” for £37 10s. The plays were specified property transferred in “playne and open market.” Alleyn is shown as a key broker in play transfers between companies: he sold I. Tamar Cam to the Admiral’s Men for £2 on October 2, 1602. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

“Staying” of entries. In 1600, the Chamberlain’s Men had four plays — As You Like It, Henry V, Every Man in His Humor, and Much Ado About Nothing — entered as “to be staied” on the Stationers’ Registers. Albright interprets this as the company reserving copyright by having a chosen publisher register the rights, rather than as suppression of publication. She documents what she identifies as a wave of piracy or threatened piracy around 1600, naming unscrupulous stationers Pavier, Danter, Ferbrand, and Ling as handling suspect texts at this period. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

Chapter IV: Conditions Affecting the Time of Publication (lines 11459–12668)

Albright examines the claim that theatrical companies systematically withheld plays from print to protect stage receipts. She finds the evidence mixed. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

Evidence for withholding: Heywood stated certain actors thought publication against their peculiar interest. The Whitefriars contract explicitly prohibited one play (“Torrismont”) from being put into print before a term expired. The possibility that reading a play in advance might dull audience interest is noted as a contemporary belief. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

Evidence against systematic withholding: Many plays were published while actively being staged. Shirley’s The Royal Master was entered on the Stationers’ Registers March 13, 1638, published the same year, and an acting licence was taken out after the print entry. Court revivals show old plays were regularly preferred; the prologue to The Lover’s Progress (Beaumont and Fletcher) claims the status of a new play for a revision. John Chamberlain wrote in 1614 that “of five new plays there is not one that pleases; and therefore they are driven to furbish over their old.” ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

Albright provides a decade-by-decade count of plays published (1580–1640) and a plague year table cross-referencing theater closures with publication numbers. Her conclusion is that no simple correlation exists between plague years or Puritan suppression and publication surges. For 1600 specifically — fifteen plays published, eleven from the Admiral’s and Chamberlain’s Men — she attributes the cluster to a bad outlook for stage receipts following the Privy Council’s restriction order and the illness of the Chamberlain, George Carey, rather than to piracy or compulsion. She identifies Henry V Q1 as a text that “can hardly be regarded as a legitimate text on any explanation of its defects.” ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

Chapter V: Sources of Play Texts Printed (lines 12669–13885)

Albright surveys contemporary and modern theories about how play texts reached the printer. She opens by noting the persistence of the assumption (citing Pope’s dismissal of quartos as “whole heaps of trash”) that early printed play texts are necessarily defective and illegitimate. She contrasts this with Pollard’s discriminating work in Shakespeare Folios and Quartos and Shakespeare’s Fight with the Pirates. Pollard’s conclusion: the players handed printers authorised texts of fourteen Shakespeare plays for quarto publication, three of them improved versions for re-issue. Pollard’s “bad quartos” are Romeo and Juliet Q1, Henry V Q1, Merry Wives Q1, and Hamlet Q1. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

Indicators of legitimacy and their limits. Markers of an authorised text include: author’s dedication or preface, actor’s dedication or preface, entry in Stationers’ Registers, publisher’s note of source. Albright documents that none of these markers is conclusive by itself and that absence of entry does not prove piracy. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

Sources of copy: author’s own manuscript; scriveners’ copies; actors’ individual roles; borrowed manuscripts; private transcripts; prompt-books. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

Piracy by stenography. Albright treats the shorthand-reporter theory with scepticism. She acknowledges shorthand textbooks were available and that one non-dramatic writer (noted at line ~3740 of the raw file) was said to have taken down sermons by shorthand. But she argues no reliable evidence proves this was done systematically with plays. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

Memorial reconstruction. An actor who had played a role could reconstruct the text from memory, with his own part as the anchor. Albright examines Greg’s theory that the Merry Wives Q1 was pirated by the actor playing the Host, arguing the Host’s speeches in Q1 and Folio are inconsistent in ways that cut against Greg’s theory. She documents how an actor-pirate’s version would tend to be accurate in his own speeches and defective in others’. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

Prompt-books. The bookkeeper’s working manuscript differed from the author’s foul papers: prompt-books contain actors’ names, stage directions added during rehearsal, bookkeeper’s notes and annotations. Albright notes that a play printed from prompt-book copy and a play printed from author’s manuscript will differ in ways unrelated to textual corruption. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

Chapter VI: Printing and Publishing Conditions (lines 13886–17245)

Typography and the monopoly system. The common contemporary criticism was that typography declined after 1560. Albright quotes Plomer attributing this to the monopoly system, which placed profitable printing work in the hands of half a dozen men; drama fell outside the class of privileged works and was handled by smaller printers competing for unprofitable jobs. She documents that Star Chamber decrees of 1566 and 1586 regulated press conditions. Albright tests Plomer’s argument by examining individual printers’ records and finds the relationship between monopoly exclusion and print quality more complicated than the broad assertion suggests. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

The Stationers’ Company. Entry on the Stationers’ Registers functioned as a form of copyright claim, not as a printing licence. The two were distinct: a book could be licensed for printing but not entered, and vice versa. The Company enforced fines for printing without entry or in violation of another member’s copyright. Albright documents specific Stationers’ actions: members’ shops nailed up, fines levied, books called in. The Company also assisted in censorship enforcement by collecting and burning condemned titles — she gives the example of the 1599 “bishops’ ban” requiring the Stationers to collect and burn several named works. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

Printers and publishers specialising in drama. Albright names Charlewood, Creede, Eld, Allde, and others as printers whose output was concentrated in play quartos. She documents the flow of plays between publishers by tracing individual titles through their entry and transfer records on the Registers. The Widow Brome is identified as publishing four Lyly plays in 1591–92 following the dissolution of the Children of Paul’s, suggesting plays entered the print market when companies dissolved or were suppressed. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]

Print economics. Play quartos were cheap books. Albright discusses the low returns to publishers from play printing, the typical print runs, and the distribution of plays through bookstalls and at the theatres themselves. She notes the paradox that publishers had little financial incentive to pirate plays, which cuts against some memorial-reconstruction theories that assume aggressive publisher demand. ^[source: dramatic_publication_england_raw.txt]
