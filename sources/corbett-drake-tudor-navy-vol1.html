Julian S. Corbett, Drake and the Tudor Navy: with a History of the Rise of England as a Maritime Power. Vol. I. London: Longmans, Green, 1898; reprinted 1912, 1917. ~17,541 lines extracted from a zip of 444 OCR’d page files. University of Virginia Library copy of the 1917 impression.

A major work of naval history, centering Drake’s biography within a broader argument about England’s emergence as a maritime power. Corbett’s central thesis is that the defeat of the Spanish Armada was not a triumph of “untutored national energy” but of advanced naval organisation and science over a maritime system that had become obsolete. Volume I covers Drake’s origins through the voyage of circumnavigation and his first appointment to the Royal Commission on the Navy (c.1545–1583), establishing his career as privateer and corsair before his transformation into admiral and administrator. ^[source: corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol1_raw.txt]

Contents — Chapter by Chapter

Preface to the Second Edition (lines ~1–100)

Corbett notes the few errors he has corrected from the first edition (1898) and defends his central tactical argument: that Drake introduced the close-hauled line-ahead in 1588. He clarifies that he does not attribute to Drake the fully developed line-of-battle formation of the 18th century; what he attributes is the first real appreciation of the broadside and the definite adoption of line-ahead as a fundamental tactical idea, as opposed to the line-abreast of medieval and galley warfare — the revolution that contained in itself the germ of the battle order. He thanks Dr. Tilton and Mr. Oppenheim for help in detecting errors. ^[source: corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol1_raw.txt]

Preface to the First Edition (lines ~100–330)

Corbett’s programmatic statement: the aim is to give a general view of the circumstances under which England first became a controlling force in the European system by virtue of her power upon the sea. He defends centering the history on Drake’s life, citing Stow’s summary of Drake’s reputation: “more skilful in all points of navigation than any that ever was before his time, in his time or since his death… his name was a terror to the French, Spaniard, Portugal, and Indian. Many princes of Italy and Germany desired his picture.” Volume I is concerned with Drake as corsair; Volume II with Drake as admiral and creator of a new force — “as the perfecter of a rational system of sailing tactics, as the father of a sound system of strategy, as the first and unsurpassed master of that amphibious warfare which has built up the British Empire… he has no rival in our history but Nelson.” The Preface also contains Corbett’s structural critique of Elizabethan naval administration: the Portugal expedition failed not from Drake’s fault but from the “inherent and abiding vice of our system of national defence” — the navy had laid the way open but no compact military force existed to exploit it. ^[source: corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol1_raw.txt]

Introduction: The Naval Art in the Middle of the Sixteenth Century (lines ~333–2720)

Corbett surveys the state of naval warfare and maritime organisation in mid-16th-century Europe, establishing the strategic and technological context for Drake’s emergence. The introduction examines the shifts from medieval galley tactics to early modern gunnery, the development of ship design, and the rise of competitive European naval powers as the Spanish and Portuguese monopolies on global trade and exploration faced mounting challenge. It frames the Elizabethan conflict with Spain not merely as religious upheaval but as three distinct streams of national sentiment: popular religious passion, governmental political rivalry, and aggressive commercial expansion — with mercantile pressure ultimately the most consequential. The introduction also surveys the dockyards, finance, and administrative machinery of the Tudor navy as Drake would have found it on entering service.

Chapter I: Drake’s Early Years (lines ~2721–3293)

Drake was born at Crowndale farm near Tavistock, Devon, property once held by Tavistock Abbey and leased from Sir John Russell (Lord High Admiral, founder of the House of Bedford). His father Edmund was a sailor of modest means; Francis was born around 1545. The family claimed connection to the Wyvern-bearing Drakes of Tavistock going back to Edward III, though contemporary sources (Camden’s natus est loco mediocri) place them in the yeoman class. Corbett emphasises the “revolutionary” middle-class character of Drake’s birth, situating him within the emerging merchant class that was becoming the controlling power of the Tudor state.

Chapter II: John Hawkins (lines ~3294–4320)

Traces the three confluent causes of Anglo-Spanish rupture: popular religious passion, governmental political rivalry, and commercial aggression — merchants’ determination to breach Spanish monopolies in the Indies. The chapter focuses on Hawkins as the exemplary merchant-prince, whose Guinea and Brazil ventures foreshadowed the assault on Spanish colonial trade. Corbett’s argument: commercial expansion was “the really most actively important” motive — but for English commercial blows to Spanish finance, Parma could have conquered the Low Countries and Elizabeth might have succumbed peacefully. Hawkins is established as the precursor to Drake’s strategy of challenging Spanish hegemony in the American seas.

Chapter III: San Juan de Ulua (lines ~4321–5051)

Hawkins’s third voyage (1567–68) was a watershed: Camden reported military and seafaring men across England demanded war after its conclusion. The squadron comprised six ships: Jesus of Lubeck (700 tons), Minion, William and John, Swallow, Angel, and Judith (50 tons, under young Drake), bearing approximately 500 men. Corbett defends Hawkins against charges of piracy, characterising him as a disciplined representative asserting legitimate English commercial claims against Spanish restriction of Indies trade. The Spanish assault at San Juan de Ulúa (modern Veracruz) — a surprise attack under flag of truce — set in motion the hostilities that would define Drake’s career: the personal grievance, the strategic conviction, and the political context.

Chapter IV: Drake’s First Service in the Navy (lines ~5052–6093)

Corbett explains 16th-century international law permitting “minor remedies” short of open war — legitimate reprisal without formal declaration. England, threatened by a Catholic coalition (France, Spanish Netherlands), was shielded by Huguenot rebellion and the Beggars’ command of the Dutch seas. In November 1568, Spanish treasure ships bound for Alva were driven into English ports; Cecil secured rumours of Hawkins’s massacre and suggested embargoing the treasure. This political moment — diplomatic ambiguity combined with state support for Protestant causes — gave Drake cover for his emerging privateering operations, setting the pattern for calculated deniability that would mark Tudor strategy against Spain throughout his career.

Chapter V: Nombre de Dios (lines ~6094–7155)

Drake’s raid on Nombre de Dios represents a new, romantic dimension of English sea power — “daring and contempt for effete traditions” — distinct from Hawkins’s merchant-backed ventures. Corbett sketches Spanish colonial structure: four governments (Peru, New Spain, Guatemala, Española), with Tierra Firme defended primarily by Cartagena, and coastal settlements practically undefended. French Huguenots had pioneered raiding the Indies since c.1545 (the year Potosí mines were discovered); San Domingo itself had been sacked. Drake’s enterprise capitalised on Spanish vulnerability and the precedent set by French privateers, transforming English participation from mercantile venture to corsairing assault — and demonstrating that the treasure-route between Panama and Nombre de Dios was the strategic centre of Spanish imperial finance.

Chapter VI: The Spanish Main (lines ~7156–8022)

Drake seized Cartagena’s harbour, capturing a Spanish frigate and a laden ship from Seville despite cannon and troop fire; Spanish messengers warned the town he had taken Nombre de Dios, ending the element of surprise. Corbett highlights Drake’s tactical innovation: rather than raiding coastal ships, he enlisted Maroon (escaped slave) allies and carried operations inland — a revolutionary departure from sea-based corsairing. Drake’s ruthless reorganisation included scuttling his brother’s ship (Swan) to man the pinnaces adequately, revealing the command discipline and calculated severity that would characterise his later voyages. The Maroon alliance established the principle of exploiting Spain’s colonial underclass as an intelligence and logistical resource.

Chapter VII: Drake and the War Party (lines ~8023–9132)

After Hawkins’s defeat, Drake vanished into obscurity (1573–75) while England’s diplomatic posture swung toward peace: the Ridolfi plot failed, Mary Stuart’s party was discredited, Alva sought reconciliation. Drake’s return with large Spanish plunder posed a diplomatic embarrassment — his case had to be treated as res judicata (settled) or he hidden, to avoid impeding negotiations with Spain. Corbett traces Drake’s probable refuge to Drake’s Pool, Ireland, and introduces the Tudor mechanism of chartered companies and patents, whereby the Crown contracted private subjects to undertake risky ventures while maintaining deniability. This mechanism — crucial in shielding Drake as he prepared for circumnavigation — is analysed as the structural foundation of Elizabethan maritime expansion.

Chapter VIII: The Voyage of Circumnavigation (lines ~9133–10509)

Spanish ambassador Mendoza could only confuse rumours that Drake was bound for Nombre de Dios when he sailed in December 1577; the true aim — circumnavigation via Magellan’s Strait — was kept secret. For thirty years Spaniards had abandoned Magellan’s route as impracticable; prior attempts by Loyasa (1525), Cabot, and others had failed catastrophically, with mutiny endemic. Drake’s fleet: Pelican (100 tons), Elizabeth, Marigold, Swan, Benedict, approximately 150 sailors, gentlemen volunteers (including the Doughtys), cartographers, and specialists in arms and warfare. The expedition represented state-sponsored exploration combined with privateering ambition, backed by Hatton and (Corbett argues) possibly Burghley. The complement of gentlemen volunteers created the social tensions that would culminate in Doughty’s execution.

Chapter IX: Voyage of Circumnavigation (continued) — St. Julian’s Bay and the Pacific (lines ~10510–11809)

Doughty’s execution in St. Julian’s Bay marked Drake’s transformation from boisterous corsair to stern commander. Mutiny threatened; Drake convened the men, confessed to his chaplain he was “bereaved of wits,” and demanded strict hierarchy: gentlemen must work rope, sailors must obey authority. He offered the Marigold to dissenters who dared return — none took it. This famous speech established the naval tradition of meritocratic discipline that would distinguish English sea-service from Spain’s hierarchical senility. Drake produced letters and the queen’s financial records, revealing the voyage as high state business involving three “mighty princes” (Elizabeth, Philip, Portugal’s king). Corbett treats this episode as the birth of the Elizabethan naval ethos.

Chapter X: Voyage of Circumnavigation (continued) — The Pacific, the Cacafuego, and the Route Home (lines ~11810–13012)

The Golden Hind captured the Cacafuego laden with silver, leaving her literally ballasted with bullion. Corbett analyses Drake’s options for the homeward route: via Moluccas and Cape of Good Hope; via Magellan; via a north-west passage (which he explored northward to approximately 48°N off California); and possibly the north-east passage the Muscovy Company pursued. Spanish pursuers — Don Luis de Toledo from Peru and the navigator Sarmiento de Gamboa — disagreed on Drake’s likely route; Toledo’s conservative course lost the pursuit. Sarmiento predicted Drake would seek the Straits of Anian (supposed north-west passage), and indeed Drake explored north California before turning west. Drake found Chinese pilots at Coronada Bay (Costa Rica) whose charts proved invaluable. The circumnavigation was completed in September 1580, producing approximately 200,000 ducats of royal Spanish plunder and 400,000 of private seizures.

Chapter XI: Knighthood (lines ~13013–14459)

Drake returned to England in autumn 1579 (arriving at Plymouth September 26, 1580); his arrival caused consternation in merchant circles fearing Spanish retaliation, yet filled the queen’s inner circle with triumph. Philip was assembling vast armament (ostensibly for Algiers, but directed by intelligence toward England or Portugal). The queen grew nervous at possible Spanish reckoning, though English corsairs had grown so dominant in the Narrow Seas that Spanish merchants habitually concealed losses and made private terms with pirate agents. Drake was knighted aboard the Golden Hind at Deptford on April 4, 1581 — the ceremony conducted by a French envoy’s hand at the queen’s invitation, underlining the diplomatic dimension of the honour. Corbett emphasises the impunity of English rovers and the climax of tension that would precipitate open war.

Chapter XII: The Navy of Elizabeth (lines ~14460–17541)

Drake’s first official appointment came as sub-commissioner on a 1583 Royal Commission investigating naval corruption, mismanagement, and readiness. The Commission — chaired by the Lord Treasurer, Lord Admiral, Lord Chamberlain, Walsingham, and Mildmay — appointed sub-commissioners from leading sea-captains (Drake, Cotton, Gorges, and masters), empowered to inspect dockyards, oversee ship-building, and report on mobilisation capacity. Corbett opens his examination of Elizabeth’s Navy as an institution: the Spanish defeat was “the triumph of advanced organisation and science over a maritime system that was dead.” Drake, having left the service at its nadir in 1569, now surveyed a reformed and scientifically managed fleet poised for conflict. The chapter analyses the Navy’s administrative reform under Hawkins as Treasurer, the new ship designs (lower, faster, broadside-optimised), and the emerging doctrine of gunnery-at-distance over boarding — the technical foundation of the Armada victory described in Volume II.
