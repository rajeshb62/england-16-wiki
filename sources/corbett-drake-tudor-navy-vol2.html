Julian S. Corbett, Drake and the Tudor Navy: with a History of the Rise of England as a Maritime Power. Vol. II. London: Longmans, Green, 1898; reprinted 1912, 1917. ~22,974 lines extracted from a zip of 508 OCR’d page files. University of Virginia Library copy of the 1917 impression.

Volume II covers Drake as admiral and administrator from 1585 through his death in 1596 — the Indies Voyage, the Cadiz raid, the Armada campaign, the Lisbon expedition, his disgrace, and his final voyage. Corbett’s central analytical contribution is a detailed account of the Armada campaign as a naval-tactical event, arguing that English victory resulted from Drake’s innovations in broadside gunnery, line-ahead formation, and disciplined gun crews, deployed most decisively at the Battle of Gravelines (July 29, 1588). A secondary argument concerns Elizabeth’s persistent strategic ambivalence: she wielded naval force as a diplomatic instrument, repeatedly restraining Drake’s offensive strategy and thus preventing the complete destruction of Spanish power that his system of amphibious warfare might otherwise have achieved. ^[source: corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol2_raw.txt]

Contents — Chapter by Chapter

Chapter I: The Indies Voyage 1585 (lines ~232–1358)

Drake’s 1585 Indies expedition marks the transition from undeclared reprisal warfare to formal Anglo-Spanish hostility. Elizabeth’s commission contained implicit authority for aggressive action against Spanish colonial infrastructure. The expedition assembled approximately 2,300 men (soldiers and sailors), led by experienced commanders including Frobisher and Carleill, with an operational plan targeting San Domingo, Cartagena, Panama, and Havana. Corbett frames this as deliberate provocation designed to precipitate open war, though Philip remained unwilling to commit fully to English conquest until his broader European strategy aligned. Epidemic disease, appearing mid-Atlantic, claimed hundreds of men and compromised combat strength throughout the campaign.

Chapter II: San Domingo and Cartagena (lines ~1359–2992)

Drake’s 1585 fleet attacked the Caribbean’s most valuable Spanish possessions, sacking San Domingo (the oldest European settlement in the Indies and the jewel of Philip’s colonial empire) after a difficult mountain march to force the defences. Corbett emphasises the scale of Drake’s strategic vision: he aimed not merely to plunder but to destroy the supply infrastructure sustaining Spanish Atlantic power and to seize advanced bases — notably Havana and Panama — for future operations. Despite achieving the tactical objectives at San Domingo and Cartagena, sickness and Spanish reinforcements curtailed the campaign’s ambitions. The voyage returned with significant prizes and demonstrated Spanish colonies’ vulnerability, precipitating Philip’s decision to build a formal invasion fleet.

Chapter III: Operations on the Spanish Coast (lines ~2993–5018)

Following Drake’s triumph and the Babington Plot’s exposure (1586), Elizabeth enters a phase of diplomatic ambiguity, keeping Drake poised for action while avoiding outright declaration of war. She permits preparation of a Portuguese expedition under Don Antonio (the legitimate Portuguese claimant), even exploring Ottoman alliance possibilities, but repeatedly suspends offensive action when diplomatic overtures seem promising. Corbett details Philip’s strategic response: rather than pursuing the English Enterprise directly at cost, he decides to build a powerful ocean fleet to secure his sea lanes and colonies — a more economical path to protecting Spanish interests. Drake is kept “in constant communication” with Don Antonio, serving both as a deterrent to Spain and as a negotiating tool.

Chapter IV: Drake’s Plan of Campaign (lines ~5019–6916)

Drake’s post-Indies intelligence convinced Elizabeth of an imminent Armada, but she refused to mobilise the fleet preemptively or dispatch Drake to harass Spanish preparations — a decision Corbett partially defends on diplomatic grounds but characterises as strategically misguided. The chapter traces the Armada’s fitful concentration at Lisbon: delayed by Santa Cruz’s pursuit of a Portuguese fleet, damaged by gales, and fractured by Spanish internal disagreement. Experienced admirals such as Recalde recognised the inherent weakness of invading England without a secure harbour; the impetuous Leyva and Philip’s stubborn adherence to original instructions overrode such counsel. By autumn 1587, the fleet could not sail; the golden opportunity for English preemption had passed, leaving England unprepared and Spain determined to proceed. Drake’s 1587 Cadiz raid (not a separate chapter but covered in context) destroyed substantial Spanish stores and delayed departure by months.

Chapter V: Attempts to Attack the Armada (lines ~6917–8079)

The English fleet gathered at Plymouth under Lord Admiral Howard numbered approximately 100 sail (69 galleons and great-ships, including 16 Royal Navy vessels), with total crews around 10,000 men; Drake served as Vice-Admiral, Hawkins as Rear-Admiral. Spain’s fleet had been reconstituted to approximately 114 sail after its initial dispersion following the Cadiz raid and Santa Cruz’s death. Howard and Drake’s attempts to attack the Spanish fleet at Coruña were thwarted by summer storms that forced the English back to Plymouth. The fundamental strategic problem: without a favourable wind and tactical surprise, neither fleet could force engagement decisively, and the Armada, once regrouped and moving up the Channel, became increasingly difficult to intercept from behind.

Chapter VI: The Fleets in Contact (lines ~8080–10062)

Howard plans to blockade and destroy the Armada at Coruña while detaching a flying squadron to intercept the Indies treasure fleet — a sound strategy that proved unexecutable against contrary winds. Corbett praises Howard’s command style: lacking Drake’s tactical genius, his “high dignity” and “assured tact” unified a fleet of competing aristocrats and sea-captains where Drake’s rough temperament might have bred faction and jealousy. Spanish leadership remained demoralised; Sidonia doubted his own fitness to command; Recalde and senior officers urged modifications to Philip’s plan but were overridden. The chapter portrays both fleets as frustrated — the English by weather and logistics, the Spanish by their admiral’s caution and the fundamental contradiction of invading a fortified island from open water without an English harbour as base.

Chapter VII: Portland and the Isle of Wight (lines ~10063–11365)

Upon encountering the English fleet off Plymouth on July 31, 1588, the Spanish concluded they must fight for the Isle of Wight — the only harbour on the eastern Channel that could serve as an advanced base for Parma’s crossing. The chapter details the tactical engagement off Portland: the English attempt to gain the weather gauge fails initially; Spanish galleasses operate effectively in the calm; Frobisher becomes dangerously isolated near Portland Bill but is rescued. Drake’s controversial dimming of his stern lantern during a night chase — he abandoned formation to pursue Spanish stragglers — confuses the English fleet’s order and earns Howard’s reproach. Corbett analyses the fundamental asymmetry: English ships’ superior sailing qualities, firepower concentration, and trained gun crews against Spanish superiority in numbers and boarding strength. By the Isle of Wight action, it becomes clear that Spanish tactics (massed crescent formation, boarding assault) cannot overcome English nimbleness and gunnery, yet the English cannot inflict decisive damage with available ammunition.

Chapter VIII: The Battle of Gravelines (lines ~11366–13276)

Corbett’s magisterial account of the decisive engagement of July 29, 1588. Sidonia’s fleet, lacking intelligence of Parma’s readiness and anchor-bound at Calais, is dispersed by fire-ships (Drake and Hawkins’s initiative) — forcing an emergency evacuation that breaks the massed formation. With the Armada scattered northeastward toward Gravelines, the English fleet delivers sustained volleys at close range: Spanish crews were appalled by English accuracy and rate of fire; boarding attempts failed repeatedly; ships lost formation in confusion. The English inflicted irreplaceable losses (seven major ships sunk or destroyed, many more crippled) while exhausting Spanish powder and shot. Corbett stresses that superior English seamanship and gun discipline — trained by Drake’s insistence on target practice — proved decisive; Spanish courage and numbers could not overcome the technological and tactical revolution. Line-ahead gunnery was the instrument; Drake’s school had trained the instrument. Gravelines established the broadside-gunned sailing warship as the decisive weapon of European naval power.

Chapter IX: The Lisbon Expedition (lines ~13277–15355)

Despite the Armada’s destruction, contemporaries felt grave disappointment — the English had not achieved the overwhelming victory expected, Spain’s navy had demonstrated resilience, and English gunnery was recognised as inadequately trained (provoking a petition to revive Henry’s Corporation of Gunners). Drake faced a changed political landscape: Leicester’s death and Walsingham’s exhaustion removed his patrons. A 1589 expedition to Portugal under Drake and Norreys (to support Don Antonio) failed disastrously — not from naval action but from plague, logistics failures, and disagreement over strategic objectives. Drake’s navy successfully attacked Coruña and destroyed ships there, but Norreys’ army could not take Lisbon, and the expedition returned discredited. Corbett argues that the campaign revealed Drake’s limitations in joint army-navy command and, more importantly, demonstrated Elizabeth’s reluctance to pursue unlimited war when diplomatic and commercial opportunities seemed available.

Chapter X: Drake’s Disgrace (lines ~15356–17043)

Drake’s successful Armada campaign paradoxically isolated him politically. Elizabeth and the older aristocratic establishment — jealous of his rise — held him responsible for the Portugal expedition’s failure, though his navy operations were sound; blame attached to Norreys and the army, but Drake shared in the discredit. With Leicester dead and Walsingham following (1590), Drake lost his advocate at Court. Strategic doctrine shifted from Drake’s offensive grand strategy (attacking Spanish sources of power at their origin) to Hawkins’s commerce-raiding model — privateering against merchant convoys rather than systematic destruction of enemy capacity. This pivot was economically attractive to Elizabeth but strategically incomplete: it never threatened Spain’s recovery. Drake was kept from active command during the crucial years 1590–94, his expertise wasted while the war devolved into petty corsairing. Corbett implies that the loss of Drake’s “master hand” explains the war’s eventual stagnation.

Chapter XI: The Last Voyage (lines ~17044–22974)

In 1595, with new Spanish fleets reported and Ireland threatened by Spanish intrigue, Drake regained favour and led an expedition to repeat his 1585 Indies successes. Corbett frames this as a tragic miscalculation: Drake believed Spain remained as vulnerable as a decade earlier, not grasping that Philip had transformed his navy into a permanent, professionally managed force with dedicated escort squadrons (the Indian Guard and Tertia). The expedition was too weak, and the strategic vision — to strike Panama and compel Philip to disperse resources — was sound in theory but impossible given Spain’s new naval reality. Drake’s final council-of-war disputes with Hawkins, the missed interception of treasure fleets, and the mounting sickness (Hawkins died off Puerto Rico in November 1595; Drake died of dysentery off Portobello on January 28, 1596) are recounted in detail. Corbett frames the last voyage as a tragedy of outdated assumptions: Drake’s contempt for Spanish seamanship, earned in 1585 when Spain was unprepared, blinded him to Spain’s post-Armada professionalisation and the impossibility of repeating easy triumphs.
