Edward Arber (ed.), A Transcript of the Registers of the Company of Stationers of London, 1554–1640 A.D. Volume V — Index. Privately printed, Birmingham, 15 March 1894. ~71,739 lines extracted from a zip of 404 OCR’d page files. Cornell University Library copy no. 227 of 230 sets.

This is the fifth and final volume of Arber’s five-volume Transcript. Volumes I–IV (1875–77) contain the full transcription of the actual Stationers’ Company registers — the day-by-day record of books entered for copyright, apprentices bound and freed, orders of the Court of Assistants, fines, and transfers of copy. Volume V does not repeat that register text; instead it is an index and reference companion to the whole work. It contains four distinct sections: Arber’s own introduction, a paper by the Company’s Clerk on the Company’s records and history, a master list of every publisher active 1553–1640, and a year-by-year bibliographical summary of English printed books 1553–1603. The volume closes with a general index (Index I) to persons and places across all five volumes. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_raw.txt]

Contents — Section by Section

Arber’s Introduction (lines ~208–1917)

Arber introduces each of the volume’s main sections. On the List of 847 Publishers: every name is a person who either registered or transferred a book at Stationers’ Hall, or who published in London without registering. The list was checked and rechecked at a cost of nearly £100. Members of the Company appear in Clarendon type; non-members in Roman capitals. The list is a “master key to English bibliography” for the period, because almost all authorised books were printed in the metropolis (with some coming from Oxford and Cambridge presses from 1584-85 onward).

On London’s book-trade geography: Paul’s Churchyard was the focus and centre of the English book trade throughout this period. Fleet Street came second, owing to its proximity to the Inns of Court. In James I’s reign the trade began moving westward along Holborn toward Westminster. Printers had by an early date abandoned Paul’s Churchyard to booksellers, needing more room for press-work. John Day’s printing house was at Aldersgate Gate (he had his shop under the Gate); Richard Tottel’s at Temple Bar.

On the career of a London stationer: Arber traces the typical path from country apprentice to master printer. A raw country lad would be bound apprentice for enough years to complete his twenty-fourth year before being made free. Having served his time he was free to publish but not to print, since by immemorial custom there were no more than twenty-two printing houses in the City (apart from the royal printers). Vacancies arose only rarely and were filled by the Court of Assistants with the Archbishop of Canterbury’s approval. The surest path to a printing business was to marry the master’s daughter or a printer’s widow — Arber gives Richard Field marrying Vautrollier’s daughter as a canonical example, and lists three widows who each had three successive printer-husbands, the printing business passing with them each time. Short of marriage, a stationer might begin with a ballad sheet, accumulate capital publishing pamphlets, come onto the Livery, and eventually apply for one of the twenty-two masterships.

On the Bibliographical Summary: its primary object is to provide a dated list of all printers and publishers in Britain during the period, plus continental printers who produced English books or books about England. Titles are greatly abbreviated but sufficient in conjunction with author, printer/publisher, date, and format to identify any edition. The intention was to carry the summary to 1640 but circumstances prevented it; it ends at 1603. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_raw.txt]

Rivington’s Paper on the Records of the Stationers’ Company (lines ~1918–4120)

A paper read 12 December 1881 by Charles Robert Rivington, Clerk of the Stationers’ Company, to the London and Middlesex Archaeological Society; revised to 1893 for inclusion here.

History of the Company. A Brotherhood or Fellowship of Stationers existed from 1403, with Wynkyn de Worde among its early members, well before printing began. The printing trade fell naturally into their hands. In 1556, Philip and Mary granted a Charter of Incorporation. Rivington quotes the charter’s preamble in full — its stated purpose was to suppress “seditious and heretical books, rhymes, and treatises” published by “scandalous, malicious, schismatical, and heretical persons.” The first Master was Thomas Dockwray; the first Wardens John Cawood and Henry Cooke. The Charter granted the Company power to search for, seize, and destroy unlicensed books, and prohibited all printing within the realm without the Company’s licence (except by patentees). Elizabeth confirmed the Charter on 10 November 1559.

The Company’s records. The most ancient record is the first Wardens’ Account Book, beginning with the Master and Wardens named in the Charter, covering receipts and disbursements from 1554 to 1557. The first Register of Copies was long thought lost until John Northouck discovered that book entries before 1571 were in the Wardens’ Accounts. There is a gap of five years; the first separate Register of Copies begins in 1576, and from 1576 to 1881 the Registers are intact.

The Company’s halls. The Brotherhood had a hall in or near Milk Street before incorporation. By some time before 1570 they had moved to St Paul’s Churchyard. In 1611 Abergavenny House (between Amen Corner and the City Wall) was purchased for the Company. In 1666 the Hall was entirely destroyed in the Great Fire; everything was lost including the Company’s Seal, but the Registers survived, probably because they were in the Clerk’s house on Clerkenwell Green. The Hall was rebuilt by 1674.

List of Masters 1554–1881. The paper includes a complete list of Masters of the Company from the first (Thomas Dockwray) through 1881, with death dates and brief biographical notes on the more notable figures. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_raw.txt]

List of 847 London Publishers, 1553–1640 (lines ~4121–11062)

Arber’s own label: “a master key to English bibliography.” Every person who registered a book at Stationers’ Hall between 1553 and 1640 is listed, plus nearly all those known to have published in London without registering (marked with †). Company members are in Clarendon type; non-members in Roman capitals.

Format of each entry. Left column: the stationer’s initials and name. Central column: approximate period of publishing. Right columns: date and volume/page reference of first entry to that stationer; date and volume/page of last entry; names and dates of all assignments to and from that stationer (i.e. copies transferred in or out). Every entry therefore gives a quick career timeline and pointers to all relevant pages in Volumes I–IV.

What it covers. About 887 names in total, classified as: 5 patentees for books; 847 London publishers; 4 university printers (Oxford and Cambridge); 1 Dutch printer at Norwich; 6 country booksellers who published in London; and 24 authors or other persons who had books printed for them. Widows who carried on business in their own names are included. Assigns or deputies of patentees are excluded.

Sample entries visible in the text: Francis Adams, Richard Adams, Thomas Adams (noting first/last register entry dates and cross-references); Edward Aggas; Thomas Archer; John Bailey; John Barnes; Randall Bearkes; Christopher Barker (named in the introduction as an example of Clarendon-type members); Richard Tottell (similarly named). The list runs alphabetically A through Y. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_raw.txt]

Bibliographical Summary of English Literature, 1553–1603 (lines ~11063–64909)

A year-by-year catalogue of every known book printed in England (and relevant books printed on the Continent) from 1553 to 1603. This is the most substantive section of the volume — approximately 54,000 lines of text covering fifty years of English printing.

Format. Within each year, entries are grouped by printer or publisher. Each entry gives: a serial number; the abbreviated title; the author’s name; the format (fol., 4to, 8to, 16s, s. sh. br., etc.); the date; and a cross-reference to the Stationers’ register entry in Volumes I–IV where applicable. The printer’s address is given in full at their first appearance in each year (e.g. “R. T.—, Richard Tottell, dwelling at the Hand and Star [between the two Temple Gates] in Fleet street; within Temple Bar.”). The asterisk () before a publisher’s initials indicates they were also a printer at the time.

Coverage. Begins with undated books from Edward VI’s reign (1552–53) and runs continuously through 1603 (Elizabeth’s last year). The intention was to extend to 1640 but was abandoned for reasons not specified. Includes not only books produced in London but also books produced for English readers at continental presses (Geneva, Frankfurt, Antwerp, etc.). Foreign-language books by or about English people are included.

Opening entries (1553). Thomas Berthelet at the Lucrece in Fleet Street printing Erasmus; William Copland at the Rose Garland; John Day dwelling over Aldersgate; Richard Grafton within Christ’s Hospital; Reginald Wolf at the Brazen Serpent in Paul’s Churchyard; William Seres at the Hedgehog at the west end of Paul’s Church; Richard Tottel at the Hand and Star within Temple Bar. The printer addresses themselves are primary-source data on the physical geography of the Elizabethan book trade.

Scope. From the visible entries the summary includes: proclamations and statutes; Bibles and prayer books; devotional and theological works; chronicles and histories; poetry and drama; legal texts; scientific and medical works; educational books; translations from classical and contemporary European authors; maps and charts. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_raw.txt]

Index I: Persons, Places, and the Stationers’ Company (lines ~64910–71739)

A general alphabetical index to persons and places appearing anywhere in Volumes I–V of the Transcript. Each entry gives the person’s name followed by volume and page references across the five-volume set, plus (for publishers) a note of their approximate period of activity (e.g. “Published from 1579 to 1603”). Places are indexed both as locations of printing houses and as the towns from which apprentices came — the index includes, for example, a list of places in Yorkshire from which stationers came between 1576 and 1605.

A note at the end states: “It was not possible to print INDEX II, see page 215.” The intended second index (presumably a title or subject index) was never completed.

This index is the fastest way into the whole five-volume Transcript: any stationer’s name, any place, any person mentioned in the registers can be located and then followed to the relevant pages in Volumes I–IV for the full primary-source entry. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_raw.txt]
