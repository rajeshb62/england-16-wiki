Edward Arber (ed.), A Transcript of the Registers of the Company of Stationers of London, 1554–1640 A.D. Volume IV — Text. Privately printed, London, 1 May 1877. Reprinted New York: Peter Smith, 1950. ~34,464 lines extracted from a zip of 554 OCR’d page files. Pennsylvania State College Library copy.

This is the fourth of five volumes of Arber’s Transcript. Volume IV contains Register D — the book entry (copyright registration) record from 10 July 1620 to 3 November 1640, covering the last four years of James I (65th–67th years), the entire reign of Charles I to November 1640 (68th–85th years), together with Calls on the Livery and Promotions to the Assistance to 31 December 1640. This is the closing volume of the primary register text. It contains the registration entry for the Shakespeare First Folio (8 November 1623), the full documents of the George Wither war with the Stationers’ Company (1623–25), the text of the Second Star Chamber Decree on Printing (11 July 1637), Sir John Lambe’s list of printers authorised under that Decree, and a [?1614] petition of journeymen printers to Parliament. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_vol4_raw.txt]

Contents — Section by Section

Introduction (lines ~600–1775)

Eight-section Introduction, the longest of the four volumes.

Section I opens with a newly discovered passage from the Life of John Bois (one of the translators of the Authorized Version of the Bible), which records that the final six revisers of the 1611 Bible — two from each of the Cambridge, Oxford, and Westminster companies — “went daily to Stationers Hall, and in Three quarters of a Year fulfilled Their Task,” receiving 30 shillings per week from the Company of Stationers. Arber uses this to trace the Company’s connection to the production of the Authorized Version, noting that the financial burden fell on Robert Barker as Queen’s Printer (not on the Company directly), and that Barker would not have begun paying the revisers before 1609 at the earliest.

Section II covers the war between George Wither the poet and the Stationers’ Company. James I had previously imprisoned Wither, but on 17 January 1623 granted him a patent giving him the monopoly on his own Hymns and Songs of the Church and — the clause the Stationers called “the sting” — requiring that no English Psalm book be sold unless bound together with Wither’s hymns. The full text of the two material clauses of the patent is reproduced. The conflict produced Wither’s Schollers Purgatory (1625), from which Arber quotes at length, including the “Character of the Honest Stationer” and the “Character of the Mere Stationer” — the second being Wither’s portrait of the bookseller as a monopolist indifferent to learning, truth, or public benefit. The embedded documents for the George Wither war appear at original pages 13–20 of this volume.

Section III gives the full text of the 1636 arbitration between master printers and journeymen printers — a 19-article Court minute settling the disputes set out in the journeymen’s petition (which is separately reproduced in the Addendum at pp. 525–6). The articles regulate: the removal of unbound workers from printing houses; the prohibition on turning over apprentices across company lines to evade the 1586 Decree; limits on the number of apprentices per master; the prohibition on standing formes except for psalters, grammars, ABCs, primers, almanacks, and prognostications; maximum impression sizes (Nonpareil: 5,000 copies; Brevier: 3,000; all other books: 1,500–2,000; not to exceed 3,000 even with the Master’s permission); and journeymen’s right to 3d per week in lieu of the customary copy of each book worked.

Section IV supplements the earlier research on the history of the Stationers’ Company name: the earliest documentary use of “Stationer” for a London citizen (1412, from Liber BROWNE in the Court of the Commissary of London); 1480 evidence from the wardrobe accounts of Edward IV showing the pre-printing Stationer craft consisted of “binders, dressers, and gilders” of manuscripts; and the 1550 will of Henry Smyth, “citizen and Stacioner of London.”

Section V describes the literary character of the period covered by this volume. Ballad entries reappear in large numbers; the 14 December 1624 Ballad Partnership created a voluntary association among the principal ballad publishers. Arber characterises this period as “the most scientific oppression to which the English people were ever subjected” — political tension made smaller stationers prefer old, non-political publications to new ones. He notes that Ben Jonson’s translation of John Barclay’s Argenis was registered on 2 October 1623 but no copy has since been seen or heard of.

Section VI closes the enquiry sustained across the four Introduction essays — the conditions under which it was possible, openly and in peace, to print and sell an English book in London.

Section VII addresses Episcopal censorship, noting that its weight must be sought in the forbidden literature of the time — specifically the Martin Marprelate tracts — rather than in the Register itself, where the Bishops’ control “was continuous, and so unquestioned that it needed not any legal process to enforce it.” The suppression of Thomas Cartwright’s Confutation of the Rhemish Testament for over thirty years, solely because the author was a Puritan, is cited. When it finally appeared in 1618, it was printed not in England but in Leyden, “owned and worked it is said by Edward Brewster, one of the self-exiled Pilgrim Fathers.”

Section VIII is Arber’s acknowledgments, and a notice of the death of Charles Childs, the printer of these volumes, on 26 December 1876. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_vol4_raw.txt]

Admissions to Clothing and Assistance, 1607–1638 (lines ~1776–2200)

A continuation of the Livery and Assistance admissions from Volume II (which runs to 1607). Lists new liverymen by date from 1607 through 1637, and Calls of Assistants from 1604 through 1637. Notable: the 1638 renewal of the Livery is described as “the largest renewal of the Livery at one time since the 1st July 1598” — over twenty men admitted to the Clothing and Assistance in a single session in 1638 including Hodgkinson, Leake, Rothwell, Badger, Dawson, Griffin, Clarke, and others. Calls of Assistants include William Leake and Richard Field (1604), Bankworth, Swinhowe, Matthew Lownes, and John Jaggard (1612), and others through 1637. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_vol4_raw.txt]

A Final Provisional List of London Printers and Publishers (lines ~2200–2570)

A continuation of the Publishers List begun in Volume V — extending the record of London printers and publishers by their date of first registered publication, from those first appearing in the Jacobean and Caroline periods (from c. 1610 to c. 1637). Names listed alphabetically within each initial letter with the date of first registered publication and the volume/page reference to the registration. This section extends the Vol. V list for stationers whose careers began after 1603. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_vol4_raw.txt]

Some Notable Transfers of Books (lines ~2570–2614)

A sample, described as a preview of what will be worked out systematically in Volume V, tracing the successive assignments of individual copies through the deaths and marriages of their owners. Examples: T. East’s books passing from his widow (1609) to T. Snodham, then to W. Stansby (1626), then to G. Bishop (1639); C. Knight’s books cycling through three assignments (1629, 1636, 1639) and returning to their starting point; A large portion of the Man family’s books assigned to B. Fisher (1635) and then mortgaged to B. Young (1637). These traces show the copy as a heritable and assignable asset across multiple generations. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_vol4_raw.txt]

Register D — Book Entries, 10 July 1620 – 3 November 1640 (lines ~2805–33756)

The copyright registration record for twenty years of English printing — the last years of James I and the full reign of Charles I to the eve of the Long Parliament. Original pages 33–524.

Entry format. The standard form continues: “[Stationer’s name] Entred for his copie vnder th[e h]andes of [licenser(s)], [abbreviated title], [fee].” Licensers named include Master Doctor Worrall, Master Cole (Warden), Master Doctor Ffeatlie, Master Doctor Goade, the Bishop of London’s chaplains, and the Master of the Revels for plays. The fee is 6d for most single entries; multiple entries in one session remain common.

Opening illustrative documents (pp. 35–38, lines ~2815–3141). The register section opens with the Scintilla, or A Light broken into darke Warehouses (London, 1641) — a rare anonymous pamphlet addressed to Parliament comparing the prices of Bibles, grammars, and school books in former times and in 1641. It surveys the King’s Printers’ Bible patents; the Cambridge University challenge (when Cambridge printed a folio Bible in 1629 at 10s rather than the London monopoly price of 12–15s, the King’s Printers immediately produced a competing edition and sold it at 5s to drive Cambridge out); and the pricing of grammars, ABCs, and almanacks under the Stationers’ Company patent. Arber’s prefatory note calls it “a remarkable testimony to the never-ending competition in the book trade; to the power of the King’s Printers, partly as patentees and partly as capitalists only.”

Key entries — Shakespeare and literary history:

  • Shakespeare First Folio (8 November 1623, lines ~8131–8156): “Entred for their Copie vnder the hands of Master Doctor WORRALL and Master Cole warden Master WILLIAM SHAKSPEERS Comedyes Histories, and Tragedyes soe manie of the said Copies as are not formerly entred to other men” — registered by Master Blounte (Edward Blount) and Isaak Jaggard. The sixteen plays listed: Comedies — The Tempest, The Two Gentlemen of Verona, Measure for Measure, The Comedy of Errors, As You Like It, All’s Well That Ends Well, Twelfth Night, The Winter’s Tale (fee: 7s); Histories — The Third Part of Henry VI, Henry VIII, Coriolanus, Timon of Athens, Julius Caesar; Tragedies — Macbeth, Antony and Cleopatra, Cymbeline (fee: 6d).
  • Ben Jonson’s translation of Barclay’s Argenis (2 October 1623): noted in the Introduction as registered but of which no copy has been found.
  • John Donne — sermons, devotions, and other works registered multiple times across the period (lines ~6373, 6539, 7559, 8370, 11449, 13390 and others).
  • Francis Bacon — multiple entries including Novum Organum, Essays, the History of Henry VII, and Historia Ventorum et Vitae et Mortis (lines ~10263, 11207, 11721, 12959, 13202 and others).
  • George Wither — his Motto (c. 1621), Philarete (c. 1622), Metrical Illustrations (c. 1635), and the assigned-over entries reflecting the Stationers’ attempts to control his copies.

The 68th Year (1623–24) — George Wither war: Book entries for this year occur alongside the embedded Wither war documents at pages 13–20 of the Introduction. The register entries themselves show the Stationers refusing to enter or selling round Wither’s hymn books. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_vol4_raw.txt]

Addendum — [?1614] Abstract of the Grievances of the Journeymen Printers (lines ~33760–33912)

Reproduced from two known copies: one in the Broadsides collection of the Society of Antiquaries (a proof with manuscript corrections), the other in the Bagford Collection (Harl. MS. 5910, fol. 137). Arber dates it to the Parliament of 1614, noting that it may have prompted the provision of £200 annually to the poor of the Company in the renewed James I Grant of 8 March 1616.

The petition is addressed to the Lords Spiritual and Temporal in the High Court of Parliament. Its central argument: the 1586 Star Chamber Decree, obtained by the masters under colour of granting the journeymen privileges and books, has been used to keep journeymen in “servile bondage all their lives” — they cannot set up their own presses (six months imprisonment without bail under the Decree), while the masters keep excessive apprentices, print by apprentice labour rather than by journeymen, raise book prices, use poor paper and bad type, and have “drawn into their own hands” all the patent books that James I’s grant was intended for the poor printers. The Stationers (booksellers) are named as the chief engrossers, who “will retail no new copies unless they be of their own” — effectively forcing printers to sell their copies at the stationers’ price or not at all. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_vol4_raw.txt]

Cambridge University Business (p. 527, line ~33910)

A State Papers reference (Dom. James I., Vol. 153, fol. 71) to the 1624 business of the Stationers against the Printer of Cambridge — the legal and political conflict over Cambridge University’s right to print under its royal charter, which the London Stationers consistently contested. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_vol4_raw.txt]

Sir John Lambe’s List of Printers for the Star Chamber Decree (July 1637, lines ~33914–34002)

From the fly-sheet of a letter addressed to himself, Sir John Lambe (Dean of the Arches) noted down those printers he considered alone worthy to be authorised under the forthcoming Decree. The list names 21 printers with Lambe’s marginal assessments of each — who is blind and rich, who has taken a partner, who reprinted The Holy Table (the forbidden book that precipitated the Decree), who prints unlawful books (“William Jones: a printer of all vnlawful bookes and therefore put out — Thomas Paine who gaue him a great Deale of mony to be partener with him may be in his roome”), and who should be replaced by a son or son-in-law. Endorsed: “note of ye 20 printer[s] for ye Star Chamber decree. Julij. 1637.” State Papers, Dom. Charles I., Vol. 364, Art. 111. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_vol4_raw.txt]

The Second Star Chamber Decree Concerning Printing (11 July 1637, lines ~34002–34464)

The full text of the 1637 Star Chamber Decree — the successor to the 1586 Decree and the most comprehensive press-regulation instrument of the Stuart period, in force until the Star Chamber’s abolition in 1641. Produced in court by Sir John Bankes, His Majesty’s Attorney General, on the advice of the Lord Keeper, the Archbishop of Canterbury, the Bishop of London (Lord High Treasurer), the Lord Chief Justices, and the Lord Chief Baron. Printed immediately by Robert Barker, Printer to the King.

The Decree’s key provisions include: prohibition on printing or importing seditious, schismatical, or offensive books; requirement that all books be licensed before entry into the Stationers’ Register; authority hierarchy for different categories of book (common law books: Lord Chief Justices; heraldic books: Earl Marshal; plays and entertainments: Master of the Revels; all divinity, philosophy, science, and other books: Archbishop of Canterbury or Bishop of London); no printer to print in England any book printed or allowed abroad; the number of Master Printers to be limited and confirmed by the Archbishop; no new printing house to be erected without the Archbishop’s consent; prohibition on printing houses outside London and the two universities; regulation of the number of presses per printer; strict apprenticeship limits; requirement that every book bear the printer’s name and the licenser’s name. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_vol4_raw.txt]
