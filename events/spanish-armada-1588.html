The Armada’s route, 1588: outbound from Lisbon through the Channel to Gravelines (red), return arc north of Scotland and west of Ireland (orange dashed). Battle of Gravelines (★) July 29; fireship attack at Calais (◆) August 7–8; Parma’s embarkation zone at Dunkirk shown in purple.

The Spanish Armada of 1588 was Philip II of Spain’s attempt to land an invasion army in England, overthrow Elizabeth I, and restore Catholic rule. The English fleet — smaller and more manoeuvrable — destroyed the Armada’s cohesion through fire-ships and sustained broadside gunnery; Atlantic storms scattered the surviving ships around the British Isles. Corbett’s two-volume Drake and the Tudor Navy provides the most analytically rigorous English account, arguing that the victory was not providential or improvisational but the product of tactical and organisational reforms — especially the introduction of the line-ahead formation and professional gunnery discipline — primarily attributable to Drake.

Background: Why 1588

The Armada was the culmination of two decades of strategic deterioration in Anglo-Spanish relations. Drake’s 1585 Indies Voyage (sacking San Domingo and Cartagena) directly precipitated Philip II’s decision to build an invasion fleet. Drake’s April 1587 Cadiz raid — “singeing the King of Spain’s beard” — destroyed an estimated 10,000 tons of shipping and seized the staves intended for the Armada’s water casks, delaying the operation by perhaps a year; Drake wrote to Walsingham afterward that he expected the delay to be a full year. Mary Queen of Scots’ inclusion in Philip’s strategic calculation also mattered: he added her cause to the justification for the Armada. After her execution in February 1587, Philip was free to prosecute the invasion for Spanish rather than Marian dynastic reasons.

Walsingham’s intelligence network provided Elizabeth’s council with advance information about the fleet’s scale and the invasion plan: a Channel crossing to link with the Duke of Parma’s Army of Flanders, which would then embark in flat-bottomed barges. The plan had a fundamental architectural flaw that Walsingham’s agents identified: the Flemish ports where Parma’s troops waited (Dunkirk, Nieuport) were shallow-draft harbours that the Armada’s deep-draft warships could not enter to protect the barges. Dutch flyboats — shallow-draft vessels that the English had supplied to the rebel provinces — blockaded the Flemish coast, meaning Parma’s men could not embark even if the Armada arrived on schedule. The rendezvous was technically impossible from the outset. ^[source: corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol2_raw.txt, walsingham_raw.txt]

Medina Sidonia and the Spanish Fleet

Philip II appointed Alonso Pérez de Guzmán, Duke of Medina Sidonia, to command the fleet. Medina Sidonia was a grande of impeccable lineage but no naval experience; he wrote a letter to the king protesting his own unsuitability in extraordinary terms — acknowledging that he was not a sailor, that he tended to seasickness, and that he had no experience of such a command. Philip overrode the protest.

The Armada’s character was deeply religious. Priests held daily services aboard all ships; mass was said before each engagement; the fleet’s self-presentation was as a holy enterprise bearing God’s cause against Protestant heresy. The fleet of 130 ships carried approximately 30,000 men, of whom perhaps 18,000 were soldiers, the rest sailors and supporting personnel. It was the largest naval expedition assembled in European history to that date. ^[source: corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol2_raw.txt, hume_history_tudor_vol2_raw.txt]

The Plymouth Myth

The story that Drake was playing bowls on Plymouth Hoe when news of the Armada’s arrival was brought to him, and that he insisted on finishing the game before sailing, does not appear in any contemporary English source. A Dutch pamphlet from 1624 mentioned Drake’s composure before the battle; the specific bowling story was not attributed to Drake until 1736. Corbett treats the account as apocryphal. The practical reason for delay in leaving Plymouth was meteorological, not dramaturgical: the wind was blowing into the harbour, and the fleet had to be warped out against it — a genuine seamanship challenge. ^[source: corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol2_raw.txt, sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

The English Fleet

Lord Admiral Charles Howard of Effingham commanded the English fleet; Drake served as Vice-Admiral and was the tactical intelligence of the operation. Howard’s contribution has often been understated — he was not a brilliant innovator but was wise enough to subordinate himself to Drake’s tactical judgment while providing the command authority that Drake lacked the aristocratic standing to exercise over the full fleet. The fleet comprised approximately 197 ships, of which 34 were royal warships and the remainder armed merchantmen and pinnaces.

Drake’s contribution to fleet organisation was the introduction of line-ahead tactics: ships fighting in column at broadside range, using sustained artillery at distance rather than traditional boarding. Corbett identifies this formation as Drake’s single most important tactical innovation — transforming English sea fighting from medieval boarding combat to early modern gunnery. The English ships’ lower freeboard (they had abandoned the high fore- and after-castles that made earlier warships essentially floating fortresses) gave them speed and handling advantage but vulnerability if the Spanish managed to close and board. ^[source: corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol2_raw.txt, corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol1_raw.txt]

The Channel Engagements and the Drake Lantern Incident (July 19–27)

The Armada — 130 ships under Medina Sidonia — entered the English Channel on July 19, 1588. The English fleet was warped out against the wind and gained the weather gauge. Engagements followed off Plymouth (July 21), Portland Bill (July 23), and the Isle of Wight (July 25). The English gunnery inflicted damage but failed to break the Spanish crescent formation; the Armada’s discipline was formidable.

During the night pursuit following the Plymouth engagement, Drake extinguished the lantern that his ships were following to keep station — and went to capture a straggling Spanish galleon himself, leaving Howard and the fleet in darkness and confusion. He later claimed his lantern had failed. He was severely criticised for this breach of fleet discipline but Elizabeth kept him in command. Sugden treats the incident as characteristic of Drake’s instinct to pursue personal profit even during a strategic engagement. Medina Sidonia anchored off Calais to await Parma’s embarkation — but Parma could not embark because Dutch flyboats blockaded the Flanders ports. ^[source: corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol2_raw.txt, sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

The Fire-Ships and Battle of Gravelines (July 28–29)

On the night of July 28–29, Drake and Hawkins launched eight fire-ships into the Calais anchorage. The Spanish commanders, fearing explosive-laden “hell-burners” (as used with devastating effect at the siege of Antwerp in 1585), cut their cables and scattered — fatally breaking the crescent formation. The fire-ships themselves caused no Spanish casualties; no Spanish ship was actually burned. The panic was the weapon. Once the formation broke, the ships could not easily reassemble in the offshore wind and confused sea conditions.

On July 29 the Battle of Gravelines followed: the English fleet, now able to close and fight at short range, inflicted irreplaceable damage on the Spanish ships. Corbett’s analysis shows that the decisive factor was trained English gunnery — crews who could reload and fire at sustained rates that Spanish crews, using different gun-mounting systems, could not match. At close range, the English rate of fire produced a volume of shot that the Spanish ships, however disciplined their soldiers, could not absorb. Five Spanish ships were sunk or run aground; many others were so badly damaged they later foundered. ^[source: corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol2_raw.txt]

Aftermath and Losses

After Gravelines the wind drove the Armada northward; the English fleet followed until it ran short of ammunition and provisions. The surviving Spanish ships attempted to return to Spain by sailing around the north of Scotland and west of Ireland. Atlantic storms wrecked approximately 20–25 ships on the Irish coast; others were lost elsewhere. Of the 130 ships that left Spain, approximately 67 returned. Spanish personnel losses were catastrophic — perhaps 20,000 dead from battle, storm, and disease. England suffered no significant warship losses in combat. ^[source: corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol2_raw.txt, hume_history_tudor_vol2_raw.txt]

Corbett’s Assessment: Why England Won

Corbett argues forcefully against the providential Protestant narrative (“God blew and they were scattered”). Three factors were decisive: (1) the line-ahead formation, which maximised English broadside gunnery and prevented the Spanish from closing to board; (2) professional gunnery discipline that Drake had developed through the 1585 and 1587 campaigns; (3) Walsingham’s intelligence, which meant the English council knew the Armada’s plan and scale before it arrived. The fire-ships were decisive not because of the fires themselves but because they broke the Spanish formation, enabling the Gravelines gunnery engagement that inflicted the real damage. The storms were secondary; the Armada was already a broken force before it reached Scotland.

Corbett’s verdict on the strategic aftermath is equally pointed: the victory left the “game fairly in her hands” — England had the strategic initiative — but Elizabeth’s parsimony and the failure to follow up with an adequately resourced counter-attack squandered the advantage. The 1589 Lisbon expedition was underfunded and failed; Spain used the interval to rebuild and professionalise its navy; and the strategic world Drake had created closed around him. ^[source: corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol2_raw.txt]

Walsingham’s Role

Walsingham’s intelligence preparation was essential to the defence. His agents had penetrated the Spanish court and the Portuguese harbours where the fleet assembled; he provided reliable assessments of the Armada’s scale and intentions to the Privy Council. After the victory he corresponded with Howard and Drake about fleet maintenance and pressed for continued offensive posture — a warning ignored by Elizabeth’s parsimony. The failure to follow up the Armada victory with adequate resources squandered the strategic advantage Walsingham had spent his career creating. He died in April 1590 without seeing whether his life’s work had permanently secured Protestant England. ^[source: walsingham_raw.txt, corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol2_raw.txt]
