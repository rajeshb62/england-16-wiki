The Tower of London was the primary instrument of royal coercive power in Tudor England — fortress, palace, state prison, treasury, armory, and place of execution. It is where Tudor monarchs sent those they could not simply dismiss: the Constable of the Tower was a high office; imprisonment in the Tower signified that the Crown took the prisoner seriously as a political threat. Under Henry VIII alone, among those imprisoned or executed at the Tower were Thomas More, John Fisher, Anne Boleyn, Thomas Cromwell, Catherine Howard, and Lady Jane Grey. The Tower functions as the terminal point in Mantel’s Cromwell trilogy — the place toward which the narrative moves — and appears throughout the sources as the space where political destruction takes its final form.

Function and History

The Tower was built by William the Conqueror after 1066 as a fortress to dominate London; by the Tudor period it had accumulated centuries of royal use as residence, treasury (the Crown Jewels were housed there), armory, Royal Mint (until 1810), royal menagerie, and state prison. Prisoners of the highest rank — including kings, queens, and great ministers — were held in the Royal Apartments or the Beauchamp Tower, while those of lesser but still significant status occupied the Bell Tower and other accommodation. Execution within the walls — on Tower Green — was reserved for those of sufficiently high rank that a public execution on Tower Hill would be inappropriate; those executed on Tower Hill outside the walls included figures of slightly lower social standing. The distinction between dying within and without the walls was itself a statement of rank. ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt]

Thomas More and John Fisher (1535): The Legal Problem of Silence

More and Fisher were imprisoned in the Tower for refusing the oath of supremacy. More’s case presented Cromwell with an unusual legal problem: More’s silence — his refusal to give a reason for refusing the oath — was technically not treason under the existing legislation. A man could not be convicted of treason for silence alone. The solution came from Richard Rich, who subsequently testified at More’s trial that More had denied the supremacy in a private conversation during a Tower visit. Rich’s perjury — widely believed to be fabricated — provided the legally necessary spoken denial. Cromwell used the occasion. More was executed on Tower Hill on July 6, 1535; Fisher had been executed there on June 22. Their deaths were the foundational act of Henrician state coercion — the demonstration that the Supremacy was enforced at the cost of life, not merely conscience. ^[source: wolf_hall_raw.txt, hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt]

Anne Boleyn (1536): The Arrow-Chest Coffin

Anne Boleyn was imprisoned in the Tower in May 1536 on charges of adultery, incest, and conspiracy to murder the king. She was held in the Tower’s Royal Apartments — the same rooms that had been prepared for her coronation. Executed on Tower Green on May 19, 1536, by a Calais swordsman rather than an English axeman, she was buried in the Chapel of St Peter ad Vincula within the Tower. Her body was placed in a narrow arrow-chest that served as a coffin; no proper funeral preparation had been made. Her brother George Boleyn and the other accused men were executed on Tower Hill two days earlier. ^[source: wolf_hall_bring_up_the_bodies_raw.txt, hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt]

Thomas Cromwell (1540): Confined to the Rooms He Rebuilt

Cromwell was arrested and sent to the Tower on June 10, 1540. He was confined in the queen’s apartments — the rooms he himself had rebuilt and redecorated for Anne Boleyn’s coronation, and later redecorated again for Jane Seymour (changing the goddesses painted on the walls from brown eyes to blue when Jane came into the king’s favour). The spatial irony — that the man who had managed Henry’s queens was imprisoned in the rooms he had decorated for them — was not lost on Mantel. Cromwell was attainted by Parliament without trial; executed on Tower Hill on July 28, 1540. The Mirror and the Light closes with Cromwell’s weeks in the Tower and the morning of his execution — the flint of the Tower sparkling like sunlight on the sea at the moment of death. ^[source: the_mirror_and_the_light_raw.txt, hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt]

William Davison (1587)

The Secretary of State William Davison was imprisoned briefly in the Tower by Elizabeth after he dispatched the death warrant of Mary Queen of Scots — held as a face-saving exercise that allowed Elizabeth to claim the warrant had been sent without her final approval. Davison was eventually released but never returned to royal service; Elizabeth paid him a pension, which was her way of acknowledging he had done what was required while refusing to admit she had required it. ^[source: walsingham_raw.txt, hume_history_tudor_vol2_raw.txt]

The Tower in Mantel’s Trilogy

The Tower functions in the Cromwell trilogy as the space where political power manifests its final logic. Mantel uses the Tower’s physical geography precisely — the Beauchamp Tower, the Lieutenant’s lodgings, Tower Green, Tower Hill — to locate her characters in the institutional reality of Tudor power. In Wolf Hall, More’s imprisonment is a warning about what refusal costs; in Bring Up the Bodies, the Tower is where Anne and the accused men are held while Cromwell builds the case against them; in The Mirror and the Light, Cromwell himself is imprisoned there in the book’s final movement, confined to the rooms he furnished, waiting for the same outcome he once administered to others. The spatial memory of the building — what it has held, who has died there, what it is — is present in every scene Mantel sets within its walls. ^[source: wolf_hall_raw.txt, wolf_hall_bring_up_the_bodies_raw.txt, the_mirror_and_the_light_raw.txt]

Foxe: Lollards’ Tower and the Rack

Richard Hun was committed to the Lollards’ Tower at St Paul’s (1514) so none of his friends might visit; he was murdered there before posthumous burning at Smithfield (vol3).

Anne Askew was sent to the Tower in 1546 when she would not name ladies of her sect; Wriothesley and Rich put her on the rack with their own hands till she was nigh dead, then reasoned with her on the bare floor (vol5-pratt).
