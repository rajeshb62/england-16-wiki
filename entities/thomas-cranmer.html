Thomas Cranmer (1489–1556) was Archbishop of Canterbury from 1533 to 1555 — the ecclesiastical architect of the English Reformation. He provided Henry VIII with the canonical mechanism for the divorce from Catherine of Aragon, presided over the liturgical reformation under Edward VI that produced the foundational texts of Anglican worship, and was burned at the stake under Mary I for heresy. His two recantations — signing documents withdrawing his Protestant beliefs under pressure, then publicly withdrawing the recantations at the stake — define both his human weakness and his final courage. He is the chief ecclesiastical figure in the backdrop of Mantel’s Cromwell trilogy and a subject of sustained analysis in Hume’s first Tudor volume.

Rise and the Divorce

Cranmer was born in 1489 at Aslockton, Nottinghamshire, and educated at Jesus College, Cambridge. He came to Henry VIII’s attention in 1529 when he suggested that the king seek opinions from European universities on the canonical validity of his marriage to Catherine of Aragon — a diplomatic-theological strategy rather than a direct papal appeal. Henry sent him on embassies to Rome and to the Emperor. During his Continental service, Cranmer secretly married the niece of a German Lutheran cleric — a marriage he later had to conceal and eventually deny under the Six Articles (1539), reportedly hiding his wife in a trunk during travel. He was consecrated Archbishop of Canterbury on March 30, 1533 — making him technically the head of the English Church at the moment Henry was about to break with Rome. On May 23, 1533, he pronounced Henry’s marriage to Catherine void; on June 1 he crowned Anne Boleyn. He thus performed both acts that made the break with Rome structurally necessary. ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt, wolf_hall_raw.txt]

The Boleyn Affair and the Letter to Henry

When Anne Boleyn’s destruction was underway in May 1536, Cranmer wrote a remarkable letter to Henry VIII that survives as one of the most diplomatically difficult documents of the reign. In it he expressed that “he never had better opinion in woman” than in Anne, while simultaneously acknowledging that if the charges were true she deserved punishment. The letter navigated the impossible position of a man who had crowned Anne, annulled Catherine’s marriage to make her queen, and now found himself required to annul Anne’s marriage in turn. He pronounced Anne’s marriage to Henry void on May 17, 1536 — two days before her execution — meaning Elizabeth was technically illegitimate at the moment her mother was condemned. Cranmer survived Henry’s entire reign through doctrinal flexibility and political discretion: he maintained Henry’s personal affection. Henry reportedly told his councillors on his deathbed that Cranmer was the one man he trusted completely. ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt, wolf_hall_raw.txt]

The Prayer Books and the Edwardian Reformation

Under the boy-king Edward VI (1547–53), Cranmer implemented the Protestant reformation he had deferred throughout Henry’s reign. His two great achievements were:

  • The First Prayer Book (1549): The first English-language order of service, replacing the Latin Mass with vernacular worship. Its language was deliberately ambiguous — acceptable to traditionalists and reformers alike. The Western Rising (Prayer Book Rebellion) protested it as too Protestant; Continental reformers complained it was too Catholic.
  • The Second Prayer Book (1552): A more explicitly Protestant revision, removing the last ambiguities about the nature of the Eucharist. The “black rubric” explicitly denied the doctrine of real presence in the communion elements.
  • The 42 Articles (1553): The doctrinal foundation of Anglicanism, drafted by Cranmer and issued in the final months of Edward’s reign — later revised as the 39 Articles under Elizabeth.

Cranmer also oversaw the first English-language Bible in church services and the dissolution of chantries. As a prose stylist, his contribution to the Prayer Books gave the English language some of its most enduring rhythms — the collect form, the marriage service, the burial office. ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt]

The Six Articles and the Hidden Wife

The Act of Six Articles (1539) reaffirmed Catholic doctrines including clerical celibacy — making Cranmer’s secret German wife suddenly and dangerously illegal. He sent her back to Germany for the duration. The episode illustrates both the doctrinal uncertainty of the Henrician Reformation and the personal cost that uncertainty imposed on those who navigated it: Cranmer was a convinced Protestant doing the work of a king whose Protestantism was jurisdictional rather than doctrinal. ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt]

Mary I, Recantation, and the Stake

When Mary I succeeded Edward in 1553 and began restoring Catholicism, Cranmer was arrested on charges of heresy and treason. He was tried and condemned; under sustained pressure — including the psychological and physical conditions of prolonged imprisonment — he signed multiple recantations of his Protestant beliefs. These recantations shamed his supporters deeply; a man who had done so much to establish Protestant England appeared to have abandoned it at the last moment.

On March 21, 1556, at the burning place in Oxford (Broad Street), he was brought to make a final public recantation before the assembled crowd. Instead, he withdrew all his recantations. When the fire was lit, he thrust his right hand — the hand that had signed the recantations — into the flames first, saying “this hand hath offended.” He died holding it in the fire. His was the most famous death among the nearly 300 Protestants burned under Mary — the final public theatre of a career that had always been conducted in the proximity of great danger. ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt]

Foxe’s Account of Cranmer at Oxford

Foxe’s Book XI–XII (foxe-acts-monuments-vol4.txt) gives the fullest narrative in this knowledge base. The life, state, and story of Thomas Cranmer, Archbishop of Canterbury, martyr, opens the Marian section for 1556. Orations of Dr Brooks (bishop of Gloucester) and Dr Martin to Cranmer at St Mary’s Church, Oxford; Cranmer’s faith and profession before the commissioners; Dr Story’s oration; talk between Martin and the archbishop; Cranmer’s fuller answer to Brooks; letters to Queen Mary; papal commission with sentence definitive; appeal to the next general council; form of degrading an archbishop; copy of Cranmer’s recantation sent abroad by the papists; sum of Dr Cole’s sermon at Oxford; Cranmer’s prayer; further letters to the queen, to Mrs Wilkinson, and to a lawyer.

Foxe applied to Archbishop Parker (c. 1562) for the certified copy of the Oxford disputation of Cranmer, Ridley, and Latimer exhibited in Convocation 1° Mariæ — probably the document used in the 1563 edition. Cranmer was sent down to Oxford with Ridley and Latimer; the order and manner of the disputation and condemnation are set out in Book X (vol6).

Hume’s Assessment

Hume acknowledges Cranmer’s intellectual contribution to the Reformation (the Prayer Books as literary and theological achievements) while condemning what he calls his “tergiversations” — the willingness to serve successive monarchs’ doctrinal positions without visible personal conviction, and the recantations under Mary before the final reversal. The recantations followed by the recantation of the recantation make Cranmer a difficult figure for any historian: was the final courage the real man, or a last calculation that dying Protestant was strategically better than living Catholic? Hume leaves the question open. Mantel, working at a longer distance, finds the question similarly unresolvable — Cranmer appears in the trilogy as a man of genuine faith and genuine pliability, which is to say a man rather than a saint. ^[source: hume_history_tudor_vol1_raw.txt]
