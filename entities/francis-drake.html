Drake’s circumnavigation, 1577–80: Plymouth → Cape Verde → Strait of Magellan → Pacific (Cacafuego captured off Ecuador, March 1579) → “New Albion” (California) → Moluccas → Cape of Good Hope → Plymouth. The Golden Hind returned ballasted with silver.

Sir Francis Drake (c.1545–1596) was the most celebrated English seaman of the Elizabethan age: privateer, circumnavigator, Vice-Admiral of the fleet that defeated the Spanish Armada, and — in Corbett’s estimation — “the perfecter of a rational system of sailing tactics, as the father of a sound system of strategy, as the first and unsurpassed master of that amphibious warfare which has built up the British Empire.” Born into modest Devon circumstances, he rose through the privateering economy to command England’s first serious bid for maritime supremacy, and his career traced exactly the arc of England’s transition from a second-rank European power to the dominant force in the Atlantic. He died at sea off Portobello on January 28, 1596, and was buried in a lead coffin in the sea near the harbour where he had won his first great triumph twenty-four years earlier.

Origins and Early Career

Drake was born at Crowndale farm near Tavistock, Devon, c.1545 — property once held by Tavistock Abbey and leased from Sir John Russell (Lord High Admiral, founder of the House of Bedford). His father Edmund was a Protestant lay reader who had been driven from Devon by the Prayer Book Rebellion of 1549 and secured a position reading Bible services to the sailors at Chatham dockyard. Camden placed Francis natus est loco mediocri (“born in a middling place”). Corbett frames this origin as significant: Drake was a product of the emerging merchant class that was becoming the controlling force of the Tudor state, not the gentry.

Drake learned his trade through coastal and Channel trading voyages before serving his apprenticeship with the Hawkins family of Plymouth — the dominant maritime dynasty of Devon. He sailed on John Hawkins’s third slaving voyage as commander of the bark Judith (50 tons). After his return from the disaster of San Juan de Ulúa (1568), Drake began his independent Caribbean operations. ^[source: corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol1_raw.txt, sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

San Juan de Ulua (1568)

The third Hawkins slaving voyage ended catastrophically at San Juan de Ulúa (modern Veracruz) in September 1568. The English squadron of six ships — including the queen’s Jesus of Lubeck (700 tons), which Hawkins commanded — was sheltering in harbour when the newly arrived Spanish viceroy Martín Enríquez violated a negotiated truce and launched a surprise assault. Four ships were lost; Hawkins barely escaped on the damaged Minion. Drake escaped on the Judith. Hawkins later accused Drake of deserting him in the Judith — “that bark, the same night, forsook us in our great misery” — a charge that estranged the two men for years, though they eventually reconciled.

The survivors left behind faced grim fates: William Orlando died in Seville dungeons; Robert Barrett was burned at an auto-da-fé; Michael Morgan was tortured and sentenced to the galleys. Camden recorded that the event produced fury in England — military men “fretted and demanded war.” For Drake personally, the Spanish betrayal under a flag of truce was the founding grievance of his career: a debt he spent his life repaying. ^[source: corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol1_raw.txt]

Nombre de Dios and the Spanish Main (1572–73)

Drake’s first major independent command was the raid on Nombre de Dios on the Isthmus of Panama — the Atlantic terminus of the Peruvian silver route. He took the harbour but was wounded before reaching the treasure house. He then forged an alliance with Maroon communities (escaped African slaves) — a tactical innovation without precedent in English privateering. Their leaders, including Pedro, brought Drake accounts of how their towns had been sacked, their people killed or captured; Sugden notes that “Drake treated them with genuine respect and they trusted him.” He scuttled his brother’s ship (Swan) to man the pinnaces adequately — a calculated ruthlessness revealing the command discipline that would characterise his later voyages.

Operating overland across the Isthmus with the Maroons as guides, Drake climbed a tree from which he could see both the Atlantic and Pacific simultaneously — “the first Englishman, or at least the first that we have exact notice of, that saw that sea” — and vowed to sail upon it. The expedition eventually intercepted a mule train of Peruvian silver from Panama. Drake returned to England having demonstrated that Spanish imperial power on the Isthmus was vulnerable to a combination of naval mobility, overland operations, and alliance with the empire’s own victims. ^[source: corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol1_raw.txt, sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

Circumnavigation (1577–80)

Drake sailed from Plymouth in December 1577 with five ships led by the Pelican (100 tons). The true aim — circumnavigation via Magellan’s Strait and raids on the Spanish Pacific — was known only to Elizabeth, Walsingham, Leicester, and Sir Christopher Hatton; the crew was told they were “bound for Alexandria.” The cover story deceived even the Spanish ambassador Mendoza, who “could only confuse rumours that Drake was bound for Nombre de Dios.”

The Doughty Affair. At Port St Julian in June 1578 — where Magellan had previously wintered and executed a mutineer — Drake tried Thomas Doughty for mutiny. Doughty was “a gentleman, a friend of Drake’s, and a servant of Hatton”; he had been “subtly undermining Drake’s authority with hints that the backers expected Doughty to share the command.” Carpenter Edward Bright testified to having heard Doughty’s private boasts. Drake convened a formal “court of forty men” — with no royal commission that clearly authorized him to execute a gentleman — convicted Doughty, and beheaded him. He then “communed with him before the end and dined with him afterward — ‘we are yet friends,’ Drake told him.” After the execution, Drake renamed the Pelican the Golden Hind — Hatton’s family emblem — a diplomatic gesture to his dead adversary’s patron. Corbett judges Doughty “probably guilty of something” but the death sentence “legally doubtful.” The speech Drake made afterward — “gentlemen must haul and draw with the mariner, and the mariner with the gentleman” — Corbett identifies as the founding statement of the Elizabethan naval meritocratic ethos. ^[source: corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol1_raw.txt]

The Straits and the Pacific. Drake navigated the Straits of Magellan in 14 days — “the fastest of the century” (Magellan himself had taken 37 days). Emerging into the Pacific, the fleet encountered catastrophic storms: the Marigold sank with all 29 men aboard; the Elizabeth’s captain John Winter eventually turned back to England, leaving Drake alone in the Golden Hind. Drake’s raids on the undefended Spanish Pacific ports exposed a strategic vulnerability of the first importance: “the entire system of Spanish imperial security was exposed as resting on the assumption that no one could reach the Pacific from Europe.”

His most significant capture was the treasure galleon Nuestra Señora de la Concepción (known as Cacafuego — “Fire-Spitter”), taken after a chase on March 1, 1579. She carried twenty-six tonnes of silver, thirteen chests of silver coins, eighty pounds of gold, and a cargo of jewels and precious stones. Drake spent six days transferring the cargo; the Golden Hind was, Corbett reports, “literally ballasted with silver.” The Spanish sent Sarmiento de Gamboa south to intercept Drake at the Magellan Strait — too late; Sarmiento predicted Drake would seek the hypothetical Straits of Anian (a north-west passage), a prediction Drake’s northern exploration seemed to confirm. ^[source: corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol1_raw.txt]

Drake explored the California coast northward to approximately 48°N — further than any European had explored in the Pacific — and named the coast Nova Albion, leaving a brass plate claiming it for the English Crown. The exact landing location remains disputed among Drake’s Bay, Bolinas Bay, and the San Francisco Bay entrance. Cold and fog defeated any attempt to find a north-west passage. In the Indian Ocean, the Golden Hind ran onto a reef and was held fast for twenty hours before breaking free. Drake stopped at the Moluccas, where he negotiated a trade agreement with the Sultan of Ternate and loaded cloves.

The Golden Hind arrived at Plymouth on September 26, 1580. Before landing, Drake sent a pinnace ashore to determine whether Walsingham was still in power and the political situation favourable — the plunder, if politically inconvenient, could be disavowed. Elizabeth came to Deptford on April 4, 1581; the knighting ceremony was performed by a French envoy at Elizabeth’s deliberate invitation — a calculated diplomatic message to Spain that England would not apologise for the Pacific raids. ^[source: corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol1_raw.txt]

Social Ascent

The proceeds of the circumnavigation funded Drake’s transformation into Devon gentry. He purchased Buckland Abbey from Richard Grenville through an intermediary for approximately £3,400 — a transaction conducted through a middleman because the Grenville family were reluctant to sell to a man of Drake’s social origins. He served as Mayor of Plymouth in 1581–82 and sat in Parliament. He pursued a coat of arms: he initially denied his humble origins and attempted to borrow the arms of the ancient Drake family of Ashe, but eventually accepted the queen’s grant of the globe with the Golden Hind. Sugden treats this anxiety about heraldry as characteristic of the social insecurity of a man who had made himself from nothing. ^[source: sir_francis_drake_raw.txt, corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol1_raw.txt]

The Indies Voyage (1585)

Drake commanded approximately 2,300 men in a large fleet, sacking San Domingo (the oldest European settlement in the Indies, captured after a mountain-march attack) and Cartagena. The campaign aimed to destroy the infrastructure of Spanish Atlantic power and seize advanced bases at Panama and Havana; epidemic disease severely compromised the force. The Indies Voyage directly precipitated Philip II’s decision to build an invasion fleet — it was the strategic trigger for the Armada. ^[source: corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol2_raw.txt]

Cadiz (1587) and the Armada (1588)

In April 1587 Drake raided Cadiz harbour — “singeing the King of Spain’s beard” — destroying an estimated 10,000 tons of shipping. He also seized the staves intended for the Armada’s water casks; without seasoned staves, the barrels could not be properly sealed, contributing to the fleet’s provisioning problems in 1588. His vice-admiral William Borough wrote a formal protest against Drake’s refusal to hold a council of war before attacking; Drake had Borough court-martialled. Elizabeth was unimpressed by his treatment of a subordinate but kept him in command. Drake wrote to Walsingham that the raid “would delay the Armada by a year” — roughly accurate. He also captured the Portuguese carrack San Felipe returning from the East Indies (cargo worth approximately £100,000).

As Vice-Admiral under Lord Admiral Howard in 1588, Drake was the tactical brain of the English fleet: the line-ahead formation, sustained broadside gunnery at range, and the fire-ship attack at Calais were all products of his tactical school. At the Battle of Gravelines (July 29, 1588) the English gunnery inflicted irreplaceable losses. One notable incident: Drake extinguished the lantern his ships were following during a night pursuit and went to capture a straggling Spanish galleon for himself, leaving Howard and the fleet in darkness. He later said his lantern had failed. Sugden treats this as characteristic — Drake’s instinct for personal profit operating even during a fleet action. ^[source: corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol2_raw.txt, sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

Lisbon (1589) and Disgrace

Drake and General Norreys commanded the 1589 Portugal expedition to place Don Antonio on the Portuguese throne. The navy successfully attacked Coruña, but Norreys’s army failed to take Lisbon. Drake was held jointly responsible and effectively removed from active command for five years (1590–94). Leicester had died in 1588; Walsingham died in April 1590; without these patrons, Drake had no court champion. Spain used the interval to rebuild and professionalise its navy — the Indian Guard convoy escorts and the Tertia escort squadrons that would make Drake’s final expedition impossible. ^[source: corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol2_raw.txt]

Last Voyage and Death (1595–96)

Drake led a final Caribbean expedition with John Hawkins in 1595, under a divided command structure in which each man held “equal authority.” Spain had professionalised its navy; the strategic world Drake had exploited no longer existed. The attack on Puerto Rico failed; the garrison had been reinforced. Hawkins died off Puerto Rico on November 12, 1595. Drake died of dysentery off Portobello on January 28, 1596, and was buried at sea in a lead coffin near the harbour where he had won his first great triumph in 1572. Philip II reportedly said on hearing the news: “It is good news, and now I will get well.” The Spanish admiral Sotomayor composed an epitaph acknowledging Drake as worthy of respect even in death. ^[source: corbett_drake_tudor_navy_vol2_raw.txt, sir_francis_drake_raw.txt]

Corbett’s Assessment

Drake’s significance lies not in romantic corsairing but in being “the perfecter of a rational system of sailing tactics, as the father of a sound system of strategy, as the first and unsurpassed master of that amphibious warfare which has built up the British Empire.” The Armada victory was not improvised heroism but trained gunnery and disciplined formation. Drake’s post-1589 failure resulted from Elizabeth’s strategic ambivalence and the loss of court patrons — not from any decline in his own capacity. Spain had learned from him and adapted; the world he had helped create had changed around him.
