Stationers' Company

The Stationers’ Company was the London guild that governed the English book trade from its royal charter of 1557 through the lapse of the Licensing Act in 1695. Every printer, bookseller, and bookbinder in England was expected to be free of the Company, and every title intended for sale was expected to be entered in the Company’s Register before printing — a requirement that served simultaneously as a commercial record, a property right, and a censorship mechanism. The Company was the institutional centre of English printing across the century that produced the Elizabethan literary renaissance, the King James Bible, and the Shakespeare First Folio.

Origins

The name “Stationer” derives from the “stations” — fixed market points at the Cheapside crosses — at which medieval book-dealers stood. By 1379 the Corporation of London was leasing a set of stations to eleven named women. The Fraternity’s pre-charter history begins with the Corporation Ordinance of 1403, which appointed two Wardens for “the Writers of Text-Letter, Limners, and others, who bind and sell books.” The Ordinance also covered Scriveners, reflecting the overlap between manuscript copying, binding, and selling that characterized the trade before print displaced the manuscript economy. The custom of registering copies before printing is believed by Hazlitt to have already been in use at the time of the 1403 Ordinance — making the Register a practice that predated the charter, which the charter then codified and enforced. Pynson and Wynkyn de Worde were probably members of the pre-incorporation Fraternity; Wynkyn de Worde is specifically cited by Hazlitt as having a relationship with the pre-charter body. ^[source: hazlitt_livery_companies_raw.txt]

The 1557 Charter

Philip and Mary granted the royal charter on 4 May 1557. The preamble — quoted in full by Hazlitt — identifies censorship of “seditious and heretical books, rhymes, and treatises” as the explicit motive: the Company was chartered as an instrument of press control. Total cost to the grantees was just over £20, including 9s. for a rejected first draft. Thomas Dockwray was named first Master; John Cawood (royal printer) and Henry Cooke were first Wardens — though Hazlitt notes that other sources name the first Wardens as Cawood, Tottel, and Wight, and the discrepancy in the sources has not been resolved. Elizabeth I confirmed the charter on 10 November 1559. Patterson’s legal analysis argues that the charter was essentially a bargain: the Crown received an efficient censorship mechanism, and the stationers received a legally enforceable monopoly over the English book trade. The apparatus built to suppress Protestants under Mary was immediately redeployed against Catholics under Elizabeth — the same charter, the same enforcement mechanism, the same Wardens’ search powers, now oriented against the other side. ^[source: hazlitt_livery_companies_raw.txt, copyright_patterson_raw.txt]

Governance

The Company followed the standard livery company structure: a Master and Wardens elected annually; a Court of Assistants as governing board; the Livery (senior freemen eligible for governance); the Yeomanry (general freemen). The Stationers — noted by Hazlitt alongside the Apothecaries and Brewers — admitted only members of the vocation, making it a true Craft Gild throughout the Elizabethan period, enforced through apprenticeship. In 1882 the Company had 312 on the Livery and 445 Freemen; approximately 100 apprentices were annually bound at the Hall. James I confirmed the charter in 1603 and granted an enlargement of privileges in 1616. ^[source: hazlitt_livery_companies_raw.txt]

The Register of Copies

Entry in the Register at Stationers’ Hall created what Patterson calls “stationer’s copyright” — an exclusive right, enforceable within the Company’s jurisdiction, to print a specific title. The right belonged to the stationer who entered the title, not to the author; the author had no legal interest in the copy once the manuscript was sold. The Register survives in full from 1557; Arber’s five-volume Transcript (1875–94) contains every entry through 1640. There is, however, a known gap in Arber’s transcript for the years 1571–76, associated with a register kept by a clerk named Wapull that was subsequently lost — creating a documentary lacuna for those years that affects copyright history for works entered in that period.

Notable entries include: the first Hamlet (26 July 1602, James Roberts, 6d); Shakespeare’s Sonnets (20 May 1609, Thomas Thorpe, 6d); the Shakespeare First Folio (8 November 1623, Blount and Jaggard — 7 shillings for the Comedies, 6d per History and Tragedy, listing 16 previously unregistered plays by name). Specific transferred copies — books that passed through multiple assignments — illustrate how the stationer’s right was treated as a commercial asset; T. East’s books, for instance, passed through several assignments across the period documented in Arber. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_vol2_raw.txt, stationers_registers_arber_vol3_raw.txt, stationers_registers_arber_vol4_raw.txt, copyright_patterson_raw.txt]

Power of Search and Censorship

The 1557 charter conferred on the Company a power of search throughout England: the right to enter premises and seize books printed without licence or in violation of the charter. This was the operational mechanism of Tudor press censorship. The Arber Registers record specific suppressions: French books burned by the Primate (1595); Raleigh’s History of the World ordered suppressed by the Archbishop of Canterbury (1614); Lithgow’s Travels erased from the Register (1616); Prynne’s Histriomastix erased and the author fined £5,000, ears cropped, and imprisoned for life (1634); the MS and whole impression of Buchanan’s History of Scotland seized. The Star Chamber Decrees of 1586 and 1637 both channelled all enforcement through the Company’s search power. ^[source: hazlitt_livery_companies_raw.txt, stationers_registers_arber_vol3_raw.txt, stationers_registers_arber_vol4_raw.txt, copyright_patterson_raw.txt]

The Patent Controversy and the John Wolfe Rebellion (1582–83)

The 1560s and 1570s saw an explosion of printing patents — grants of exclusive rights to print specific categories of text (law books, almanacs, ABC-books, psalters). These patents were separate from the stationer’s copyright and created bitter divisions within the trade: small printers and journeymen found that the most commercially valuable titles were locked up by patent-holders, many of whom farmed out the work to others rather than printing it themselves.

Christopher Barker, appointed Queen’s Printer in 1577 (through Walsingham’s patronage), produced a detailed Report in 1582 — submitted to Walsingham — describing the state of the trade, the specific grievances of general stationers against the patent-holders, and the inequity of a system in which a few privileged printers controlled the most profitable texts while the rest of the trade scrambled for the remainder. The Report is an important documentary source for understanding how the patent system was experienced from inside the trade.

In 1582–83, John Wolfe — a printer who had worked in Florence and returned with Continental ideas about printing freedom — led an open rebellion against the monopoly patents, arguing they were legally void and that any stationer had the right to print any book. He and a group of journeymen began printing books in defiance of the existing patents. The Company arrested and fined several of the rebels. The controversy was ultimately defused not by prosecution but by incorporating Wolfe into the system: he was made a member of the Company and eventually appointed Beadle, and the more legitimate grievances of general stationers were addressed through the creation of shared investments in printing rights. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_vol2_raw.txt, copyright_patterson_raw.txt, hazlitt_livery_companies_raw.txt]

Common Stock and English Stock

The Company operated collective investment funds: the Latin Stock (scholarly books), the Irish Stock (printing rights in Ireland), the ballad stock, and the Bible stock. These arrangements developed through the 1570s and 1580s partly in response to the patent controversy — giving more Company members a financial stake in the privileged printing rights rather than concentrating all benefit in individual patent-holders. About 1601 a Crown grant consolidated these into the English Stock — a joint enterprise giving Company members a proportional share of revenue from the most commercially valuable printing rights (ABC/catechism, almanacs, psalms). The English Stock represented the institutional resolution of the Wolfe rebellion’s legitimate grievances: widened participation in profits without abandoning the monopoly structure. In 1882 the English Stock still had capital of £41,000–42,000 among 306 members, distributing £400 annually to Company poor. ^[source: hazlitt_livery_companies_raw.txt, copyright_patterson_raw.txt]

George Wither and the Challenge to Stationer’s Copyright (1623–25)

The most sustained direct challenge to the stationer’s copyright from an author’s perspective came from the poet George Wither. Wither obtained a royal patent requiring his Hymns and Songs of the Church (1623) to be bound with every copy of the Company’s metrical psalter — a requirement that would have compelled the Company to distribute his work whether they wished to or not. The Company resisted vigorously, seeking to suppress the patent. Wither responded by writing The Schollers Purgatory (c.1625), a detailed polemic attacking the Company’s monopolistic practices, arguing that the stationer’s system subordinated authors’ interests entirely to those of booksellers and printers. Patterson cites the Wither dispute as the clearest Jacobean articulation of a counter-theory of authorial rights — a theory the law did not yet recognize. ^[source: copyright_patterson_raw.txt, hazlitt_livery_companies_raw.txt]

Journeymen Disputes and the 1637 Decree

By the 1630s, tensions within the trade had shifted from patent-holders versus general stationers to masters versus journeymen. Journeymen printers — wage employees without a share in copyright or patents — complained that impression sizes (the number of copies printed from a single setting of type) were being increased to maximize masters’ profits at the cost of journeymen’s employment. The Star Chamber Decree of 1637 addressed this directly by specifying maximum impression sizes tied to type size: Nonpareil (very small type) was limited to 5,000 impressions; Brevier (small text type) to 3,000. These limits were designed to ensure that type was re-set more frequently, generating more compositing work for journeymen rather than allowing masters to run off unlimited copies from a single composition. ^[source: stationers_registers_arber_vol4_raw.txt, copyright_patterson_raw.txt]

Hall History

The Company occupied five successive halls: (1) Clement Court, Milk Street, Cheapside (probably mid-15th century); (2) Peter College at the south-west angle of St Paul’s churchyard, c.1550 — formerly converted to a printing office by William Seres after the Dissolution; (3) the frontage of Peter College, let to Edward Kynaston (vintner) in 1606, who made it the Feathers Tavern; (4) Abergavenny House, acquired 1611; (5) the rebuilt Hall, completed 1674 after the 1666 Great Fire, with wainscoting contracted with Stephen College, the “Protestant Joiner,” for £300. The Great Fire of 1666 reduced the Hall “to a wreck, with the contents and even the common seal, the registers excepted.” The registers survived because they were with the Clerk at his house in Clerkenwell Green. ^[source: hazlitt_livery_companies_raw.txt]

Quo Warranto Proceedings

The Company faced Quo Warranto proceedings in 1663 and 1684. The 1663 writ arose from “a belief that the Company was not sufficiently vigilant and strict in its capacity as a curb on the press, and connived at the publication of inflammatory or scurrilous literature” — an extraordinary inversion in which the censorship body was attacked for insufficient censorship. The charter was restored by Act of 2 William and Mary (1690). ^[source: hazlitt_livery_companies_raw.txt]

Notable Members

Thomas Berthelet (king’s printer, first-year Master); Reginald Wolfe (four times Master); John Cawood (royal printer, first Warden); Richard Jugge; Richard Tottell (law printer, patent-holder through three reigns for law books); the Harrisons; the Days; the Nortons; the Barkers; the Rivingtons; Christopher Barker (queen’s printer from 1577, patron-appointed through Walsingham); Richard Field (publisher of Shakespeare’s Venus and Adonis). Later: Samuel Richardson (Master 1754); Jacob Tonson; Henry Sampson Woodfall (printer of the Letters of Junius, Master 1797). ^[source: hazlitt_livery_companies_raw.txt, typographical_antiquities_ames_raw.txt]

The Stationers’ Commonwealth: Knowledge and Credibility (Johns)

Adrian Johns’s The Nature of the Book (1998) reframes the Stationers’ Company not as a trade guild that happened to enforce censorship but as an epistemic community — a “Stationers’ commonwealth” — that set the conditions under which all printed knowledge was produced, circulated, and judged in early modern England. “In early modern England, knowledge had to be made, articulated, communicated, and defended in a Stationers’ commonwealth.” This commonwealth was “distinctive not only socially, but epistemologically.”

Credibility as a social achievement. The central problem of print in Johns’s account was that it provided no automatic guarantee of credibility. “Credit was fragile and ephemeral to an extraordinary degree. The conditions in which knowledge must be constructed, maintained, and defended were riven by private deals, intrigue and distrust.” Any printed book could be pirated — reprinted without authorization, falsely attributed, or corrupted by compositors. Authors who wanted their works credited had to actively navigate the Stationers’ practices: visiting the printing house, supervising composition, using the trade’s social mechanisms to vouch for their texts. The Quakers appointed individual booksellers to oversee the press precisely for this reason; the Royal Society developed its own conventions for the same purpose. The Stationers’ Company was “the most authoritative and effective” of such disciplining efforts.

The concept of “copy” as an epistemic tool. The Company’s principle of “copy” — recognizing another stationer’s prior claim to print a work in which they had invested time, skill, capital, and credit — was not merely a commercial mechanism. It was “the most vivid representation” of the uncertainty pervading the book trade, and the primary institutional response to it. Holding the “copy” of a text was a claim about credit and investment, contested by piracy and “countering” (unauthorized reprinting). The conventions of propriety embedded in the copy system were, in Johns’s formulation, “a way of looking at the world, as well as a means of sharing it.”

Stationers’ Hall as architectural authority. Johns argues that the physical architecture of Stationers’ Hall itself embodied the Company’s disciplinary hierarchy. The Hall’s spatial organization — with its graduated zones of access, from the outer spaces open to the general trade to the restricted chambers where the Court of Assistants met — mirrored the hierarchy of trust and authority within the Company. Stationers’ customs, not statute law, were the primary mechanism of discipline; the Company’s conventions of propriety gave print its credibility in Tudor and Stuart England. ^[source: nature_of_the_book_raw.txt]
