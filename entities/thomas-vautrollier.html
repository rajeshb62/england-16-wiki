Thomas Vautrollier (d. before 4 March 1587–8) was a Huguenot refugee printer who established one of the finest presses in Elizabethan London — operating from the liberty of Blackfriars outside the strict jurisdiction of the City — and who ran a secondary press in Edinburgh, producing theological and literary texts of exceptional quality. He was Richard Field’s master; Field married his widow and inherited the press on Vautrollier’s death. Ames/Herbert’s Typographical Antiquities documents Vautrollier’s career, his typography, and the books he produced.

Origins and Admission to the Stationers’ Company

Vautrollier was a French Protestant — probably from the Paris or Troyes region. He was admitted to the Stationers’ Company as a “stranger” (foreign printer) on October 2, 1564 — under the specific arrangements that allowed alien craftsmen to work in London’s liberties. He established his press in Blackfriars — a former Dominican friary dissolved under Henry VIII and now a liberty (an enclave not subject to normal City jurisdiction), which made it a natural home for printers operating under the Company’s regulatory reach while enjoying some insulation from it. His device was an anchor with the motto Anchora Spei (“Anchor of Hope”) — an emblem that linked him to the Continental humanist printing tradition and signalled the aspirations of his press. ^[source: typographical_antiquities_ames_raw.txt, stationers_registers_arber_vol1_raw.txt]

The Blackfriars Press and Continental Network

Vautrollier’s press specialised in high-quality literary, classical, and theological printing that required sophisticated typographic skill: Greek and Latin texts, scholarly publications in the Continental humanist tradition, Reformed theological works. His Huguenot background gave him direct connections to the Continental Reformed publishing world — French, Swiss, and Low Countries printers who were producing the intellectual output of the Protestant Reformation. From 1570 onwards, from July 1581, he appears to have been frequently absent from London, possibly maintaining connections with Continental publishing centres and managing the Edinburgh operation. ^[source: typographical_antiquities_ames_raw.txt]

The Edinburgh Press

Vautrollier operated a press in Edinburgh — a significant undertaking connecting the London and Scottish book trades. In Edinburgh he printed primarily theological and Reformed texts, serving the Scottish Protestant readership in the aftermath of John Knox’s reformation. The Edinburgh operation illustrates how skilled printers of the period operated across national jurisdictions: Vautrollier moved between London and Edinburgh as his commissions and regulatory circumstances required. The episode of Giordano Bruno’s works is connected to this period: Vautrollier printed two of Bruno’s works in London in 1584, and the diplomatic and religious controversy this generated contributed to his subsequent need to operate in Edinburgh, where the London regulatory apparatus had less reach. ^[source: typographical_antiquities_ames_raw.txt]

Major Publications

Vautrollier’s press produced works of lasting significance:

  • Thomas North’s Plutarch’s Lives (1579): The translation of Plutarch that Shakespeare used as the source for his Roman plays (Julius Caesar, Antony and Cleopatra, Coriolanus). The connection between Vautrollier’s press and Shakespeare’s dramaturgy runs through this text — continued by Field when he inherited the press.
  • Byrd and Tallis’s Cantiones Sacrae (1575): Music printing under the monopoly patent granted to William Byrd and Thomas Tallis — one of the few Elizabethan music printing patents; Vautrollier produced it under license.
  • Giordano Bruno’s works (1584): Two of the Italian philosopher’s texts, printed during Bruno’s London stay and his period of residence at the French embassy. The association with Bruno — whose cosmological and philosophical heterodoxy would eventually cost him his life in Rome — illustrates both the reach of Vautrollier’s intellectual networks and the risks that came with it.
  • Theological works in Latin: Calvin, Beza, and other Reformed authors.
  • Classical texts in Latin and Greek, in the humanist tradition of high-quality scholarly printing.

The quality of Vautrollier’s typography — noted by Ames/Herbert — placed his press among the leading London printers of the 1570s and 1580s. ^[source: typographical_antiquities_ames_raw.txt, stationers_registers_arber_vol1_raw.txt]

Death and Succession

Vautrollier died before 4 March 1587–8 — the exact date uncertain, but bounded by that record. His widow Jacqueline could not legally continue operating the press herself: the Stationers’ Company’s regulations, like those of other livery companies, made it extremely difficult for a widow to maintain a business independently without a male freeman as head. She married Richard Field, Vautrollier’s former apprentice, who then took over the press and its London operation. This pattern — widow marrying a trained journeyman to maintain the enterprise and satisfy Company requirements — was typical of the Elizabethan book trade, and the Company’s regulations were designed partly to facilitate such transfers. Through Field, Vautrollier’s press became the vehicle for Shakespeare’s first publications. ^[source: typographical_antiquities_ames_raw.txt]
